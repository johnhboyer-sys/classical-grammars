# Dative Case

and `topics/middle-voice.md`. Every claim carries a section citation;

## 1. Quick answer

The Greek dative is used for three cases: the dative proper, and two lost cases, the instrumental and the locative (§1450). Goodell names them the true Dative, the To or For case; the Instrumental or Sociative, the With or By case; and the Locative, the At or In case (Goodell §519). Monro reaches the same result by comparison with Sanskrit, and adds that there is reason to think distinct forms for the three survived down to a comparatively late period, which the traces of instrumental and locative forms in Homer and the readiness with which the uses of the Greek dative, especially in Homer, can be reapportioned between the three make probable (Monro §142). Goodell's table sets the two languages side by side: the nominative, accusative and vocative stayed distinct in both, while in Greek the old ablative went to the genitive and the instrumental and locative to the dative (Goodell §528). So there is no one meaning. The working question is which of the three a given dative belongs to, and the form will not tell you.

## 2. Recognition

- The dative as the sole complement of a verb that English makes transitive. Verbs of benefiting, helping, injuring, pleasing, being friendly or hostile, blaming, being angry, threatening and envying: βοηθεῖν τοῖσιν ἠδικημένοις to help the wronged, ἐμοὶ ὀργίζονται they are angry at me (§1461).
- Verbs of meeting, approaching and yielding: ποίοις οὐ χρὴ θηρίοις πελάζειν what wild beasts one must not approach (§1463).
- Verbs of obeying, serving, trusting, advising and commanding: τοῖς νόμοις πείθου obey the laws, ἐπίστευον αὐτῷ αἱ πόλεις the cities trusted him (§1464).
- Verbs of being like or unlike, comparing, befitting: ἐοικέναι τοῖς τοιούτοις to be like such men (§1466).
- The indirect object beside an accusative: Κῦρος δίδωσιν αὐτῷ ἓξ μηνῶν μισθόν Cyrus gives him pay for six months (§1469).
- The dative of the possessor with εἶναι: ἄλλοις μὲν χρήματά ἐστι, ἡμῖν δὲ ξύμμαχοι ἀγαθοί others have riches, we have good allies (§1476).
- The dative of the agent with a perfect passive or with a verbal adjective: ἐμοὶ καὶ τούτοις πέπρᾱκται has been done by me and these men (§1488).
- An instrumental dative of means: ἔβαλλέ με λίθοις he hit me with stones, ταῖς μαχαίραις κόπτοντες hacking them with their swords (§1507).
- A dative of degree of difference beside a comparative: κεφαλῇ ἐλᾱ́ττων a head shorter, οὐ πολλαῖς ἡμέραις ὕστερον ἦλθεν he arrived not many days later (§1513).
- A dative of respect: ἀνὴρ ἡλικίᾳ ἔτι νέος a man still young in years, ἀσθενὴς τῷ σώματι weak in body (§1516).
- A dative of cause with a verb of emotion: τούτοις ἥσθη he was pleased at this (§1517).
- A comitative dative of association: ἀλλήλοις διειλέγμεθα we have conversed with each other, Κῡ́ρῳ πολεμοῦντες waging war with Cyrus (§1523).
- A dative of accompanying circumstance, usually an abstract substantive with an attribute: πολλῇ βοῇ προσέκειντο they attacked with loud shouts (§1527).
- A locative dative of time with an attributive: τῇ δὲ ὑστεραίᾳ on the day following, τρίτῳ μηνί in the third month (§1540).
- A locative dative of place, which in prose is used only of proper names: Ἰσθμοῖ at the Isthmus, Μαραθῶνι at Marathon, but ἐν Μαραθῶνι and ἐν Πλαταιαῖς occur, and some deme-names require ἐν (§1534).
- A dative required by the preposition inside a compound verb: ἐμβλέψᾱς αὐτῷ looking at him, αὐτοῖς ἐπέπεσε τὸ Ἑλληνικόν the Greek force fell upon them (§1545).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Ask whether the dative completes the verb or only comments on it. It is a necessary complement when the information given by the verb is incomplete without it, as πείθεται he obeys needs the addition of τοῖς νόμοις the laws (§1451). It is a voluntary complement when it adds something unessential, as αὐτοῖς οἱ βάρβαροι ἀπῆλθον the barbarians departed to their advantage (§1452). The boundary line between the necessary and the voluntary complement is not always clearly marked (§1453).
2. If the dative names a person, start with the dative proper. The dative proper denotes that to or for which something is or is done (§1457); it is largely personal and denotes the person who is interested in or affected by the action, and it is not often used with things, where there is usually personification or semi-personification (§1459).
3. Decide which kind of interest the dative shows. The person for whom something is or is done, or in reference to whose case an action is viewed, is put in the dative (§1474). The dative of the possessor names the person for whom a thing exists (§1476). The dative of advantage or disadvantage names the person or thing for whose advantage or disadvantage anything is or is done, and often has to be translated as if the possessive genitive were used, though the meaning is different (§1481). The dative of feeling often uses the personal pronouns of the first and second person to denote the interest of the speaker, or to secure the interest of the person spoken to (§1486).
4. If the dative is a possessor, ask which question is being answered. The dative of the possessor denotes that something is at the disposal of a person or has fallen to his share temporarily, while the genitive of possession lays stress on the person who owns something: the dative answers what is it that he has, the genitive answers who is it that has something (§1480).
5. If the verb is a perfect or pluperfect passive, or a verbal adjective, expect the agent in the dative. With passive verbs, usually in the perfect and pluperfect, and regularly with verbal adjectives in -τός and -τέος, the person in whose interest an action is done is put in the dative, and the notion of agency does not belong to the dative but is a natural inference (§1488). Smyth explains the restriction to completed action by the agent being placed in the position of viewing an already completed action in the light of its relation to himself (§1489), and notes that the dative of the agent is rarely used with other tenses (§1490). Monro reads the Homeric evidence the same way, as a special application of the true dative, because the victory is gained by the victor and a past fact is thought of as a kind of possession (Monro §143).
6. If the dative is not a person, try the instrumental. The dative denotes instrument or means, manner, and cause (§1506). Means includes the dative of price, rarely the dative with verbs of filling, and the dative of material and constituent parts (§1508). χρῆσθαι use takes the dative, and a predicate noun may be added: τούτοις χρῶνται δορυφόροις they make use of them as a body-guard (§1509). That by which anything is measured or judged is put in the dative (§1512).
7. If a comparative stands nearby, read the dative as the measure of difference. The dative of manner is used with comparative adjectives and other expressions of comparison to mark the degree by which one thing differs from another: τοσούτῳ ἥδῑον ζῶ ὅσῳ πλείω κέκτημαι the more I possess the more pleasant is my life (§1513). Goodell puts the same use under the instrumental, with οὐ πολλοῖς ἔτεσιν ὕστερον not many years later (Goodell §526).
8. If the dative names something that shares in the action, it is comitative. The comitative form of the instrumental dative denotes the persons or things which accompany or take part in an action (§1521). Smyth's test uses the noun: a person not regarded as the instrument or means, or any other living being, or a thing regarded as a person, or an action, belongs here, and the instrument or means belongs with the instrumental dative proper (§1504). Monro finds the comitative sense chiefly in the plural, where the dative denotes attendants, surroundings and adjuncts (Monro §144).
9. If the dative is a place or a time, it is locative. In prose the dative of place is used only of proper names, but ἐν Μαραθῶνι and ἐν Πλαταιαῖς occur (§1534), and with names of countries and places ἐν is more common than the locative dative (§1536). The dative without a preposition is commonly used to denote a definite point of time, chiefly day, night, month, year or season; it contrasts one point of time with another and is usually accompanied by an attributive (§1539). Monro finds the locatival dative of place much commoner in Homer than in later Greek, though already restricted to a comparatively narrow range (Monro §145).
10. If the grammars cannot decide, leave the question open. Smyth says that it is not clear whether the dative with verbs of ruling is a dative proper, a locative, or an instrumental (§1538), and that some of the datives of space and time that may be regarded as comitative are instrumental rather than comitative (§1528). Monro splits the class instead: the dative with verbs meaning to give commands and to lead the way is apparently the true dative, but that does not apply to verbs meaning to have power or to be king, where ἀνασσέμεν Ἀργείοισι probably means to be king among the Argives (Monro §143). He then puts κρατέω, ἀνάσσω and βασιλεύω under the locatival dative of persons (Monro §145).
11. With an adjective, use the related verb as a guide. Adjectives, adverbs and substantives of kindred meaning with those verbs take the dative to define their meaning: βασιλεῖ φίλοι friendly to the king, φόρῳ ὑπήκοοι subject to tribute (§1499). Some adjectives, as φίλος, ἐχθρός, may be treated as substantives and take the genitive (§1499). With ὁ αὐτός the dative marks what the comparison is with: τὴν αὐτὴν γνώμην ἐμοὶ ἔχειν to be of the same mind as I am (§1500).
12. With a compound verb, check the preposition. The dative is used with verbs compounded with σύν regularly, with many compounded with ἐν and ἐπί, and with some compounded with παρά, περί, πρός and ὑπό, because the preposition keeps a sense that requires the dative (§1545). Compounds of σύν take the instrumental, compounds of ἐν the locative dative (§1548).

## 4. Translation guidance

- The plain indirect object is English "to", and it marks an interest in the person that a preposition does not mark: λέγειν ταῦτα τοῖς στρατιώταις to say this to the soldiers, where λέγειν πρός τινα lacks the personal interest of the dative (§1469).
- The dative of the possessor is English "have": ἐνταῦθα Κῡ́ρῳ βασίλεια ἦν there Cyrus had a palace, ὄνομα αὐτῇ Κορσώτη its name was Korsote (Goodell §524).
- The dative of advantage often comes out as an English possessive, though the sense is not the same: ἐπειδὴ αὐτοῖς οἱ βάρβαροι ἐκ τῆς χώρᾱς ἀπῆλθον after the barbarians had departed from their country (§1481).
- The dative of feeling is English "please", "you see" or "let me tell you", and sometimes cannot be given in English at all: μέμνησθέ μοι μὴ θορυβεῖν pray remember not to make a disturbance (§1486).
- A participle usually denoting inclination or aversion is added to the dative of the person interested, and English sometimes wants a verb of wishing: τῷ πλήθει τῶν Πλαταιῶν οὐ βουλομένῳ ἦν τῶν Ἀθηναίων ἀφίστασθαι the Plataean democracy did not wish to revolt from the Athenians (§1487).
- The dative of the agent is English "by", though the Greek says "for": ἐπειδὴ αὐτοῖς παρεσκεύαστο when they had got their preparations ready (§1488).
- The dative participle without a noun is English "as you" or "if you": ἡ Θρᾴκη ἐστὶν ἐπὶ δεξιὰ εἰς τὸν Πόντον εἰσπλέοντι Thrace is on the right as you sail into the Pontus (§1497).
- Many particular substantives of accompanying circumstance have no attribute and are used adverbially: θεῖν δρόμῳ to run at full speed, βίᾳ by force, σῑγῇ in silence (§1527).
- The dative with αὐτός is English "and all": εἶπεν ἥκειν εἰς τᾱ̀ς τάξεις αὐτοῖς στεφάνοις he bade them come to their posts, crowns and all (§1525). Goodell renders the commonest form of it with the crews: Τέτταρας ναῦς ἔλαβον αὐτοῖς ἀνδράσι they took four ships with the crews (Goodell §525).
- The dative of degree of difference is English "by", or nothing at all beyond the comparative: πολλῷ μείζων ἐγίγνετο ἡ βοή the shouting became much louder (§1513).
- Goodell warns that Greek often expresses a for relation where English puts the matter in some other way, as in ἀνάβηθί μοι καὶ ματύρησον come up, please, and testify (Goodell §523).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Using the dative for an agent who is plainly doing the action. The person by whom, not for whom, an action is explicitly said to be done is put in the genitive with ὑπό (§1491), and ὑπό with the genitive of the personal agent is used when the subject is a person, a city, a country, or is otherwise quasi-personal, when the verb is intransitive even if the subject is a thing, and in a few cases with an impersonal subject, usually for the sake of emphasis (§1493).
- Treating πολλῷ and ὀλίγῳ as the only forms available. With comparatives the accusatives τί, τὶ, οὐδέν, μηδέν without a substantive are always used, and in Attic prose outside Thucydides πολύ and ὀλίγον are more common than πολλῷ and ὀλίγῳ (§1514).
- Keeping the dative of respect and the accusative of respect apart by rule. The accusative of respect is often nearly equivalent to the dative of respect (§1516 a), and for the accusative of respect the instrumental dative is also employed, as are the prepositions εἰς, κατά, πρός (§1603).
- Expecting a dative of purpose on the Latin model. For the dative of purpose, common in Latin with a second dative, Greek uses a predicate noun: ἐκείνῳ ἡ χώρᾱ δῶρον ἐδόθη the country was given to him as a gift (§1473).
- Taking every dative with a verb of ruling as a genitive gone astray. Verbs of ruling often take the dative, especially in Homer, and rarely in prose, as ἡγεῖσθαί τινι to serve as guide to some one and ἐπιστατεῖν τινι to be set over one, while ἄρχειν τινί means only to be archon; only when stress is not laid on the idea of supremacy is the dative used instead of the genitive (§1537).
- Adding ἐν to a dative of time that does not need it, or leaving it off one that does. ἐν is added to words denoting time when there is no attributive, sometimes when the attributive is a pronoun, to statements of the time within the limits of which an event may take place, and always with adjectives or adverbs used substantively (§1542).
- Forgetting that these datives can become subjects. An intransitive verb taking the dative can form a personal passive, the dative becoming the nominative subject of the passive (§1468), and Smyth gives the list: ἀπειλεῖν, ἀπιστεῖν, ἐγκαλεῖν, ἐπιβουλεύειν, ἐπιτῑμᾶν, ὀνειδίζειν, πιστεύειν, πολεμεῖν, φθονεῖν (§1745 b).
- Reading a dative of relation as a dative of interest. The dative may be used of a person to whose case the statement of the predicate is limited: φευγειν αὐτοῖς ἀσφαλέστερόν ἐστιν ἢ ἡμῖν it is safer for them to flee than for us (§1495).
- Missing the dative of reference altogether. The dative of a noun or pronoun often denotes the person in whose opinion a statement holds good: πᾶσι νῑκᾶν τοῖς κριταῖς to be victorious in the judgment of all the judges (§1496).
- Reading a dative with a participle in an expression of time as an absolute construction. Ξενοφῶντι πορευομένῳ οἱ ἱππεῖς ἐντυγχάνουσι πρεσβῡ́ταις has the dative of the person interested in the action of the subject (§1498).
- Assuming the dative of a thing is always instrumental. Goodell's locative dative covers state and condition as well as place, as in ταῖς ψῦχαῖς ἐρρωμενέστεροι stronger in their souls, and the specifying dative shades out of it (Goodell §527).
