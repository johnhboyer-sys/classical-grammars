## 1. Quick answer

οὐ is the negative of fact and statement, and contradicts or denies; μή is the negative of the will and thought, and rejects or deprecates (§2688). The difference holds for the compounds as well, so that οὔτε μήτε, οὐδέ μηδέ, οὐδείς μηδείς divide the same way (§2688). Goodell says that οὐ, or a compound of οὐ, simply denies, while μή, or a compound of μή, presents the negation as willed, desired, hoped, aimed at, or assented to, or as part of an imagined or assumed case (Goodell §486). In practice the construction settles it before the sense does, and Smyth reduces the choice to six places where μή stands: with the imperative; in clauses with εἰ and ἐᾱ́ν; with the subjunctive, except after μή meaning lest; with the optative, except after μή lest, or when the optative has ἄν or stands in indirect discourse; with the infinitive, except in indirect discourse; and with participles when they have a conditional or general force (§2702). Everything else, and every exception, depends on the one warning Smyth gives beside the rule: Greek often conceives of a situation as marked by feeling where English regards it as one of fact, and hence uses μή where we should expect οὐ (§2689).

## 2. Recognition

- οὐ standing before a verb of saying or willing where English would put the negative on the infinitive after it. This is adherescent οὐ, which does not merely negative the simple verb but gives it an opposite meaning, so that οὔ φημι is I deny, I refuse, and not I say not (§2691). Monro records the same for Homer, where οὔ φημι is not I do not say but I deny, refuse, and οὐκ ἐῶ is I forbid (Monro §355).
- οὐ before an adjective or adverb, giving the opposite: οὐκ ὀλίγοι for πολλοί, οὐχ ἥκιστα for μάλιστα, οὐκ ἀφανής famous (§2694).
- A redundant μή after a verb that already means deny, refuse, hinder, forbid or avoid, added to confirm the negative idea of the leading verb: καταρνῇ μὴ δεδρᾱκέναι τάδε; "dost thou deny that thou hast done this?" (§2739). Goodwin gives the same rule and adds that this μή can be omitted without affecting the sense (GMT §815).
- μὴ οὐ, where that same leading verb is itself negatived or stands in a question implying a negative: οὐδεὶς πώποτ᾽ ἀντεῖπεν μὴ οὐ καλῶς ἔχειν αὐτούς "no one ever denied that they were excellent" (§2742).
- οὐ μή together, in an emphatic negative prediction or prohibition (§2754). Goodell treats the same idiom as one of uncertain explanation: οὐ μή with the subjunctive is a strong denial referring to the future, and οὐ μή with the future indicative may in the second person be a prohibition (Goodell §489).
- A compound negative after a simple one, which does not cancel it. If in the same clause one or more compound negatives follow a negative with the same verb, the compound negative simply confirms the first (§2761). Goodell states the rule starting from οὐ: after οὐ, alone or in composition, a compound of οὐ repeats and strengthens the negation, and so does a compound of μή after μή (Goodell §487).
- οὐδείς against μηδείς. οὐδείς and οὐδέν denote that which is actually non-existent or of no account; μηδείς and μηδέν denote that which is merely thought of as non-existent (§2736). Goodell gives their make-up: from οὐδὲ εἷς and μηδὲ εἷς, not even one, are formed the general negatives οὐδείς and μηδείς (Goodell §189).
- The phrases μὴ ὅτι and οὐχ ὅπως, meaning not to speak of, not only, not only not, or so far from, and probably due to an ellipsis of a verb of saying (§2763).
- οὐ μὴν ἀλλά and οὐ μέντοι ἀλλά, elliptical phrases meaning nevertheless, which require a verb to be supplied from the context (§2767); and οὐ μὴν οὐδέ, nor yet again (§2768).
- μόνον οὐ and ὅσον οὐ, almost, all but: μόνον οὐ διεσπάσθην "I was almost torn in pieces" (§2766).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Find the construction the negative is in, and read the rule for that construction before reading the sense. Smyth's general rule for μή is a list of constructions, not of meanings (§2702).
2. A plain statement takes οὐ. Statements expressed by simple sentences and independent clauses take οὐ, and the independent future indicative has μή only in questions (§2703).
3. A direct question takes either, and the choice tells you what answer is wanted. οὐ, ἆρ᾽ οὐ and οὐκοῦν expect the answer yes, as Latin nonne; μή, ἆρα μή and μῶν expect the answer no, as num (§2651). Goodell reads the μή questions the same way, as intimating a hope of a negative answer, and gives μή τι νεώτερον ἀγγέλλεις; "No serious news, I hope?" (Goodell §488). Gildersleeve adds the opposite idiom with οὐ: in questions the future indicative with οὐ is often used as an imperative, as οὐκ ἀποκτενεῖτε; (Gildersleeve §271).
4. A wish takes μή, with the indicative or the optative: εἴθε σε μήποτ᾽ εἰδόμᾱν "would that I had never seen thee" (§2704). That ὤφελον takes μή and not οὐ shows that it has lost its verbal nature to a certain extent (§2704 a).
5. A command or prohibition takes μή, because the imperative is a mood of will (§2709). Gildersleeve states the same and adds the tense rule: the imperative in prohibitions takes the negative μή (Gildersleeve §414), and μή with the present imperative has to do with a course of action, meaning sometimes keep from and sometimes cease to, while μή with the aorist imperative or subjunctive gives a total prohibition (Gildersleeve §415). Gildersleeve also warns that μή with the future indicative in prohibitions is rare and hardly sure, resting in Attic prose on two suspicious passages, and that the jussive future has οὐ (Gildersleeve §270).
6. A subjunctive takes μή, because the subjunctive is a mood of will (§2706). That covers the hortatory, prohibitive, deliberative and doubtful subjunctive alike (§2707), and the dependent uses, final clauses and object clauses after verbs of effort among them (§2708). The one exception is the clause after μή meaning lest, where οὐ is used (§2708 a).
7. A conditional clause regularly takes μή: εἰ μὴ ῡ̔μεῖς ἤλθετε, ἐπορευόμεθα ἂν ἐπὶ βασιλέᾱ "if you had not come, we should be marching against the king" (§2705 c). Goodwin gives the rule: the negative particle of the protasis is regularly μή, that of the apodosis οὐ (GMT §383).
8. If a conditional clause has οὐ instead, ask what the οὐ is attached to. When οὐ is found in a protasis it is generally closely connected with a particular word, especially the verb, so that its negative force does not affect the protasis as a whole (GMT §384). Smyth lists the cases: adherescent οὐ, an emphatic assertion of fact, εἰ used for ὅτι after verbs of emotion, εἰ approaching the idea of ἐπεί, and a bimembered protasis (§2698). Goodwin's clearest example is Smyth's too, εἰ μὴ Πρόξενον οὐχ ὑπεδέξαντο, ἐσώθησαν ἄν, where the μή affects the whole clause and the οὐ only the verb (GMT §384).
9. If the text is Homer, expect the position of the clause to decide. Homer has εἰ and the indicative with οὐ twelve times when the subordinate clause precedes the main clause, but usually εἰ μή when the subordinate clause follows (§2699). Monro states the same law from Vierke and calls it curious: with εἰ and the indicative οὐ is used when the clause with εἰ precedes the principal clause, and μή when it follows (Monro §359).
10. A relative clause takes οὐ with a definite antecedent and μή with an indefinite one, and μή is thus used when the case in question is typical of a class (§2705 d). Where the antecedent is thought of in respect of its character, of such a sort, μή appears even of a definite person, and Sophocles is especially fond of this generic μή (§2705 g).
11. A result clause splits by mood. Consecutive clauses with ὥστε take οὐ with the indicative and optative (§2705 h); ὥστε with the infinitive takes μή in ordinary result clauses, μή sympathetic after verbs of hindering, and μὴ οὐ after a negatived verb of hindering (§2759).
12. An infinitive not in indirect discourse takes μή; an infinitive in indirect discourse takes οὐ, though sometimes μή (§2711). μή is the regular negative after all verbs, adjectives, adverbs and substantives which take an infinitive not in indirect discourse (§2713), including verbs of commanding, asking and advising (§2720) and the infinitive of purpose or result (§2717). Verbs of saying and thinking take οὐ, retained from the direct discourse: λέγοντες οὐκ εἶναι αὐτόνομοι "saying that they were not independent" (§2722). Goodell gives the same usual rule and the same exception, that the negative with an infinitive is usually μή except when the infinitive represents an original finite verb which had οὐ (Goodell §564).
13. If the leading verb is one of hoping, promising or swearing, expect μή even in indirect discourse. μή is often used with verbs and expressions of asseveration and belief, where the use of μή indicates strong assurance, confidence and resolve, generally about the future: ὑπῑσχνοῦντο μηδὲν χαλεπὸν αὐτοὺς πείσεσθαι "they promised that they should suffer no harm" (§2725). Smyth lists them from ἐλπίζω and προσδοκῶ through ὑπισχνοῦμαι and ὄμνῡμι to μαρτυρῶ and ἀπειλῶ, with μή regular after verbs of promising and common after verbs of hoping and swearing (§2726). Goodwin lists the same intermediate class, verbs of hoping, promising and swearing with those signifying to agree, to trust, to be persuaded and to testify, and notes that the infinitive occasionally has μή even after φημί, λέγω, νομίζω and ἡγοῦμαι (GMT §685). Monro says the usage begins in Homer, where a dependent infinitive takes μή in oaths and generally after verbs of promising (Monro §361).
14. A participle takes οὐ when it states a fact and μή when it states a condition: οὐ πιστεύων is since he does not believe, μὴ πιστεύων is if he does not believe (§2728). The negative of the participle is οὐ except when the participle has a general or conditional force, or stands in a sentence which requires μή (§2045). With ὡς, ὥσπερ, ἅτε, οἷον and οἷα the participle has οὐ, which shows that there is nothing conditional in the use of ὡς however often it is translated as if (§2732), and participles of opposition or concession take οὐ (§2733).
15. If the participle has the article, ask whether a definite person is meant. The participle with the article has οὐ when a definite person or thing is meant, and μή when the idea is indefinite and virtually conditional: οἱ οὐκ ὄντες is the dead, while οἱ μὴ δυνάμενοι is any who are unable (§2734). μή with the articular participle is the abridged equivalent of a conditional relative sentence (§2728 a).
16. The same test applies to nouns. οὐ and μή are used with substantives and substantivized adjectives with the same difference as with participles, and there the generic μή is much more common: ἡ τῶν γεφῡρῶν οὐ διάλυσις is the non-destruction of the bridges, ὁ μὴ ῑ̓ᾱτρός is the non-physician (§2735). The negative here stands for the negative compounds Greek lacks (§2735 a).
17. In indirect discourse introduced by ὅτι or ὡς, keep the negative of the direct form (§2710). The same negative used in the direct discourse is commonly kept in the indirect, though with the infinitive and participle μή sometimes takes the place of οὐ (§2608). Indirect questions likewise usually preserve the negative of the direct form, with οὐ usual after interrogative pronouns and adverbs, and μή after verbs of seeing and considering when there is an idea of purpose or of preventing something (§2676).
18. If μή stands where you expected οὐ, look up the sentence for a verb that governs it. Where μή is used when we expect οὐ, the negative expression usually depends on a verb that either has μή or would have it if negatived, so after imperatives, after conditional expressions, and after a verb such as κελεύει or ὑπέσχετο, which would take μή if it were negatived (§2737).
19. If οὐ stands where you expected μή, look for the word it is stuck to. οὐ appears there when it goes strictly with the leading verb though it stands with the infinitive, when it marks a contrast, when a compound negative takes up an οὐ used with the leading verb, and when οὐδείς may be resolved into οὐ and τὶς (§2738). The same reasoning covers οὐ in a protasis (§2696) and the οὐ that clings to verbs of saying and thinking and to many verbs of will or desire (§2692).
20. Count the negatives before you translate them. If a simple negative with a verb follows another negative in the same clause, each keeps its own force when they belong to different words, and they make an affirmative when they belong to the same word: οὐδεὶς οὐκ ἔπασχέ τι is "no one was not suffering something", that is, everybody suffered (§2760). A compound negative after a simple one only confirms it (§2761), and a negative may be repeated in another clause for emphasis or through lax structure (§2762). Monro traces the habit to its cause: double negation is caused by the tendency to repeat the negative particle with any word or phrase to which the negation especially applies (Monro §357).
21. If the sentence is Homeric, do not expect the later compounds. The form μηδείς is post-Homeric except the neuter μηδέν, which occurs once, and even οὐδέν is only occasionally found in Homer, sometimes as an emphatic adverb and sometimes as a substantive (Monro §356).

## 4. Translation guidance

- Adherescent οὐ moves into the English verb. οὐκ ἔφη ἰέναι is "he refused to go", and οὔ φᾱσιν εἶναι ἄλλην ὁδόν is "they say that there is no other road" (§2692). Smyth lists the verbs from οὔ φημι and οὐ φάσκω deny through οὐκ ἐῶ forbid to οὐκ ἐπαινῶ disapprove (§2692 a).
- Where οὐ negatives an adjective, English usually uses the positive. οὐκ ἐλάχιστος is greatest, οὐ καλῶς is basely, and οὐ πάνυ χαλεπόν is easy (§2694).
- The redundant μή after a verb of hindering is not translated. Smyth prints English and French and German parallels for it, and Goodwin says that when μή or μὴ οὐ with the infinitive follows a verb of hindrance, neither μή nor μὴ οὐ can be translated (GMT §816).
- μὴ οὐ after a negatived verb is likewise untranslatable, because the οὐ after the strengthening μή only confirms the οὐ prefixed to the leading verb (§2742 a). But where μή really negatives the infinitive, μὴ οὐ must be translated by one negative (GMT §816).
- Where the leading verb is not negative in meaning but is itself negatived, μὴ οὐ carries a real negative: οὐκ ἂν πιθοίμην μὴ οὐ τάδ᾽ ἐκμαθεῖν σαφῶς "I cannot consent not to learn this exactly as it is" (§2745). The same construction follows expressions of impossibility, impropriety and moral repugnance even when nothing negatives them (§2746, GMT §817).
- With αἰσχῡ́νη and δεινόν the negative idea is in the noun: ὥστε πᾶσιν αἰσχῡ́νην εἶναι μὴ οὐ συσπουδάζειν is "so that all were ashamed not to coöperate zealously" (§2748).
- μὴ οὐ with a participle after a negatived expression has the force of except or unless: οὐκ ἄρα ἐστὶν φίλον τῷ φιλοῦντι οὐδὲν μὴ οὐκ ἀντιφιλοῦν "nothing then is beloved by a lover except it love in return" (§2750).
- οὐ μή makes an emphatic future denial or a blunt prohibition. οὐ μὴ δυνήσεται Κῦρος εὑρεῖν is "Cyrus will not be able to find", and οὐ μὴ καταβήσει is "don't come down" (§2755, §2756). Goodell's rendering of οὐ μὴ λαλήσεις is "Don't keep chattering" (Goodell §489).
- Prohibitions need different English for the two tenses. μὴ λάλει may be stop talking or keep mum according to circumstances, while μὴ θαυμάσῃς is a total prohibition, don't be astonished (Gildersleeve §415).
- Redundant οὐ after πλήν, χωρίς, ἐκτός, ἄνευ and after πρίν preceded by a negative is not translated: πλὴν οὐκ εἰς Ἀθήνᾱς is "except to Athens" (§2753).
- μὴ ὅτι and οὐχ ὅπως want not only, not only not, or, in the second of two balanced clauses after a negative, much less: οὐδὲ πλεῖν, μὴ ὅτι ἀναιρεῖσθαι τοὺς ἄνδρας δυνατὸν ἦν is "it was not possible even to sail, much less to rescue the man" (§2763). Beside them stands the ordinary οὐ μόνον . . . ἀλλὰ καί, not only but also (§2764).
- ὅ τι μή and ὅσον μή, without any verb, limit a preceding assertion and want except or so far as: οὐ γὰρ ἦν κρήνη, ὅ τι μὴ μία ἐν αὐτῇ τῇ ἀκροπόλει "for there was no spring, except one on the acropolis itself" (§2765).
- Where two negatives make an affirmative, say the affirmative. οὐδὲ τὸν Φορμίων᾽ ἐκεῖνος οὐχ ὁρᾷ is "he sees him very well" (§2760).
- The οὐ of swearing sits in μά. μά is negative except when preceded by ναί, so that μὰ τὸν Δία and οὐ μὰ τὸν Δία are both "nay, by Zeus", against ναὶ μὰ τὸν Δία and νὴ τὸν Δία (§1596 b).
- οὐ μὴν ἀλλά and similar phrases want nevertheless, and the colloquial οὐ γὰρ ἀλλά has about the force of nay, for indeed (§2767).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading two Greek negatives as cancelling each other. A compound negative following a negative with the same verb only confirms it, so that οὐδεὶς οὐδὲν πενίᾳ δρᾱ́σει is "no one will do anything because of want" (§2761). The affirmative appears only when the two negatives belong to the same word or expression (§2760).
- Translating the redundant μή after a verb of hindering or denying. It is there to confirm the negative idea of the leading verb (§2739), and after verbs negative in meaning μή and μὴ οὐ cannot be translated in modern English (§2742 a).
- Reading μὴ οὐ as a double negative that yields a positive. οὐ there is the sympathetic negative and is untranslatable (§2745).
- Taking the negative that stands with the infinitive as belonging to the infinitive. οὐ may go strictly with the leading verb although it stands with the infinitive (§2738 b), and the negative may be placed in front of an infinitive where English transfers it to another verb (§2690 c). Monro finds the same wavering in Homer, where a negative that belongs in sense to the infinitive is placed before the governing verb, and where the language sometimes seems to use the two forms indifferently (Monro §360).
- Deciding between οὐ and μή from the English rather than from the Greek construction. Greek uses μή where the situation is conceived as marked by feeling, even when English regards it as one of fact (§2689).
- Reading a μή in a conditional clause as though it were the only possibility, and being stopped by an οὐ. οὐ is sometimes found in clauses introduced by εἰ and ἐᾱ́ν, and the use of μή or οὐ was determined by the feeling of the speaker at the moment as to the scope of his negation (GMT §384).
- Reading a participle with μή as a statement of fact. μή with a participle marks a condition (§2728), and it also appears when the reason for an action is regarded as the condition under which it takes place (§2731).
- Missing the difference between τὰ οὐκ ὄντα and τὰ μὴ ὄντα. The first is that which does not exist independently of any opinion of the writer; the second is that which is regarded as not existing, the whole sum of things outside actual knowledge (§2688 a).
- Treating οὐδείς and μηδείς as free variants. οὐδείς denotes what is actually non-existent or of no account and μηδείς what is merely thought of so, though the construction may also decide the choice (§2736).
- Expecting the negative to stand before the word it negatives. οὐ and μή are generally placed before that word but may follow when emphasis is laid on a particular word, as in contrasts, and a contrast must be supplied in thought when the negative precedes the article, a relative, a conjunction or a preposition (§2690).
- Reading μή as the negative adverb when it is a conjunction. Goodell notes that μή is often a subordinating conjunction, lest, that, that not, after expressions of fear or caution (Goodell §610), and Smyth's own rule for the subjunctive makes an exception for that use, since after μή lest the negative is οὐ (§2708 a).
- Forgetting that the compounds keep the difference. Goodell notes that οὐδέ and μηδέ are the negatives of δέ and καί and differ in meaning exactly as simple οὐ and μή do (Goodell §667).
- Reading the μή that follows a verb of hindering with the article as an ordinary negative. Smyth lists those under verbs of negative meaning, where the article τό or τοῦ may be added (§2744), and Goodell states that with such verbs the infinitive may have the article τοῦ as a from genitive (Goodell §572).
- Assuming Homer has the later apparatus. μηδείς is post-Homeric except for one instance of μηδέν (Monro §356), and Homer's μή with the indicative is confined to a few idioms, μὴ ὤφελλον, oaths, questions after ἦ, and verbs of fearing that relate to a past event (Monro §358).
