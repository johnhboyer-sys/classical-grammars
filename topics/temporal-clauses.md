# Temporal Clauses

Status: drafted 2026-09-07 on the pattern of `topics/genitive-absolute.md`
and `topics/result-clauses.md`. Every claim carries a section citation;
`scripts/verify_topic_pages.py` checks them. `topic: temporal-clauses`
names the derived record in `data/smyth/topics.json`.

## 1. Quick answer

Temporal clauses are introduced by conjunctions or relative expressions having the force of conjunctions, and Smyth sorts them by the time they mark against the principal verb: the same time, ὅτε, ὁπότε, ἡνίκα, ὁπηνίκα when, ὁσάκις as often as, ἕως, μέχρι, ὅσον χρόνον so long as, ἕως, ἐν ᾧ while; prior time, ἐπεί, ἐπειδή after, after that, ἐπεὶ πρῶτον, ὡς τάχιστα as soon as, ἐξ οὗ, ἀφ᾽ οὗ since, ever since, ὡς when, as soon as; and subsequent time, ἕως, ἔστε, μέχρι, μέχρι οὗ until, and πρίν, πρότερον ἤ before, until (§2383). A temporal clause is definite when the action occurs at a definite point of time (§2391), and indefinite when it occurs in the indefinite future, recurs an indefinite number of times, or continues for an indefinite period (§2392). That distinction fixes the mood: when the time is definite, the indicative; when indefinite, the subjunctive with ἄν, the optative, or rarely the indicative (§2394). Temporal conjunctions with the subjunctive take ἄν, and ἄν is not used with the optative except when the optative is potential (§2394).

## 2. Recognition

- A conjunction from one of Smyth's three lists, with a demonstrative adverb often answering it in the principal clause, such as ὅτε answered by τότε, ἕως by τέως, and ἐπεί by τότε (§2384).
- ὅταν, ὁπόταν, ἐπᾱ́ν, ἐπήν, ἐπειδά̆ν: the addition of ἄν to the conjunction produces these forms, and ἐπᾱ́ν and ἐπήν are both rare in Attic (§2399 a).
- The indicative in a clause fixed to one occasion: ὅτε ταῦτα ἦν, σχεδὸν μέσαι ἦσαν νύκτες "it was about midnight when this was taking place"; ἡνίκα δὲ δείλη ἐγίγνετο, ἐφάνη κονιορτός "but when it was getting to be afternoon, a cloud of dust appeared" (§2395).
- ἐν ᾧ of a stretch of time inside which something happens: ἐν ᾧ ὡπλίζοντο, ἧκον . . . οἱ σκοποί "while they were arming, the scouts came" (§2395).
- The subjunctive with ἄν in a clause looking forward: μέχρι δ᾽ ἂν ἐγὼ ἥκω, αἱ σπονδαὶ μενόντων "but until I return, let the armistice continue"; περιμένετε ἔστ᾽ ἂν ἐγὼ ἔλθω "wait until I come" (§2401).
- The optative without ἄν answering an optative in the principal clause: πεινῶν φάγοι ἂν ὁπότε βούλοιτο "when hungry he would eat whenever he wished" (§2404).
- The subjunctive with ἄν under a present of habit or a general truth: μαινόμεθα πάντες ὁπόταν ὀργιζώμεθα "we are all mad whenever we are angry" (§2410).
- The optative under an imperfect of habit: ἐθήρευεν ἀπὸ ἵππου ὁπότε γυμνάσαι βούλοιτο ἑαυτόν "he was wont to hunt on horseback, whenever he wanted to exercise himself" (§2414).
- μέχρι τούτου in the principal clause as the implied or expressed antecedent of an until clause: μέχρι τούτου Λᾱσθένης φίλος ὠνομάζετο, ἕως προὔδωκεν Ὄλυνθον "Lasthenes was called a friend (up to the time when) until he betrayed Olynthus" (§2428).
- πρίν, which is construed like other conjunctions meaning until except that it takes the infinitive as well as the indicative, subjunctive and optative (§2430).
- πρότερον ἤ, πρόσθεν ἤ, πρὶν ἤ and Homeric πάρος in the sense of πρίν (§2458, §2459, §2460, §2461).
- Goodell groups the same clauses by their conjunctions: ὅτε and ὁπότε (Goodell §627), ἐπεί and ἐπειδή (Goodell §629), ἡνίκα and ὁπηνίκα, which generally define the time more precisely, just when, at the very time when (Goodell §630), ἕως, ἔστε, μέχρι and ἄχρι (Goodell §631), and πρίν (Goodell §643).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Find the conjunction and look it up in Smyth's three lists, which sort the conjunctions by whether the clause marks the same time as the principal verb, time prior to it, or time subsequent to it (§2383). ἕως, μέχρι and ἔστε each appear in two of the three lists, so the list alone will not settle the sense (§2383).
2. Read ἕως with care, because Smyth distinguishes its two values by what the actions do: ἕως means so long as in reference to actions that are coëxtensive, and while in reference to actions not coëxtensive (§2383). The duration use and the termination use have separate rules, one for so long as, while (§2422), and one for until, till (§2425).
3. Do not force the clause into one exclusive time relation. The time denoted by a temporal clause is not always solely contemporaneous, antecedent or subsequent to that of the principal clause, but may overlap with it, before and at the same time, at the same time and after, until and after (§2388). Smyth's own example is read that way: τοιαῦτα ἐποίει ἕως διεδίδου πάντα ἃ ἔλαβε κρέᾱ "he kept doing thus until he saw that (and so long as) he was distributing all the meat he had received" (§2388).
4. Decide whether the clause is definite or indefinite, because that decides the mood. A temporal clause is definite when the action occurs at a definite point of time, and definite temporal clauses usually refer to the present or to the past (§2391). It is indefinite when the action occurs in the indefinite future, recurs an indefinite number of times, or continues for an indefinite period, and indefinite temporal clauses refer either to the future or to general present or past time (§2392). The same temporal conjunction may refer either to definite or to indefinite time, sometimes with a difference of meaning (§2393).
5. Now check the mood against that decision. When the time is definite, the indicative is used; when indefinite, the subjunctive with ἄν, the optative, or rarely the indicative (§2394). Present or past temporal clauses take the indicative when the action is marked as a fact and refers to a definite occasion (§2395). The future indicative is rarely used in temporal clauses, and when used refers to definite time (§2398); it is rare because that tense does not usually make clear the difference between action continuing and action simply occurring in the future, and ὅτε with the future indicative has thus been almost entirely displaced by ὅταν with the subjunctive (§2398 a).
6. If ἄν is missing where you expect it, do not conclude that the clause is definite. The subjunctive without ἄν is sometimes found in poetry and in Herodotus, and in Attic prose with μέχρι and μέχρι οὗ until and with πρίν (§2402). ἄν is frequently omitted in Homer, and occasionally in lyric and dramatic poetry and in Herodotus (§2412). GMT gathers the same omission after ἕως and the other particles meaning until, most frequent in tragic poetry and occurring with ἐς ὅ or ἐς οὗ in Herodotus and with μέχρι and μέχρι οὗ in Herodotus and Thucydides (GMT §620), and separately for πρίν even in Attic prose (GMT §648).
7. Separate temporal from causal. Some temporal conjunctions also denote cause: ὅτε, ὁπότε, ἐπεί, εὖτε, ἐπειδή since, whereas, and ὡς because (§2385). Causal ὅτε and ὁπότε are rare, and Smyth's examples of them are ὅτε τοίνυν τοῦθ᾽ οὕτως ἔχει "since then this is the case" and χαλεπὰ . . . τὰ παρόντα ὁπότ᾽ ἀνδρῶν στρατηγῶν τοιούτων στερόμεθα "the present state of affairs is difficult since we are deprived of such generals" (§2240 b). The structural test is Smyth's own: ὅτε, ἔνθα and ὡς are subordinating conjunctions when the clause introduced by them fixes the time, place or manner of the main clause, and relative adverbs when they serve only to define the antecedent (§2389 b). So ask whether the clause tells you when, or tells you why. Goodell reports the same double value for ὅτε and ὁπότε, which often take a causal meaning and rarely a concessive one (Goodell §627), and for ἐπεί (Goodell §629).
8. If the clause looks causal, check the mood as well. Causal clauses denoting a fact regularly take the indicative after primary and secondary tenses (§2241), while causal clauses denoting an alleged or reported reason take the optative after secondary tenses (§2242). GMT states the same pair, with the indicative as the rule and the negative οὐ (GMT §713), and the optative when the speaker implies that a cause was assigned by some other person (GMT §714). An optative in a clause that could be either causal or temporal is therefore not automatically a temporal optative.
9. Separate temporal from conditional, and expect the two to come close. A temporal sentence and a conditional sentence may occur in close conjunction without marked difference of signification (§2386), and a temporal conjunction is often used in Greek where English uses a conditional or a concessive conjunction (§2387), as in οὐκ ἂν ἔγωγε Κρονῑ́ονος ἆσσον ἱκοίμην, . . . ὅτε μὴ αὐτός γε κελεύοι "I would not draw nearer to Cronus' son unless (lit. when not) he should himself bid me" (§2387). The temporal conjunctions have, in general, the same constructions as conditional ἐᾱ́ν or εἰ, so that ὁπόταν answers to ἐᾱ́ν ποτε and ὁπότε to εἴ ποτε (§2399 b). Clauses introduced by relative adverbs of time have in general the same constructions as clauses introduced by relative pronouns (§2389). GMT puts the point in terms of what the antecedent does: an until clause referring to an actual past occurrence is a temporal clause with the construction of a relative clause with a definite antecedent, but when it refers to the future it becomes a conditional relative clause, and the conditional force comes from the indefinite antecedent (GMT §612). Monro says the same of ἐπεί in Homer, whose use implies that the action is prior in time to the action of the principal clause, so that clauses with ἐπεί properly fall under the definition of the conditional clause (Monro §296).
10. Read the negative as a confirmation of that decision, not as the decision itself. The negative of a definite temporal clause is οὐ, except when the special construction requires μή (§2391); the negative of an indefinite one is μή (§2392). In a present or past clause the negative is μή only when the temporal relation is regarded as conditional: ὁπότε τὸ δίκαιον μὴ οἶδα, ὅ ἐστι, σχολῇ εἴσομαι "when (if) I do not know what justice is, I am scarcely likely to know" (§2397). GMT states the same rule for relative and temporal clauses together: when the antecedent is indefinite the negative is μή, when definite οὐ, unless the general construction requires μή (GMT §518).
11. If the conjunction means until, ask what the principal clause is doing. When the principal clause is affirmative, it is implied that its action continues only up to the time when the action of the until clause takes place (§2429). When the principal clause is negative, it is implied that the action of the principal clause does not take place until the action of the until clause takes place: οὐ πρότερον ἐπαύσαντο ἕως τὴν πόλιν εἰς στάσεις κατέστησαν "they did not stop until they divided the city into factions" (§2429 a). GMT frames the same reading as an inference the reader supplies: the idea of a clause with until is that the action of the leading clause continues to a time at which that of the dependent clause takes place, and that the former action then ceases is an inference generally made, but not positively implied in the language, and not necessary (GMT §611).
12. If the mood is the optative without ἄν after a secondary tense, test for indirect discourse before reading a wish or a potential. After a secondary tense introducing indirect discourse, real or implied, the optative may represent the subjunctive with ἄν as the form in which the thought was conceived: ἐπιμεῖναι κελεύσαντες ἔστε βουλεύσαιντο, ἐθῡ́οντο "ordering them to wait until they had taken counsel, they proceeded to sacrifice", where the direct form was ἐπιμείνατε ἔστ᾽ ἂν βουλευσώμεθα (§2408). The same optative may carry an expectation, hope or purpose on the part of the subject of the main verb (§2420). GMT gives that use of ἕως as its own heading, and notes that the subjunctive may be kept instead, to retain the mood in which the purpose would be originally conceived (GMT §614).
13. If the conjunction is πρίν, apply Smyth's rule first. After an affirmative clause πρίν usually takes the infinitive and means before (§2431). After a negative clause πρίν means until, and usually takes the indicative of definite time, or the subjunctive or optative of indefinite time (§2432). The subjunctive or optative is never used with πρίν unless the principal clause is negative, and when the principal clause is negative πρίν is construed like ἕως and the other words for until (§2432 a, §2432 b). GMT reaches the same rule from the history: πρίν takes the infinitive chiefly when the leading clause is affirmative, and the subjunctive and optative are never used unless the leading clause is negative or involves a negative idea (GMT §622), a principle that seems to have allowed of no exceptions (GMT §647).
14. Test a doubtful πρίν against Smyth's contrasting examples. ταῦτα ἐποίουν πρὶν Σωκράτης ἀφῑ́κετο is "I was doing this until Socrates arrived"; οὐ ταῦτα ἐποίουν πρὶν Σωκράτης ἀφῑ́κετο is "I was not doing this until (or before) Socrates arrived"; ταῦτα ἐποίουν πρὶν Σωκράτην ἀφικέσθαι is "I was doing this before Socrates arrived" (§2434). It is correct to say οὐ ποιήσω τοῦτο πρὶν ἂν κελεύσῃς and ποιήσω (or οὐ ποιήσω) τοῦτο πρὶν κελεῦσαι, but incorrect to say ποιήσω τοῦτο πρὶν ἂν κελεύσῃς (§2435).
15. Watch for a principal clause that is affirmative in form but virtually negative, because πρίν with the subjunctive is then in order. Smyth reads αἰσχρόν there as οὐ καλόν or οὐ δεῖν, and glosses πρὶν ἂν ῡ̔μεῖς περὶ αὐτῶν ὅ τι ἂν βούλησθε ψηφίσησθε as "until you have voted what you wish" (§2447). GMT reads that same sentence as practically equivalent to I refuse to stop until you have voted what you wish (GMT §647).
16. If a πρίν clause has the infinitive after a negative clause, read before and not until. The infinitive must be used, even with negative clauses, when πρίν must mean only before, and it is obligatory in Attic when the action of the πρίν clause does not take place or is not to take place (§2453, §2453 a). When the main clause is negative, πρίν with the infinitive generally means before, rarely until, and when before and after are contrasted, until is out of place and the πρίν clause often precedes (§2455). GMT gives the reader's test for the same cases: πρὶν ἂν ἀκούσῃ, until he hears, would suggest the wrong idea in καί μοι μὴ θορυβήσῃ μηδεὶς πρὶν ἀκοῦσαι, and in another example the irony of the question would make until absurd (GMT §628).
17. If the principal clause is a past indicative with ἄν denoting non-fulfilment, expect the temporal clause to denote non-fulfilment too. By assimilation of mood the temporal clause then has a past tense of the indicative denoting non-fulfilment (§2396), as in οὐκ ἂν ἐπαυόμην . . . ἕως ἀπεπειρᾱ́θην τῆς σοφίᾱς ταυτησῑ́ "I would not cease until I had made trial of this wisdom" (§2396). A πρίν clause behaves the same way: χρῆν τοίνυν Λεπτίνην μὴ πρότερον τιθέναι τὸν ἑαυτοῦ νόμον πρὶν τοῦτον ἔλῡσε "Leptines ought not then to have proposed his own law until (before) he had repealed this" (§2442). GMT records the same construction for πρίν, with examples only of the aorist indicative after negative sentences (GMT §637).
18. If the passage is Homer, drop the Attic rules for πρίν. Homer commonly uses the infinitive with πρίν meaning before and until, and never uses the indicative with πρίν, the required sense being given by ἕως or πρίν γ᾽ ὅτε δή; he has the optative with πρίν only once (§2438). Homer does not use κέ or ἄν with the subjunctive after πρίν, since πρίν is there adverbial and its clause is simply coördinated (§2444 b). GMT counts the Homeric usage: no case of πρίν with the indicative, four cases of πρίν γ᾽ ὅτε with the indicative, six of πρίν without ἄν or κέ with the subjunctive, one with the optative, and 81 with the infinitive (GMT §623). Monro adds the reason for the moods: the subjunctive is used without κεν or ἄν because it is not meant to lay stress on a particular occasion when the condition will be fulfilled, and the use of πρὶν ἄν with the subjunctive is post-Homeric (Monro §297).
19. When the context does not decide between two readings, keep both open in the English. Smyth himself leaves the choice open where a temporal clause and a conditional clause differ so little (§2386), and where a temporal clause overlaps the time of the principal clause (§2388). Where the clause could be temporal or causal, note that Smyth marks ὅτε and ὁπότε in the causal sense as rare (§2240 b) and that the causal force of ἐπεί and ἐπειδή is ordinary (§2385), and let the leading clause decide rather than the conjunction.

## 4. Translation guidance

- ὅτε and ὁπότε are when and whenever; ἡνίκα and ὁπηνίκα have the force of what time, at the moment when, and are more precise than ὅτε (§2383). Goodell renders ἡνίκα just when, at the very time when (Goodell §630).
- ἕως is so long as of actions that are coëxtensive and while of actions that are not (§2383). Of a definite present or past duration it takes the indicative, and the present often connotes cause, while, now that, because (§2422).
- ἕως until takes the indicative of a definite present or past action, and there too the present connotes cause (§2425). English then wants until: ἔμειναν ἕως ἀφῑ́κοντο οἱ στρατηγοί "they waited until the generals arrived" (§2395).
- ἐπεί and ἐπειδή are after, after that, and less exactly when; ἐπεὶ πρῶτον, ὡς τάχιστα and ἐπειδὴ τάχιστα are as soon as; ἐξ οὗ, ἐξ ὅτου and ἀφ᾽ οὗ are since, ever since (§2383). Smyth's example of the last: ἐξ οὗ φίλος εἶναι προσποιεῖται, ἐκ τούτου ῡ̔μᾶς ἐξηπάτηκεν "ever since Philip pretended to be friendly, from that time on he had deceived you" (§2395).
- After a conjunction of antecedent action English usually wants a perfect or a pluperfect where Greek has an aorist. Smyth notes that the aorist, and not the pluperfect, is commonly used to denote time previous to that of the main verb (§2395).
- In an indefinite clause the tense marks the stage of the action rather than its date: the present marks the action as continuing (not completed), the aorist marks it as simply occurring (completed); the present usually sets forth an action contemporaneous with that of the leading verb, the aorist an action antecedent to it (§2400).
- Under a past habit the optative is best rendered by an English past habitual: περιεμένομεν ἑκάστοτε ἕως ἀνοιχθείη τὸ δεσμωτήριον "we used to wait about on each occasion until the prison was opened" (§2414).
- ὁσάκις is as often as, and takes the indicative of particular events of repeated occurrence: ὁσάκις κεχορήγηκε . . . νενῑ́κηκε "as often as he has been choregus, he has gained a victory" (§2417).
- πρίν after an affirmative clause is before: ἐπὶ τὸ ἄκρον ἀναβαίνει Χειρίσοφος πρίν τινας αἰσθέσθαι τῶν πολεμίων "Chirisophus ascended the height before any of the enemy perceived him" (§2454).
- πρίν after a negative clause is until: οὐ πρότερον κακῶν παύσονται αἱ πόλεις πρὶν ἂν ἐν αὐταῖς οἱ φιλόσοφοι ἄρξωσιν "States will not cease from evil until philosophers become rulers in them" (§2444), and μὴ ἀπέλθητε πρὶν ἂν ἀκούσητε "do not go away until you hear" (§2433). Goodell gives the same two values, an indicative of past fact and a subjunctive usually with ἄν of a supposed or anticipated case (Goodell §644).
- Where the negative clause is a report, English keeps the should: ἀπηγόρευε μηδένα βάλλειν πρὶν Κῦρος ἐμπλησθείη θηρῶν "he forbade any one to shoot until Cyrus should have had his fill of hunting" (§2449).
- A πρίν clause with the infinitive after a negative main clause is still before: καί μοι μὴ θορυβήσῃ μηδεὶς πρὶν ἀκοῦσαι "and let no one raise a disturbance before he hears" (§2455).
- φθάνω in the principal clause takes the English succeed in doing a thing first: φθήσονται πλεύσαντες πρὶν τὴν ξυμφορᾱ̀ν Χίους αἰσθέσθαι "they will succeed in making their voyage before the Chians hear of the disaster" (§2440 a). GMT collects the same idiom and translates ἔφθησαν ἀπικόμενοι πρὶν ἢ τοὺς βαρβάρους ἥκειν "they arrived before the barbarians came" (GMT §660).
- ἢ πρίν is than before, with a past tense suppressed after ἤ, and occurs first in Xenophon (§2457). GMT prints the fuller example, πολὺ μὲν ἐλάττονές εἰσιν νῦν ἢ πρὶν ἡττηθῆναι, and reports that this ellipsis occurs first in Xenophon (GMT §631).
- The English of a Homeric πρίν clause is usually before, whatever the leading clause: ναῖε δὲ Πήδαιον πρὶν ἐλθεῖν υἷας Ἀχαιῶν "he dwelt in Pedaeon before the sons of the Achaeans came" (§2456).
- πρότερον ἤ is sooner than, before, and is used especially in Herodotus and Thucydides, with the indicative, the infinitive, or rarely the subjunctive without ἄν (§2458). πρόσθεν ἤ is the same (§2459), as in ἀπεκρῑ́νατο . . . ὅτι πρόσθεν ἂν ἀποθάνοιεν ἢ τὰ ὅπλα παραδοίησαν "he answered that they would die before (sooner than that) they would surrender their arms" (§2459).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading ὅτε or ὁπότε as causal because English since would fit. As causal conjunctions they are rare (§2240 b), and their ordinary force is temporal.
- Reading ἐπεί as temporal when it is causal. ἐπεί and ἐπειδή also mean since, whereas (§2385), and Goodell reports the causal use as ordinary alongside the temporal one (Goodell §629).
- Insisting on one exclusive time relation. The clause may overlap the time of the principal clause in either direction (§2388), and conjunctions of antecedent action usually take the aorist and only rarely the imperfect, except when that tense represents overlapping action (§2388 a).
- Expecting ἄν wherever the subjunctive appears. Its omission is normal in Homer (§2412) and occurs in Attic prose with μέχρι and μέχρι οὗ (§2402); Smyth adds that the omission is more common after temporal conjunctions than after εἰ, and that in writers later than Homer it lends an archaic colouring to the style (§2402).
- Adding ἄν to the optative. ἄν is not used with the optative except when the optative is potential (§2394); ἕως ἄν with the optative is rare and generally to be defended as potential (§2421), and πρὶν ἄν with the optative is rare and suspected (§2452).
- Reading an optative in an until clause as a wish or a bare potential when it is reported thought. Such an optative may represent ἄν with the subjunctive after a primary tense (§2427 b), and the same optative in a πρίν clause is in indirect discourse, actual or implied (§2449).
- Taking a πρίν subjunctive after a plainly affirmative clause. The subjunctive or optative is never used with πρίν unless the principal clause is negative (§2432 a), so an apparent case is either a virtually negative idea (§2447) or a text worth checking.
- Rendering πρίν as until where the Greek requires before. The infinitive is obligatory in Attic when the action of the πρίν clause does not take place or is not to take place (§2453 a), and the choice of construction, not the context alone, tells you which English word to use (§2434).
- Reading the aorist in a πρίν clause as a statement about time. πρίν commonly takes the aorist in all the moods because of its negative force, the aorist having an affinity for the negative since it marks simple and total negation of an action regarded in its mere occurrence (§2439).
- Missing the negative force of πρίν altogether. The comparative idea in πρίν explains it: an event A happened before another event B, that is, A occurred when B had not yet occurred (§2439). GMT reaches the same result and notes that οὔπω or μήπω with a temporal participle may generally be substituted for πρίν and the infinitive (GMT §621).
- Expecting a future indicative in a clause about the future. The future indicative is rarely used in temporal clauses, and when used refers to definite time (§2398).
- Reading a present indicative in a general sentence as an error for the subjunctive. It is very rarely so used, but Smyth records the case (§2413), and GMT calls the same use rare in temporal sentences and prints two examples (GMT §535).
- Taking ἕως to mean only until. It also means so long as and while, and Smyth gives each value its own rule: the indicative when the action of the clause denotes definite duration in the present or past (§2422), and the indicative of a definite present or past action, with the aorist normally used of past action, in the sense until (§2425).
- Missing the antecedent that governs the English. Conjunctions meaning until may have μέχρι τούτου as an implied or expressed antecedent (§2428), and πρότερον or πρόσθεν may stand in the principal clause as a forerunner of πρίν (§2440). GMT collects the Attic cases of that forerunner (GMT §658) and the Homeric double πρίν, of which 20 of the 43 Iliadic cases with the infinitive have a preceding πρίν or another adverb (GMT §657).
- Applying the Attic πρίν rule to Homer. Homer has the infinitive after affirmative or negative clauses alike, before and until, and often where a finite verb would be used in Attic (§2456); GMT reports the same for the Homeric infinitive (GMT §626), and the Homeric subjunctive without κέ or ἄν (GMT §639).
- Forgetting that assimilation reaches temporal clauses too. A temporal clause takes a past tense of the indicative in dependence on a past tense of the indicative denoting unreality (§2185 b), and takes the optative by assimilation when an optative of the principal clause refers to future time (§2186 b).
