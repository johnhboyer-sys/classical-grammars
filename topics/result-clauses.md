# Result Clauses

Status: drafted 2026-09-07 on the pattern of `topics/genitive-absolute.md`.
Every claim carries a section citation; `scripts/verify_topic_pages.py`
checks them. `topic: result-clauses` names the derived record in
`data/smyth/topics.json`.

## 1. Quick answer

A clause of result denotes a consequence of what is stated in the principal clause (§2249). It is introduced by the relative word ὥστε, rarely by ὡς, and the principal clause often has the demonstrative words οὕτως thus, τοιοῦτος such, τοσοῦτος so great (§2250). There are two main forms, ὥστε with the infinitive and ὥστε with a finite verb; with the infinitive the negative is generally μή, with a finite verb οὐ (§2251). The infinitive denotes an anticipated or possible result, and the actual occurrence of the result is not stated, and is to be inferred only (§2260). ὥστε with the indicative states the actual result of the action of the leading verb, and is especially common in narrative statements with the aorist tense (§2274). ἔχω τριήρεις ὥστε ἑλεῖν τὸ ἐκείνων πλοῖον is "I have triremes (so as) to catch their vessel", while ὥστε εἷλον would mean "so that I caught", with an essentially different meaning (§2259).

## 2. Recognition

- ὥστε (rarely ὡς) after a principal clause in which οὕτως, τοιοῦτος or τοσοῦτος is expressed (§2250). Herodotus often writes οὕτω ὥστε together, οὕτω referring to the whole leading sentence (GMT §593).
- ὥστε with the infinitive after expressions denoting ability, capacity, or to effect something: ὁ ποταμὸς τοσοῦτος βάθος ὡς μηδὲ τὰ δόρατα ὑπερέχειν "the river of such a depth that the spears could not even project above the surface" (§2263).
- ὥστε or ὡς after a comparative with ἤ than: ἐλᾱ́ττω ἔχοντα δύναμιν ἢ ὥστε τοὺς φίλους ὠφελεῖν "too little power to benefit his friends"; after a comparative, ὡς is as common as ὥστε (§2264).
- A ὥστε clause after a principal clause that is negatived, including a question expecting the answer no: τίς οὕτως ἐστὶ δεινὸς λέγειν ὥστε σε πεῖσαι; "who is so eloquent as to persuade you?" (§2265).
- A ὥστε clause after a principal clause that expresses a condition (§2266).
- ὥστε with the infinitive stating a condition or a proviso, on condition that, provided that: ὑπῑσχνοῦντο ὥστε ἐκπλεῖν "they gave their promise on the condition that they should sail out" (§2268).
- ἐφ᾽ ᾧ and ἐφ᾽ ᾧτε on condition that, for the purpose of, with the infinitive or less often the future indicative: αἱρεθέντες ἐφ᾽ ᾧτε συγγράψαι νόμους "having been chosen for the purpose of compiling laws" (§2279).
- ὥστε introducing an independent sentence, where it practically has the force of οὖν, τοίνυν, τοιγαροῦν and so, therefore, consequently (§2274 a).
- ὥστε with an imperative, a hortatory or prohibitory subjunctive, or an interrogative verb, where the clause is coördinate rather than subordinate: ὥστε θάρρει "and so be not afraid" (§2275).
- Result may also be expressed by relative clauses (§2256), which usually take the indicative, and there ὅστις is commoner than ὅς (§2556). οἷος of such sort as to and ὅσος of such amount as to denote result and commonly take the infinitive (§2497).
- ὥστε (ὡς) occurs rarely with the participle, instead of the infinitive, by attraction to a preceding participle (§2276).
- In Homer, consecutive ὥστε (ὡς) with a finite verb does not occur; he uses coördination instead (§2253). In Homer ὥστε, or rather ὥς τε, is found with two exceptions only in the sense of as, as in similes: ὥς τε λέων ἐχάρη (GMT §585). The familiar construction of ὥς τε with the infinitive occurs only twice, Il. 9.42 ἐπέσσυται ὥς τε νέεσθαι "is eager to return" and Od. 17.20 (Monro §235).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Find ὥστε (or consecutive ὡς) and look back for the demonstrative in the leading clause (§2250). Consecutive ὡς occurs almost always with the infinitive, chiefly in Herodotus, Xenophon, Aeschylus and Sophocles, and with a finite verb only occasionally in Herodotus and Xenophon (§2252). Outside those authors ὡς here is very rare, and ὥστε gradually became almost the only word that introduces consecutive sentences (GMT §608).
2. Decide which of the two forms you have: ὥστε with the infinitive, or ὥστε with a finite verb (§2251).
3. If a finite verb follows, read its mood exactly as you would in an independent sentence. Any form used in simple sentences may follow ὥστε, and ὥστε has no effect on the mood of a finite verb (§2273). So ὥστε may take a past tense of the indicative with ἄν, potential and unreal (§2277), and rarely the optative without ἄν by assimilation to a preceding optative, or the potential optative with ἄν (§2278). GMT states the same rule and adds that the ὥστε clause may hold a prohibitory subjunctive, an imperative, or an interrogative (GMT §602).
4. If that finite verb is an imperative, a hortatory or prohibitory subjunctive, or an interrogative, stop treating the clause as subordinate. There ὥστε has the force of καὶ οὕτως: ὥστε πόθεν ἴσᾱσιν; "and so how do they know?" (§2275).
5. If an infinitive follows, do not report the result as a fact. The clause with ὥστε and the infinitive is merely added to the clause containing the main thought in order to explain it, and the consequence is stated without any distinction of time and only with difference of stage of action (§2254). GMT puts the same point as a rule of inference: οὕτως ἐστὶ δεινὸς ὥστε σε πεῖσαι states no more than that he has the skill to persuade you, and that he does persuade is an inference which does not lie in the words (GMT §584).
6. Read the negative, which is a strong presumption and not a proof. μή stands in ordinary result clauses, including such as express an intended result (§2759 a), and μή is sympathetic after verbs of hindering, ἀπεχόμενοι ὥστε μὴ ἐμβάλλειν (§2759 b). οὐ stands when the ὥστε clause depends on a clause itself subordinate to a verb of saying or thinking (§2759 c). μὴ οὐ stands after a negatived verb of hindering, and also when the ὥστε clause depends on a negatived verb (§2759 d). οὐ μή with the subjunctive occurs as well, οὕτως ἐπετεθῡ́μηκα ἀκοῦσαι ὥστε οὐ μή σου ἀπολειφθῶ (§2759 e). With the indicative and the potential optative the negative is οὐ (§2705 h), and GMT states that pair as one rule: the regular negative of the infinitive after ὥστε is μή, that of the indicative and potential optative οὐ (GMT §606). Do not stop there, though, because the next step is a whole class of ὥστε οὐ with the infinitive that the rule does not reach.
7. If you find ὥστε οὐ with an infinitive, test for indirect discourse before calling it an anomaly. A result clause with ὥστε and the indicative, dependent on an infinitive in indirect discourse and itself quoted, takes the infinitive and usually retains the negative of the direct form: ἔφασαν τοὺς στρατιώτᾱς εἰς τοῦτο τρυφῆς ἐλθεῖν ὥστ᾽ οὐκ ἐθέλειν πῑ̔νειν, direct ὥστε οὐκ ἤθελον πῑ̔νειν (§2269). ὥστε μή may still be used in indirect discourse, even with the future infinitive or the infinitive with ἄν (GMT §595). Occasionally ὥστε οὐ with the infinitive represents a finite mood with οὐ of direct discourse when there is no preceding infinitive to assimilate it (GMT §597), and in a few cases none of those explanations will apply (GMT §598), where the likeliest account is a mixture of two equivalent constructions (GMT §599).
8. If the clause looks like purpose, keep the result reading and let the purpose come from the verb. A clause of intended result is often used where ὅπως might occur in an object clause after a verb of effort (§2267 b); the infinitive there expresses only the result, and the idea of purpose comes only from the general sense and especially from the meaning of the leading verb, whereas ἵνα μή would express only purpose (§2267 a). Object clauses after verbs of effort themselves take the future indicative with ὅπως (§2211), and ὅπως marks them off, not the future tense. A future indicative may stand in a consecutive clause: any form used in simple sentences may follow ὥστε, which has no effect on the mood of a finite verb (§2273), and the consecutive ὡς of νομίζω οὕτως ἔχειν ὡς ἀποστήσονται αὐτοῦ αἱ πόλεις has a future indicative (§2274). GMT gives two more with ὥστε itself, ταῦτα πεποίηκα ἀκόντων Ἀθηναίων, ὥστ᾽ ... ἐμοὶ δὲ πιστεύσετε and οὕτως ἐναργές ἐστι, ὥσθ᾽ εὑρήσετε (GMT §601). The future indicative expresses an intended result in a consecutive relative clause too (§2558), and ἐφ᾽ ᾧ and ἐφ᾽ ᾧτε take it less often than the infinitive (§2279).
9. If the leading word is a verb of will or desire, an impersonal expression, or an adjective, consider that ὥστε adds nothing to the sense. ὥστε is often used with the infinitive when the infinitive without ὥστε is regular or more common (§2271), and there is hardly a construction in which the simple infinitive was used where ὥστε is not occasionally prefixed to it (GMT §588).
10. If ἄν stands with the infinitive after ὥστε, read possibility. ἄν with the infinitive here represents either a potential indicative or a potential optative (§2270), and GMT gives ὥστε μὴ ἂν δύνασθαι ἐπανελθεῖν οἴκαδε "so that he would not be able to return" (GMT §592).
11. Check the tense of the infinitive before you translate. The infinitive with ὥστε is usually present or aorist, rarely perfect, and the future is common only in indirect discourse (§2261). GMT adds that the perfect is sometimes used to express completion or decisiveness of the action (GMT §590), and that the future infinitive with ὥστε is common only when it depends on an infinitive in indirect discourse and represents a future indicative of the direct form (GMT §591).
12. If the passage is Homer, do not look for a consecutive clause with a finite verb (§2253); ask instead whether ὥς τε introduces a comparison (GMT §585).
13. When the context does not decide between tendency and fact, leave the English open. There are many examples in which ὥστε with the infinitive and ὥστε with the indicative seem to amount to essentially the same thing, although the processes by which the meaning is expressed are essentially different (GMT §583). Smyth states a preference, not a rule: ὥστε with the infinitive is preferred in clauses containing or implying a negative, and ὥστε with the indicative after εἰς τοῦτο ἥκει and like phrases when affirmative (§2258 a). If both constructions stand in one sentence, translate each in its own way: ὥστε πάροδον μὴ εἶναι παρὰ πύργον, ἀλλὰ δι᾽ αὐτῶν μέσων διῇσαν (§2259), which GMT reads as so as to allow no passage outside, but so that the men passed through the inside (GMT §603).

## 4. Translation guidance

- ὥστε with the infinitive means as to, so as to; but with a subject necessary in English it must often be translated by so that (§2260 a). GMT states that ὥστε means only as, or, including the antecedent, so as, and never so that except in the construction with the finite moods, although so that is often a necessary makeshift in our translation (GMT §588).
- ὥστε with the indicative is so that, of the actual result: ἐπιπῑ́πτει χιὼν ἄπλετος ὥστε ἀπέκρυψε καὶ τὰ ὅπλα καὶ τοὺς ἀνθρώπους "an immense amount of snow fell so that it buried both the arms and the men" (§2274). GMT gives the same value: ὥστε with the indicative means properly so that, and expresses the actual result of the action of the leading verb (GMT §601).
- The English contrast depends on one word. οὕτω σκαιὸς εἶ ὥστ᾽ οὐ δύνασαι is "are you so stupid that you are not able", of a definite fact, while with μὴ δύνασθαι the meaning would be "so stupid as not to be able", expressing a characteristic (§2274). GMT quotes Shilleto's pair for the same distinction: οὕτως ἄφρων ἦν ὥστε οὐκ ἐβούλετο "he was so foolish that he did not wish", the real result; οὕτως ἄφρων ἦν ὥστε μὴ βούλεσθαι "he was so foolish as not to wish", the natural consequence (GMT §584).
- After a comparative, English uses too ... to: οἱ ἀκοντισταὶ βραχύτερα ἠκόντιζον ἢ ὡς ἐξικνεῖσθαι τῶν σφενδονητ ῶν "the javelin throwers hurled their javelins too short a distance to reach the slingers" (§2264). ὥστε may be omitted there: κρείσσον᾽ ἢ φέρειν κακά "evils too great to be endured" (§2264 a), and GMT collects further cases in which ὥστε seems to be omitted even when its antecedent is expressed (GMT §600).
- A positive adjective before the infinitive carries the same too: ψῡχρόν ἐστιν ὥστε λούσασθαι "the water is too cold for bathing" (§1063). So also ἡμεῖς γὰρ ἔτι νέοι ὥστε τοσοῦτον πρᾶγμα διελέσθαι "for we are still too young to decide so important a matter" (§2271).
- ἄν with the infinitive after ὥστε becomes could or would: ὥστε καὶ ἰδιώτην ἂν γνῶναι "that even a common man could understand" (§2270).
- A proviso is on condition that, provided that (§2268), and the same for ἐφ᾽ ᾧ and ἐφ᾽ ᾧτε, which are also for the purpose of: ἔφασαν ἀποδώσειν ἐφ᾽ ᾧ μὴ καίειν τᾱ̀ς οἰκίᾱς "they would surrender the dead on condition that he would not burn their houses" (§2279). Goodell gives the same value for the construction (Goodell §567), and GMT notes that Herodotus and Thucydides sometimes have ἐφ᾽ ᾧ or ἐφ᾽ ᾧτε with the future indicative (GMT §610).
- Sentence-initial ὥστε is consequently or and so: καὶ εἰς μὲν τὴν ὑστεραίᾱν οὐχ ἧκεν· ὥσθ᾽ οἱ Ἕλληνες ἐφρόντιζον "and on the next day he did not come; consequently the Greeks were anxious" (§2274 a). Goodell renders such a clause the same way and adds that it is often independent: ὥστε βασιλεὺς τὴν μὲν πρὸς ἑαυτὸν ἐπιβουλὴν οὐκ ᾐσθάνετο "so that the king did not perceive the plot against himself" (Goodell §639).
- Where the result is intended, English may say so as to and let the leading verb carry the aim: πᾶν ποιοῦσιν ὥστε δίκην μὴ διδόναι "they use every effort (so as) to avoid being punished" (§2267). Goodell states that ὥστε denotes result, especially an intended result, and the idea of intention gradually fades out (Goodell §566).
- Consecutive relative clauses take the English of a ὥστε clause: τίς οὕτω μαίνεται ὅστις οὐ βούλεται σοὶ φίλος εἶναι; "who is so mad that he does not wish to be a friend to you?" (§2556). GMT notes that this occurs chiefly after negative clauses, or interrogatives implying a negative (GMT §575), and Goodell adds that ὅστις with an indicative often suggests cause, purpose, or result by treating a definite antecedent as indefinite in order to characterise it (Goodell §619).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reporting the result as accomplished when the Greek has the infinitive. ὥστε with the infinitive does not state a particular fact (§2258 a); the actual occurrence is to be inferred only (§2260).
- Turning ὥστε μή into a pure purpose clause. The infinitive expresses only the result, and ἵνα μή would express only purpose (§2267 a).
- Calling ὥστε οὐ with an infinitive an error. It is the regular form when the ὥστε clause is quoted inside indirect discourse and keeps the negative of the direct form (§2269), and GMT collects further cases where no assimilating infinitive and no leading clause with ὅτι or ὡς is present (GMT §598).
- Reading a sentence-initial ὥστε as subordinating. With an imperative, a hortatory or prohibitory subjunctive, or an interrogative verb, the clause is coördinate and ὥστε has the force of καὶ οὕτως (§2275).
- Reading a result where ὥστε is merely pleonastic after a verb of will or desire: ἔπεισαν τοὺς Ἀθηναίους ὥστε ἐξαγαγεῖν ἐκ Πύλου Μεσσηνίους "they prevailed upon the Athenians (so as) to withdraw the Messenians from Pylus" (§2271). GMT judges that in many such cases ὥστε added nothing to the sense and the expressions were stereotyped in usage (GMT §588).
- Taking consecutive ὡς for the ὡς of indirect discourse or of purpose. Consecutive ὡς occurs almost always with the infinitive, and with a finite verb only occasionally in Herodotus and Xenophon (§2252).
- Getting the negative wrong in a relative clause of result. The negative is οὐ when the relative clause approximates ὥστε with the indicative, and μή when it expresses an intended or anticipated result (§2556); the future indicative there often expresses an intended result, with μή (§2558). The indicative is normal after οὐκ ἔστιν ὅστις, οὐδείς ἐστιν ὅστις, οὐκ ἔστιν ὅπως (§2557).
- Treating a participle after ὥστε as a slip. ὥστε (ὡς) occurs rarely with the participle by attraction to a preceding participle (§2276), and GMT explains it as assimilation of the ὥστε clause to a participle in indirect discourse (GMT §607).
- Reading a plain infinitive of result as purpose. The infinitive may be used with ὥστε, sometimes with ὡς, to denote a result, often an intended result (§2011); the bare infinitive of purpose is a separate construction (§2008).
- Missing that οἷος and ὅσος take the infinitive by themselves, like the fuller τοιοῦτος οἷος and τοσοῦτος ὅσος (§2003).
- Expecting Homer to build result clauses like Attic. Consecutive ὥστε with a finite verb does not occur in Homer (§2253), and the Attic poets are the first to use ὥστε freely with the infinitive, Sophocles being the first with the finite moods (GMT §585).
- Forgetting that a ὥστε clause with the infinitive is merely added to the clause containing the main thought in order to explain it (§2254), while one with a finite verb contains the main thought itself and is often so loosely connected with the leading verb as to be practically independent and coördinate (§2255).
