## 1. Quick answer

The participle (μετοχή participation) is a verbal adjective, in part a verb, in part an adjective (§2039). Goodwin compares it to the infinitive: as the infinitive is a verbal noun, so the participle is a verbal adjective, both retaining all the attributes of a verb which are consistent with their nature (GMT §821). It has three main uses. Attributive, as an attributive to a substantive; circumstantial or adverbial, denoting some attendant circumstance and qualifying the main verb like an adverbial phrase or clause; and supplementary, as a supplement to a verbal predicate which without such a supplement would be incomplete (§2046). GMT counts the same three and adds the warning that the distinction between the second and third is less clearly marked than that between the first and the two others (GMT §822, GMT §823). The circumstantial and supplementary participles are predicate participles (§2047). The test is to remove the participle: the attributive and circumstantial participles are commonly not necessary to the construction, but the removal of a supplementary participle may make the construction incomplete (§2048).

## 2. Recognition

- As a verb. The participle shows the distinctions of voice and tense, can have an object in the same case as the finite forms, is modified by adverbs and not by adjectives, and may take ἄν (§2040).
- As an adjective. It is inflected, it admits the article before it, and it follows the rules of agreement like other adjectives (§2041). Goodell states the verb and adjective uses together: as a verb the participle has voice and tense and may take an object or an adverbial modifier, and as an adjective it is declined and agrees with a noun or pronoun, attributively or as a predicate (Goodell §580).
- It is always used in connection with a substantive or a substantive pronoun, which may be contained in a verbal form, as διάγουσι μανθάνοντες "they spend their time in learning" (§2042).
- Attributive: the article stands before it, or it modifies a substantive like any other adjective, as ὁ παρὼν καιρός "the present crisis" and οἱ ὄντες ἐχθροί "the existing enemies" (§2049).
- Circumstantial: no article at all. In agreement with a noun and its article it stands before the article or after the noun, that is, in the predicate position (§2054).
- Absolute: the participle agrees with a genitive noun or pronoun which is not in the main construction of the sentence (§2070), or stands in the accusative when it is impersonal (§2076).
- Supplementary: removing it may make the construction incomplete (§2048).
- Goodell sorts the same material into attributive (Goodell §582), circumstantial (Goodell §583) and predicate participles (Goodell §584).

## 3. The attributive participle

The attributive participle, with any modifier, with or without the article, modifies a substantive like any other adjective: ὁ ἐφεστηκὼς κίνδῡνος τῇ πόλει "the danger impending over the State", τὸ Κοτύλαιον ὀνομαζόμενον ὄρος "the mountain called Cotylaeum" (§2049).

- If the noun is omitted, the participle becomes a substantive. The substantive with which the attributive participle (with the article) agrees directly may be omitted, the participle thus becoming a substantive, as ὁ οἴκαδε βουλόμενος ἀπιέναι "whoever wants to go home"; neuter participles are often substantival, as τὰ δέοντα "duties" (§2050).
- English usually needs a noun or a relative clause for these: ὁ φεύγων "the exile" or "the defendant", τὸ μέλλον "the future", οἱ θανόντες "the dead", οἱ ἀδικούμενοι "those who are (being) wronged" (§2050 a). Goodell gives the same rule, that a short phrase may be rendered by an English noun and a longer one by a relative clause: οἱ λέγοντες "the speakers", ἡ τεκοῦσα "the mother" (Goodell §582).
- When the verbal nature has ceased to be felt the participle takes adjectives or a genitive, as τὰ μῑκρὰ συμφέροντα τῆς πόλεως "the petty interests of the State"; in poetry many are simple substantives, as ὁ τεκών "father", ἡ τεκοῦσα "mother" (§2051).
- The article is either generic or particular: ὁ λέγων is the definite speaker on a particular occasion, or the orator in general (§2052). Under the generic article Smyth lists ὁ βουλόμενος "any one who wishes" and ὁ τυχών "any chance comer" (§1124), and Goodell lists the same two phrases as common (Goodell §582).
- A participle and its substantive often correspond to a verbal noun with the genitive or to an articular infinitive: μετὰ Συρᾱκούσᾱς οἰκισθείσᾱς "after the foundation of Syracuse", ἐλῡ́πει αὐτὸν ἡ χώρᾱ πορθουμένη "the ravaging of the country grieved him" (§2053). Except in expressions of time, this construction is in place only when the participle is necessary to the sense (§2053 a).
- GMT adds the substantival use without the article, and the neuter singular of the present participle with the article used as an abstract noun (GMT §827, GMT §829).

## 4. The circumstantial participle

The circumstantial participle is added, without the article, to a noun or pronoun to set forth some circumstance under which an action, generally the main action, takes place; it qualifies the principal verb like an adverbial clause, so that μετὰ ταῦτα εἶπε "afterwards he said" answers to γελῶν εἶπε "he said laughingly" (§2054). It has two main constructions, each equivalent in meaning to a clause of time, condition, cause and the rest (§2055).

The relations it can carry, in Smyth's order:

- Time. The time denoted by the participle is only relative to that of the governing verb and is to be inferred from the context; several temporal participles have an adverbial force, as ἀρχόμενος "in the beginning", τελευτῶν "at last" (§2061).
- Manner. παρήλαυνον τεταγμένοι "they marched past in order" (§2062). Several participles of manner have an idiomatic sense: ἀνύσᾱς "quickly", λαθών "secretly", κλαίων "to one's sorrow", χαίρων "with impunity" (§2062 a).
- Means, often the present participle: μὴ κρῖν᾽ ὁρῶν τὸ κάλλος, ἀλλὰ τὸν τρόπον "judge by regarding not beauty, but character" (§2063).
- Cause. ἀπείχοντο κερδῶν αἰσχρὰ νομίζοντες εἶναι "they held aloof from gains because they thought them disgraceful" (§2064). Here belong τί μαθών and τί παθών, used with the general sense of wherefore in direct questions expressing surprise or disapprobation, and with ὅ τι in indirect questions (§2064 a).
- Purpose or object, expressed by the future and sometimes the present participle, especially after verbs denoting to come, go, send and summon: ὁ βάρβαρος ἐπὶ τὴν Ἑλλάδα δουλωσόμενος ἦλθεν "the barbarians proceeded against Greece with the purpose of enslaving it" (§2065).
- Opposition or concession. πολλοὶ γὰρ ὄντες εὐγενεῖς εἰσιν κακοί "for many, albeit noble by birth, are ignoble" (§2066).
- Condition, where the negative is always μή: σὺ δὲ κλύων εἴσει τάχα "but if you listen you shall soon know" (§2067). GMT lists the same use as the participle standing for a protasis, its tenses representing the various forms of condition (GMT §841).
- Any attendant circumstance. συλλέξᾱς στράτευμα ἐπολιόρκει Μῑ́λητον "having collected an army he laid siege to Miletus" (§2068). ἔχων, ἄγων, φέρων, χρώμενος and λαβών are used where English employs with: ἔχων στρατιᾱ̀ν ἀφικνεῖται "he arrives with an army", βοῇ χρώμενοι "with a shout" (§2068 a). Goodell prints the same five, ἔχων, ἄγων, λαβών, φέρων, χρώμενος, and says they may often be translated with (Goodell §583).

The list does not assign each participle to one class. The force of these participles does not lie in the participle itself but is derived from the context, and unless there is a modifying adverb the context often does not decide whether the participle is temporal, causal, conditional or concessive; πατὴρ δ᾽ ἀπειλῶν οὐκ ἔχει μέγαν φόβον may mean that a father when, or because, or if, or though he threatens does not excite much fear (§2069). GMT says the same, that no exact distinctions of all circumstantial participles are possible, as many express various relations at the same time (GMT §846).

## 5. The absolutes

- Genitive absolute. A circumstantial participle agreeing with a genitive noun or pronoun which is not in the main construction of the sentence stands in the genitive absolute, and like other circumstantial participles it expresses time, cause, condition, concession or any attendant circumstance: ταῦτ᾽ ἐπρᾱ́χθη Κόνωνος στρατηγοῦντος "these things were effected while Conon was in command" (§2070). The subject of the participle is not identical with the subject of the leading verb (§2058).
- Accusative absolute. When the participle has no definite subject, that is, with impersonal verbs, the accusative absolute is used instead of the genitive: συνδόξαν τῷ πατρὶ καὶ τῇ μητρὶ γαμεῖ τὴν Κυαξάρου θυγατέρα "on the approval of his father and mother he married the daughter of Cyaxares" (§2059). Smyth's list includes δέον, ἐξόν, μετόν, παρόν, προσῆκον and the like, with the passive participles used impersonally and adjectives with ὄν as well (§2076). GMT lists the same impersonals and adds that the participle stands in the neuter singular where other participles with their subjects would stand in the genitive absolute (GMT §851).
- The participle of a personal verb may be used absolutely if it is preceded by ὡς or ὥσπερ, and cases without those particles are rare (§2078).

The genitive absolute has its own page. See that page for the cases where the rule is broken, for the omitted subject, and for the comparison with the Latin ablative absolute (§2072, §2073, §2074).

## 6. The tense of the participle, and what it says about time

This is where readers of the participle most often go wrong, and the grammars do not put it the same way twice.

- Smyth's rule. The tenses of the participle except the future, when the participle is not in indirect discourse, are timeless, and denote only stage of action; whether the action is antecedent, coincident or subsequent to that of the leading verb depends on the context (§2043). The present participle is generally coincident, the aorist generally antecedent but sometimes coincident when it defines or is identical with the action of the leading verb, the perfect completion with permanent result: δειπνήσᾱς ἐχώρει "after supper he advanced" against εὖ γ᾽ ἐποίησας ἀναμνήσᾱς με "you did well in reminding me" (§1872).
- In indirect discourse the tense does mark time. There the tenses denote the same time relative to that of the leading verb as the corresponding tenses of the indicative in direct discourse: τὸν Μῆδον ἴσμεν ἐπὶ τὴν Πελοπόννησον ἐλθόντα "we know that the Mede came against the Peloponnese" (§1874). Gildersleeve draws the same distinction and notes that in this use the participle takes all the tenses, the future as well as the present, the perfect and the aorist, and represents the indicative only after verbs of intellectual perception (Gildersleeve §354).
- The future participle is the exception in either construction. It marks an action as in prospect at the time denoted by the leading verb, and since it expresses an idea of will it shows that an action is purposed, intended or expected: ὁ ἡγησόμενος οὐδεὶς ἔσται "there will be no one to guide us" (§2044).
- Gildersleeve differs in emphasis. The participle as a verbal adjective is chiefly used in the present, aorist and perfect, the temporal relation is that of the kind of time, and the sphere of time depends on the context (Gildersleeve §329). He then does what Smyth does not, and tabulates the participle against each tense of the leading verb: the present participle is generally used of contemporaneous action (Gildersleeve §330), its action is sometimes prior, where an adverb of time often makes the relation plain (Gildersleeve §337), and the action of the aorist participle is ordinarily prior but may be coincident, especially when the leading verb is aorist or future (Gildersleeve §339).
- Goodell disagrees with Smyth on the circumstantial participle. He states the timeless rule first, that the present, aorist and perfect tenses of the participle denote the kind of action rather than time, and adds that when a participle represents an indicative clause the tenses retain the tense meaning of the indicative (Goodell §581). But under the circumstantial participle he writes that it represents an indicative without ἄν, and that the tense therefore generally denotes time (Goodell §583). Smyth's §2043 applies the timeless rule to the same cases and says that time depends on the context. Where the two differ, Smyth's statement is the more cautious and Goodell's the more usable; the participle itself does not decide.
- Homer is not Attic here. Monro records that the aorist participle almost always represents an action as past at the time given by the verb, unlike the aorist infinitive, which generally conveys no notion of time, though he adds that this is not because the participle itself conveys any notion of past time (Monro §244). The aorist participle may still express a fact coincident in time with the verb, especially when the time of the fact is the important point (Monro §245).

## 7. The negatives

- The usual negative is οὐ. The negative of the participle is οὐ, except when the participle has a general or conditional force, or occurs in a sentence which requires μή (§2045).
- The rule stated from the other side: the participle has οὐ when it states a fact, μή when it states a condition, so that οὐ πιστεύων is "since he does not believe" and μὴ πιστεύων is "if he does not believe" (§2728).
- The negative therefore tells you which participle you have. ὁ οὐ δρᾱ́σᾱς is the definite person who did not do something, ὁ μὴ δρᾱ́σᾱς is any one who did not do something, a supposed case (§2052). Goodell states the same test and applies it to a pair: τοῖς μὴ πειθομένοις is any who did not take his advice, while τοῖς οὐ πειθομένοις would have meant a definite class who in fact did not (Goodell §582).
- Conditional participles regularly take μή (§2067), and μή with the articular participle is the abridged equivalent of a conditional relative sentence (§2728 a).
- In a negative sentence οὐδέ or μηδέ takes the place of καί with a concessive participle (§2083 b), and GMT records the same substitution (GMT §859).

## 8. What is built on the participle

- The article with the participle, which turns any participle into a noun (§2050) and can denote an entire class (§1124).
- Periphrastic tenses. The present and perfect participle are freely used with the forms of εἰμί to form a periphrasis, especially when the participle has an adjectival character (§1961); the periphrasis with ἔχω and the aorist participle is analogous to the perfect and emphasizes the permanence of the result attained (§1963). GMT lists the same forms under the attributive participle, noting that in the future perfect active the periphrastic form is generally the only one in use (GMT §831).
- The participle with ἄν. The participle with ἄν represents either a past tense of the indicative with ἄν or the optative with ἄν, and the context determines which; the participle with ἄν is post-Homeric (§1845). The present participle with ἄν stands for the imperfect indicative with ἄν or the present optative with ἄν, as πόλλ᾽ ἂν ἔχων ἕτερ᾽ εἰπεῖν, παραλείπω "though I might be able to say much else I pass it by" (§1846); the aorist participle with ἄν stands for the aorist indicative or the aorist optative with ἄν (§1848). Goodell states the rule in the same words, that a participle with ἄν represents an optative or indicative with ἄν, the context alone determining which (Goodell §595).
- The adverbs that make the relation explicit. Adverbs are often used to set forth the relations of time, manner, cause and concession implied in the participle, and they occur with the genitive and accusative absolute too (§2079). With participles of opposition or concession, καίπερ, and καὶ ταῦτα "and that too": συμβουλεύω σοι καίπερ νεώτερος ὤν "I give you advice though I am your junior" (§2083). With participles of cause, ἅτε and οἷα or οἷον, which state the cause as a fact on the authority of the speaker or writer: ἅτε παῖς ὤν "inasmuch as he was a child" (§2085). GMT gives ἅτε and οἷα or οἷον the same causal rule, that the cause assigned is stated merely on the authority of the speaker or writer (GMT §862), and puts καίπερ and καὶ ταῦτα with the participle of opposition or concession (GMT §859); Goodell records ἅτε as causal and καίπερ as concessive (Goodell §593).
- ὡς with the participle, which is the one that changes the meaning. It sets forth the ground of belief on which the agent acts and denotes the thought, assertion or presumed intention in the mind of the subject of the principal verb, so that ἀπῆλθον ὡς νῑκήσαντες may mean either that they departed under the impression that they had been victorious or that they departed pretending it, while ἀπῆλθον νῑκήσαντες means that as a matter of fact they had been victorious (§2086).
- ὥσπερ, an adverb of comparison, which denotes that the action of the main verb is compared with an assumed case: κατακείμεθ᾽ ὥσπερ ἐξὸν ἡσυχίᾱν ἄγειν "we lie inactive just as if it were possible to take one's ease" (§2087). Goodell calls ὥσπερ a strengthened ὡς and prints the same sentence (Goodell §593).
- The omission of ὤν, which leaves a predicate adjective or noun standing by itself, chiefly after ἅτε, οἷα, ὡς or καίπερ (GMT §875). ἑκών and ᾱ̓́κων are properly participles and are treated as such (§2071).

## 9. Traps

- Reading the tense of the participle as time when the participle is not in indirect discourse. There the tenses except the future are timeless and denote only stage of action (§2043).
- Reading it as stage when the participle is in indirect discourse. There each tense denotes the same time relative to the leading verb as the corresponding tense of the indicative did in direct discourse (§1874).
- Assigning a circumstantial participle to one relation and translating only that one. It is often impossible to assign a participle exclusively to any one of these relations, which are purely logical (§2060), and the context often does not decide (§2069).
- Taking the presence of the article as having no force. It is the mark of the attributive participle (§2049), and the circumstantial participle has no article at all (§2054).
- Taking a participle in the predicate position for an attributive one. In agreement with a noun and its article the circumstantial participle stands before the article or after the noun (§2054).
- Assuming that an attributive participle cannot also be circumstantial. οἱ μὴ δυνάμενοι διατελέσαι τὴν ὁδὸν ἐνυκτέρευσαν ἄσῑτοι means those who, that is if any, were unable to complete the march passed the night without food (§2048). GMT makes the same point with ὁ μὴ δαρεὶς ἄνθρωπος, the unflogged man, which involves a condition (GMT §823).
- Assuming a participle cannot be both circumstantial and supplementary. ἀδικούμενοι ὀργίζονται is "they are enraged at being wronged" or "because, when, if, they are wronged" (§2048).
- Reading ὡς with a participle as a comparison. It sets forth the ground of belief on which the agent acts (§2086); ὥσπερ is the adverb of comparison (§2087).
- Concluding from ὡς that the writer disowns what the participle says. Smyth's own rule adds that the use of ὡς implies nothing as to the opinion of the speaker or writer (§2086 a), and Goodwin puts it as a correction: it is a mistake to suppose that ὡς implies that the participle does not express the idea of the speaker or writer, and the point is determined, if at all, by the context (GMT §865).
- Missing that ἅτε and ὡς are not interchangeable. ἅτε states the cause as a fact on the authority of the speaker or writer (§2085), and ὡς states it as the thought of the subject (§2086). GMT keeps them apart in the same way, ἅτε and οἷα stating the cause merely on the authority of the speaker or writer (GMT §862).
- Looking for a verb where ὤν has been left out. A predicate adjective or noun may stand by itself, chiefly after ἅτε, οἷα, ὡς or καίπερ (GMT §875).
- Taking a future participle for a plain future. It expresses an idea of will and shows that an action is purposed, intended or expected, and its nearest approach to mere futurity appears in general only after verbs of knowing and perceiving (§2044).
- Reading a genitive absolute when the noun does belong to the main construction. The genitive absolute is regularly used only when a new subject is introduced into the sentence, though the principle is sometimes violated to make the participial clause more prominent (GMT §850).
- Forgetting that the negative is evidence. οὐ states a fact and μή states a condition (§2728).
