# The Indicative

## 1. Quick answer

The indicative mood makes a simple, direct assertion of fact, or asks a question anticipating such an assertion: ἦλθε, he came, οὐκ ἦλθε, he did not come, ἐλεύσεται, he will come (§1770). Gildersleeve says that the indicative represents the predicate as a reality, and that the use of the Greek indicative is in the main the same as that of the English (Gildersleeve §361, Gildersleeve §362). Goodell says that the indicative presents the action or state as real, in assertions, denials, exclamations and questions, and sends the reader on from there to his own unreal uses, the imperfect with ἄν of something that would have been in the past, or would be now, in an imaginary case, known to be unreal, and the aorist with ἄν of something that would have occurred in an imaginary case, known to be unreal (Goodell §452, Goodell §461, Goodell §467). That is most of what a reader needs, and the rest of this page is about the exceptions. A past tense of the indicative with ἄν denotes past potentiality, probability (cautious statement) or necessity, usually in the aorist and less commonly in the imperfect (§1784), or unreality (§1786), or repeated or customary past action, which is post-Homeric (§1790). A past tense with εἴθε or εἰ γάρ states a wish known to be unattainable (§1780). And a small fixed set of imperfects, ἔδει, χρῆν and the like, has a modal force of its own without any particle at all (§1774). Monro gathers a different set under one name: the conditional protasis with εἰ, the apodosis of imaginary consequence, and final and object clauses (Monro §323). All such uses, in which the indicative does not assert, may be called modal uses, and the tendency of language appears to be to extend the modal uses of the indicative and consequently to diminish the range of the other moods (Monro §323).

## 2. Recognition

- The plain case, which needs no analysis: a statement, a denial, or a question that anticipates one, as πότε ταῦτα ποιήσει; when will he do this? (§1770).
- An assumption in the form of a statement. The indicative states particular or general suppositions and makes assertions which may or may not be absolutely true, often after καὶ δή, as καὶ δὴ τεθνᾶσι, and suppose they are dead (§1771).
- μή or μὴ οὐ in front of a present or past indicative with no verb of fearing governing it, which is a doubtful assertion: ἀλλὰ μὴ τοῦτο οὐ καλῶς ὡμολογήσαμεν, but perhaps we did not do well in agreeing to this (§1772). Goodwin sets it beside the verb of fearing: as φοβοῦμαι μὴ πάσχει means I fear that he is suffering, so μὴ πάσχει may mean I suspect he is suffering (GMT §269). Goodell reads the same μή as an expression of desire on the part of the speaker to negative the statement, a desire that may be merely assumed, or may be nothing more than surprise that the statement should be true (Goodell §488).
- ἄν beside an imperfect, aorist or pluperfect. That is the mark of the three modal uses: the past potential, which denotes past potentiality, probability or necessity (§1784); the unreal, where the indicative of the historical tenses with ἄν denotes unreality (§1786); and the iterative, where the imperfect and aorist with ἄν express repeated or customary past action (§1790). Gildersleeve's three headings are the same, unreal, potential of the past, and intermittent action (Gildersleeve §429, Gildersleeve §430, Gildersleeve §431).
- τὶς or an indefinite second person beside a past tense with ἄν, which points to the past potential: ἔγνω ἄν τις, one might have known, and ἐπέγνως ἄν, you would have observed (§1784). Gildersleeve prints ἔγνω τις ἄν and ὀλίγους ἂν εἶδες for the same use (Gildersleeve §430).
- A protasis with the optative in front of an ἄν clause, which points to the iterative use: Εἴ τινες ἴδοιέν πῃ τοὺς σφετέρους ἐπικρατοῦντας, ἀνεθάρσησαν ἄν, whenever any saw their friends in any way victorious, they would be encouraged (GMT §162). Smyth's own examples are main clauses with no protasis: διηρώτων ἄν, I used to ask, and ἂν ἔλεξεν, he was wont to say (§1790). Gildersleeve's example of the same use is διαλεχθεὶς ἄν μοι . . . ᾤχετο ἀπιών, he would have a talk with me and then he was off (Gildersleeve §431).
- Herodotus's iterative forms with ἄν, which are the same use with the tense marked twice: κλαίεσκε ἄν, she kept weeping (§1792).
- εἴθε or εἰ γάρ in front of a past tense, which is an unattainable wish: εἴθε σοι τότε συνεγενόμην, would that I had then been with thee (§1780).
- ὤφελον with an infinitive, which expresses the same wish: ὤφελε Κῦρος ζῆν, would that Cyrus were now alive (§1781). Gildersleeve prints ἀλλ᾽ ὤφελε . . . Κῦρος ζῆν and glosses the literal sense, Cyrus ought to be alive (Gildersleeve §367).
- An imperfect of obligation or possibility with an infinitive and no ἄν: ἔδει, χρῆν or ἐχρῆν, προσῆκε, καιρὸς ἦν, ἄξιον ἦν, εἰκὸς ἦν, δίκαιον ἦν, αἰσχρὸν ἦν, ἐξῆν, καλῶς εἶχεν, and the verbals in -τόν or -τέον with ἦν (§1774). Goodell's list is the same and his heading names what it does, that these imperfects state an obligation or propriety in past time while the context often implies that the obligation was not met (Goodell §460).

## 3. The uses, and how to tell them apart

1. If there is no ἄν and no wishing particle and no verb of obligation, the indicative asserts a fact. Two shapes meet all three of those negative conditions: μή or μὴ οὐ in front of a present or past indicative, which may express a doubtful assertion (§1772), and the indicative used alone where in English we employ an auxiliary verb (§1773). Gildersleeve's caution explains most false alarms: the English indicative and the English subjunctive coincide to a considerable extent in form, whence an apparent coincidence in syntax where there is real diversity (Gildersleeve §362).
2. If there is an ἄν, first rule out the two tenses that never take it. ἄν is not used with the present and perfect indicative (§1794). Gildersleeve says the same: beginners sometimes make a mistake here owing to the great variation in the position of ἄν (Gildersleeve §433). So an ἄν near a present or a perfect belongs to some other verb or is not ἄν.
3. If the tense is a future, expect the construction to be Homeric and rare. Homer and the early poets use ἄν or κέν with the future indicative with a conditional or limiting force, as καί κέ τις ὧδ᾽ ἐρέει, and in such a case some one will say thus; in Attic ἄν is found with the future in a few passages which are now generally emended (§1793). Monro reports the Homeric distribution, that Homer not infrequently uses κεν with the future while the future with ἄν is very rare (Monro §326). Gildersleeve reports it from the Attic side: the future indicative with ἄν, theoretically a legitimate construction, has been kept out of use by the optative with ἄν and by ἄν with the subjunctive, and was counted a solecism even in antiquity (Gildersleeve §432). But he adds that a number of instances occur in MSS and editions, most of them corrected or easily corrigible, and of one famous passage, Plato, Rep. 615D, that if ever ἄν was needed with the future indicative, it is needed here (Gildersleeve §432).
4. With a past tense and ἄν, ask next whether the action happened. If it did, the use is iterative; if it did not, the use is unreal; if the question does not arise, it is the past potential. Smyth's account of the iterative makes the test explicit: the construction denoted originally what could or would take place under certain past circumstances, but in actual use, since the action of the verb did take place, it has become a statement of fact (§1791).
5. The unreal is the commonest of the three and the easiest to name. The indicative of the historical tenses with ἄν or κέν may denote unreality: τότε δ᾽ αὐτὸ τὸ πρᾶγμ᾽ ἂν ἐκρῑ́νετο ἐφ᾽ αὑτοῦ, but the case would then have been decided on its own merits (§1786). Goodell's is the same sentence, and he calls the construction the hypothetical indicative, noting that the three uses of the imperfect with ἄν are closely related, that the hypothetical one is by far the most frequent, and that the second and third cannot always be distinguished (Goodell §461).
6. Read the tense of an unreal for its own time reference, which is not the ordinary one. The imperfect refers to the present or the past, the aorist to the past and rarely to the present, the pluperfect to the present and less commonly to the past (§1788). Gildersleeve sorts the same three by what each opposes: the imperfect opposes a continued action in present or past, the aorist opposes attainment chiefly in the past, and the pluperfect opposes completion, more frequently in the present (Gildersleeve §429).
7. If no condition is present at all, read the past potential, and do not supply a protasis. The past tenses of the indicative with ἄν, usually the aorist and less commonly the imperfect, denote past potentiality, probability or necessity, as ὃ οὐκ ἂν ᾤοντο, which they could not have expected (§1784); a protasis may often be extracted from a participle or intimated in some other word, but there is no reference to any definite condition and a definite ellipsis is not to be supplied (§1785). Goodwin derives all these uses from this one: as the potential optative represents a future act as dependent on future circumstances, so the potential indicative originally represents a past act as dependent on past circumstances, so that while ἦλθεν means he went, ἦλθεν ἄν means he would have gone under some past circumstances (GMT §243). His minimal pair for the two moods is: οὐδὲν ἂν κακὸν ποιήσειαν is they could do no harm if they should try, and οὐδὲν ἂν κακὸν ἐποίησαν is they could have done no harm if they had tried (GMT §245).
8. If the tense is an imperfect with ἄν and you cannot decide between present and past reference, use Goodwin's rule about conditions. When no definite condition is understood, the imperfect with ἄν regularly refers to past time, according to the older usage, like the aorist; the reference to present time, which is common in apodosis after Homer, appears in these potential expressions chiefly in a few simple phrases, especially ἐβουλόμην ἄν (GMT §246). Monro dates the same split: in later Greek the imperfect with ἄν may express either a continuous action which would have occurred at some past time, or an action which would be occurring at the moment of speaking, and the latter of these uses is not Homeric (Monro §324).
9. Do not treat the unreal indicative as an original meaning of the past tenses. Smyth says it is not inherent in the meaning of the past tenses of that mood but has been developed from the past potential, with which the unreal indicative is closely connected (§1787). Monro reconstructs the development: this way of speaking of a conditional event ordinarily implies that the condition on which it depended was not fulfilled, since if the assertion ἦλθεν is true we can hardly ever have occasion to limit it by saying ἦλθεν ἄν, and hence a past tense with κεν or ἄν naturally came to be used where the event had not happened (Monro §324). `topics/conditional-sentences.md` handles the apodosis of the unreal condition itself.
10. For the iterative use, Monro's derivation covers it too, and Homer does not have it. The rule about non-fulfilment does not apply to events that occur repeatedly or on no particular occasion, since there is no contradiction in saying of such an event that it happened when a condition was fulfilled; hence the iterative sense, which is not Homeric (Monro §324). Goodwin gives the same derivation and reports the received opinion against it: the iterative use is generally thought to have no connection with the potential indicative with ἄν, but ἦλθεν ἄν, meaning originally he would have gone under some past circumstances, might easily come to have a frequentative sense, and the construction is not Homeric but is found in Herodotus and common in Attic (GMT §249, GMT §162). Smyth derives it the same way and reports no dispute, saying the construction is connected with the past potential and denoted originally what could or would take place under certain past circumstances (§1791).
11. If a past indicative carries a wishing particle, it is an unattainable wish, and the tense fixes the time. A wish referring to the present or past which cannot be realized is expressed by a past tense of the indicative with εἴθε or with εἰ γάρ, negative μή, the imperfect referring to present time and the aorist to past time (§1780). Gildersleeve says the same and states the force of each tense: the imperfect after εἴθε or εἰ γάρ denotes a wish for continuance, regularly in opposition to the present, and the aorist a wish for attainment, regularly in opposition to the past (Gildersleeve §367). Goodell's version pairs the two tenses with the two facts wished away, the fact being generally present with the imperfect and generally past with the aorist (Goodell §470).
12. Do not expect the wish without its particle. Goodwin gives the reason: the indicative cannot be used in wishes without εἴθε or εἰ γάρ, as it would occasion ambiguity, which cannot arise with the optative, since the optative is not regularly used in independent sentences without ἄν except in wishes (GMT §733). He adds that this form of wish is common in the Attic writers but is unknown to Homer, and that the imperfect and aorist indicative are distinguished here as in the unreal condition (GMT §732).
13. ὤφελον with the infinitive says the same thing, and its infinitive carries the tense the indicative would have carried. An unattainable wish may be expressed by ὤφελον with the present or aorist infinitive, negative μή, and εἴθε or εἰ γάρ may stand before it: μήποτ᾽ ὤφελον λιπεῖν τὴν Σκῦρον, would that I had never left Scyros (§1781). Gildersleeve states the correspondence as a rule: when ὤφελον is used, the tenses of the infinitive are employed in the same way, the present infinitive like the imperfect indicative and the aorist infinitive like the aorist indicative (Gildersleeve §367). Goodwin gives ὤφελον its literal value, the aorist of ὀφείλω, owe, and notes that in Homer the imperfect ὤφελλον is sometimes used the same way (GMT §734). Goodell records the same pairing of infinitive tense with indicative tense (Goodell §470).
14. ἐβουλόμην is a third way of saying it, with and without ἄν. ἐβουλόμην followed by an infinitive may express an unattainable wish, as ἐβουλόμην μὲν οὐκ ἐρίζειν ἐνθάδε, I would that I were not contending here (§1782), and ἐβουλόμην ἄν, which Latin renders vellem, may do the same: ἐβουλόμην ἂν Σίμωνα τὴν αὐτὴν γνώμην ἐμοὶ ἔχειν (§1789). Goodell notes the difference in English: Greek puts the suggestion of unreality with the leading verb, English puts it with the subordinate clause (Goodell §461). Do not confuse it with βουλοίμην ἄν, which is the optative and a different construction; `topics/optative-mood.md` has that one.
15. With ἔδει and its class, ask whether the infinitive happened, not whether the obligation did. With the imperfect indicative of impersonal expressions denoting obligation, propriety, necessity or possibility, the action of a dependent infinitive is usually not realized (§1774): ἔδει σε ταῦτα ποιεῖν is you ought to be doing this but are not, and ἔδει σε ταῦτα ποιῆσαι is you ought to have done this but did not (§1775, §1776). Goodwin calls the whole construction a peculiar form of potential indicative without ἄν and gives the same reading, that ἔδει τοῦτον ἀποθανεῖν means he ought to have perished but did not (GMT §415). Goodell prints the same imperfects and the same reading, that they state an obligation or propriety in past time while the context often implies that the obligation was not met (Goodell §460).
16. Take the tense of the infinitive as the tense of the whole. Present or past time is denoted when the present infinitive is used, and when the reference is to present time the action of the present infinitive is always denied; past time is denoted when the aorist infinitive is used (§1778). Goodwin's rule is the same, that when the present infinitive is used the expression is present or past, and with the aorist infinitive it is always past (GMT §415).
17. Do not assume the denial. These same expressions may also refer to simple past obligation and have the ordinary force of past indicatives, as ἔδει μένειν, he had to remain and did remain; the context determines the meaning, and τί τὸν σύμβουλον ἐχρῆν ποιεῖν; by itself might mean either what was it the duty of the statesman to do, or what was it his duty to have done (§1779). Goodwin says the same and calls the denial idiomatic rather than inherent: these are all originally expressions of past necessity involving no reference to any condition, and it is only by idiomatic usage that the denial of the action of the infinitive comes to be implied in them, and that a past tense comes to express present time (GMT §417).
18. If ἄν appears beside ἔδει, the denial applies to the obligation instead. With these impersonal expressions ἄν is regularly used when the obligation, possibility or propriety, and not the action of the dependent infinitive, is denied (§2315), and with ἄν it is implied that the obligation does not exist, without ἄν that the action of the dependent infinitive is not realized (§2316). Smyth's paraphrase of the form without ἄν is that ἔδει and ἐξῆν are auxiliaries there, the emphasis falls on the infinitive, and ἔδει αἰτιᾶσθαι αὐτόν is virtually equivalent to δικαίως ἂν ᾐτιᾶτο (§2313). Goodwin gives the same equivalence, that ἔδει τοῦτον ἀποθανεῖν is as a construction equivalent to οὗτος δικαίως ἂν ἀπέθανεν (GMT §415).
19. Treat that rule as a working rule and not a law. Goodwin says the common rule for distinguishing ἔδει with the infinitive from ἔδει ἄν with the infinitive often cannot be applied, though as a working rule it can be used in the great majority of cases, and the essential distinction is that the form without ἄν is used when the chief force of the apodosis falls on the infinitive, while the leading verb takes ἄν when the chief force falls on the necessity, propriety or possibility of the act (GMT §423). Smyth's own hedge is milder, that a protasis may often be supplied in thought for ἔδει and χρῆν in simple sentences (§2313).
20. If ἐχρῆν refers to the present, expect the modal sense and expect the manuscripts to waver. Gildersleeve puts both together: when ἔδει, ἐχρῆν and the like refer to the present and not to the past a modal sense is set up and the dependent infinitive becomes unreal, manuscripts and editors often fluctuate between ἔδει and δεῖ and between χρῆν and χρή, and the present indicative gives the simple statement while the imperfect gives surprise or disappointment (Gildersleeve §364). His example is ζῆν ἐχρῆν σ᾽, ὅτ᾽ οὐκέτ᾽ εἶ, and Smyth's is τί σῑγᾷς; οὐκ ἐχρῆν σῑγᾶν, why art thou silent? Thou shouldst not be silent (§1775).
21. Where English uses an auxiliary and the Greek has none, that is the same phenomenon in general. The indicative may be used alone where in English we employ an auxiliary verb, as ὀλίγου εἷλον τὴν πόλιν, a little more and they would have taken the city, and ἀπωλλύμεθα, we might have perished (§1773). Gildersleeve generalises it: the Greek language expresses possibility and power, obligation and necessity, and abstract relations generally, as facts, whereas our translation often implies the failure to realize (Gildersleeve §363). Smyth says the same for the impersonal expressions, that the Greek usage simply states the obligation as a fact which existed in the past and may continue to exist in the present, while in English we usually express the non-fulfilment of the action (§1777). Monro finds the construction in Homer and reads it the same way: the imperfect without ἄν or κεν may express what ought to have been, if the meaning of fitness or obligation is given by the verb or predicate, as κέρδιον ἦεν, it would have been better, and so in Attic with ἐχρῆν and ἔδει (Monro §324).

## 4. Translation guidance

- The plain indicative takes the plain English tense. Goodell's statement of the mood covers the plain case: it presents the action or state as real, in assertions, denials, exclamations and questions (Goodell §452). `topics/tenses.md` covers the tenses themselves.
- The doubtful assertion takes an English hedge: ἀλλὰ μὴ τοῦτο οὐ καλῶς ὡμολογήσαμεν is but perhaps we did not do well in agreeing to this, and such sentences are often regarded as questions with the effect of doubtful affirmation (§1772). Goodwin's renderings are I suspect he is suffering and I suspect he does not suffer (GMT §269).
- The past potential takes "might have", "could have" or "would have been likely to". τίς γὰρ ἂν ᾠήθη ταῦτα γενέσθαι; is who would have expected these things to happen? (§1784). Goodwin's Θᾶττον ἢ ὥς τις ἂν ᾤετο is sooner than one would have thought (GMT §244), and Goodell reads the same class as what was to be expected or was probably true (Goodell §467).
- The unreal takes "would have" in the past and "would" in the present, following the tense rule of step 6. τότε δ᾽ αὐτὸ τὸ πρᾶγμ᾽ ἂν ἐκρῑ́νετο ἐφ᾽ αὑτοῦ is the case would then have been decided on its own merits (§1786). Goodell's pluperfect example is Ἐν τούτῳ τῷ τρόπῳ ἦσθα ἄν με τετῑμωρούμενος, in that way you would have revenged yourself on me (Goodell §469).
- The iterative takes "used to" or "would": διηρώτων ἄν is I used to ask (§1790). Goodwin says it is equivalent to our narrative phrase he would often do this or he used to do it (GMT §162), and Goodell's rendering of Ἐκλεγόμενος τὸν ἐπιτήδειον ἔπαισεν ἄν is picking out the right man, he would strike him (Goodell §467).
- The unattainable wish takes "would that": εἴθε σοι τότε συνεγενόμην is would that I had then been with thee (§1780). Goodwin's Εἴθε σοι, ὦ Περίκλεις, τότε συνεγενόμην is the same sentence with its vocative (GMT §732), and Goodell's εἰ γὰρ τοσαύτην δύναμιν εἶχον is O that I had so much power (Goodell §470).
- ὤφελον takes the same English, not its literal sense: ὤφελε Κῦρος ζῆν is would that Cyrus were now alive, and the Greek says Cyrus ought to be alive (§1781). Goodell prints the pair, Ἀλλ᾽ ὤφελε Κῦρος ζῆν rendered both ways (Goodell §470).
- ἔδει without ἄν takes an English "ought" plus the denial spelled out: τούσδε μὴ ζῆν ἔδει is these men ought not to be alive (§1775), and μένειν ἐξῆν is he might have remained (§1776). Goodwin renders the same two, for these ought not to be living as they are, and he might have stood his ground but really he ran away (GMT §419).
- ἔδει with ἄν takes an English that denies the obligation instead: εἰ ταῦτα ἐποίει, ἔδει ἂν αἰτιᾶσθαι αὐτόν is if he were doing this, it would be necessary to blame him, but as the case now stands it is not necessary (§2315).
- ἐβουλόμην ἄν takes "I should have liked" or "I wish": ἐβουλόμην ἂν Σίμωνα τὴν αὐτὴν γνώμην ἐμοὶ ἔχειν is I should have liked Simon to be, or I wish Simon were, of the same mind as myself (§1789).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading every past tense with ἄν as an unreal. There are three: past potentiality or probability (§1784), unreality in the historical tenses (§1786), and repeated or customary past action (§1790). Goodell says that the second and third of his own three cannot always be distinguished, and that only the context shows which meaning is intended and whether past or present time is meant (Goodell §461, Goodell §467).
- Assuming that an unreal apodosis denies its own action. Goodwin argues that it does not: the denial of the apodosis is not an essential character of the construction, and when a potential form is made the apodosis of an unreal condition it simply states that something would happen in a case which did not arise (GMT §412). Smyth's own account agrees at the root, that there is nothing in the past tenses themselves that requires unreality (§1787).
- Supplying a protasis for a past potential. There is no reference to any definite condition, and a definite ellipsis is not to be supplied (§1785). Gildersleeve says ellipses are often easily supplied but are not necessary, and that the translation is freer than in an elliptical conditional sentence (Gildersleeve §430).
- Expecting ἄν with a present or perfect indicative. The present and perfect indicative are not used with ἄν (§1794, Gildersleeve §433).
- Emending away every ἄν beside a future. Smyth reports the Attic instances as generally emended and one passage in Plato as an anacoluthon (§1793), and Gildersleeve agrees that most instances have been corrected while noting one famous passage where, if ever ἄν was needed with the future indicative, it is needed there (Gildersleeve §432). Homer is a separate matter: κεν with the future is not infrequent there (Monro §326).
- Taking the iterative indicative for a Homeric construction. It is not one. Monro says the use is not Homeric (Monro §324), Goodwin that it is not Homeric but is found in Herodotus and common in Attic (GMT §249), and Smyth calls the iterative indicative of repeated or customary past action post-Homeric (§1790).
- Taking the iterative and the potential as unrelated because they are printed apart. Goodwin reports that separation as the received view and rejects it: the iterative use is generally thought to have no connection with the potential indicative with ἄν, but there is no difficulty in understanding it as an offshoot of the latter once it is seen that the potential did not originally involve any denial of its own action (GMT §249). Smyth derives it the same way, saying the construction is connected with the past potential and denoted originally what could or would take place under certain past circumstances (§1791). Goodwin's own cross-reference at the iterative section still tells the reader to distinguish the two constructions (GMT §162), so the two statements have to be read together.
- Reading ἔδει as a plain past. It may be one, as in ἔδει μένειν, he had to remain and did remain (§1779). Goodwin gives the same example and adds χρῆν γὰρ Κανδαύλῃ γενέσθαι κακῶς, for Candaules was doomed to fall into trouble (GMT §417).
- Trusting the ἔδει against ἔδει ἄν rule as a law. Goodwin says the common rule often cannot be applied, though it works in the great majority of cases, and restates the distinction in terms of where the chief force of the apodosis falls (GMT §423). Smyth states the same rule: with ἄν it is implied that the obligation does not exist, without ἄν that the action of the dependent infinitive is not realized (§2316).
- Reading a present-time ἐχρῆν as a copyist's error. Gildersleeve records that manuscripts and editors do fluctuate between the imperfect and the present here, but the imperfect with present reference is the construction, and it gives surprise or disappointment where the present would give the simple statement (Gildersleeve §364).
- Expecting a Greek auxiliary wherever English needs one. Greek expresses possibility and power, obligation and necessity as facts, whereas our translation often implies the failure to realize (Gildersleeve §363), and in English we usually express the non-fulfilment of the action (Smyth §1777).
- Taking μή beside an indicative as a sign of a wish or a prohibition. It may mark a cautious statement instead, and Goodell separates the two shapes it takes, the question intimating a hope of a negative answer and the bare deprecatory statement (Goodell §488).
- Forgetting that the indicative also works in subordinate clauses, where these pages do not follow it. The indicative is used in final sentences, in object sentences after verbs of effort, caution and fearing, in consecutive sentences with ὥστε, in conditional and temporal sentences, and in object sentences after ὅτι and ὡς with a verb of saying or the like (§1783). Gildersleeve's summary of the same uses adds that the indicative is the only mood used in causal sentences, except as in oratio obliqua (Gildersleeve §368). Monro adds the past tense by assimilation, where a past tense relating to an event which has not happened is followed by a subordinate clause whose verb may also be in a past tense, the event it expresses being equally imaginary, and he says the idiom is the same in principle as the use of past tenses in final clauses, which is common in Attic (Monro §325).
- Reading Greek's generic sentences by the English pattern. In generic sentences the Greek has a tendency to other modal constructions, and in practical questions, personal argument, as in poetry and proverbs, the indicative may be used as in English and Latin, as ἃ μὴ κατέθου, μὴ ἀνέλῃ (Gildersleeve §365).
