# Accusative Case

and `topics/middle-voice.md`. Every claim carries a section citation;
`scripts/verify_topic_pages.py` checks them. `topic: accusative-case`

## 1. Quick answer

The accusative is the case of the direct object, and it is used with all transitive verbs, with some intransitive verbs used transitively, with some verbal nouns, and with adjectives (§1553). But the direct object is of two kinds, and the whole case turns on the difference. The internal object is already contained or implied in the verb and its addition is optional, as in ὁ ἀνὴρ τύπτει πολλᾱ̀ς πληγᾱ́ς; the external object is not contained in the verb but is necessary to explain or define the character of the action, as in ὁ ἀνὴρ τύπτει τὸν παῖδα (§1554). Goodell says the accusative is used mostly with verbs, but also with some adjectives and nouns and with certain prepositions (Goodell §529). He calls the two the direct or outer object of a transitive verb (Goodell §530) and the inner or cognate accusative (Goodell §536). So the first question about a Greek accusative is not which of eighteen uses it is. It is whether the noun restates the verb or receives it, and everything else on this page falls out of that.

## 2. Recognition

- An external object with a transitive verb, which is the ordinary case (§1554). Goodell's example is Γνῶθι σεαυτόν (Goodell §530).
- An accusative where English uses a preposition. Many Greek verbs are transitive whose ordinary English equivalents are intransitive, so σιωπᾶν τι and σῑγᾶν τι mean to keep silence about something (§1557). Goodell gives πλέω τὴν θάλατταν, sail over the sea, and φυλάττομαι, guard against, in the same list (Goodell §530).
- An accusative after a verb that is usually intransitive: ἀσεβεῖν sin against, χαίρειν rejoice at, δακρῡ́ειν weep for (§1558). Compounding also makes the verb transitive, so that διαβαίνειν means pass over and ὑπερβαίνειν means omit (§1559).
- A noun of the same origin as the verb, with an attribute: πολλὴν φλυᾱρίᾱν φλυᾱροῦντα talking much nonsense, ξυνέφυγε τὴν φυγὴν ταύτην he shared in the recent exile (§1564). That is the cognate accusative proper.
- A noun merely of kindred meaning with the verb: ἐξῆλθον ἄλλᾱς ὁδούς they went forth on other expeditions, ἠσθένησε ταύτην τὴν νόσον he fell ill of this disease (§1567).
- A neuter adjective or pronoun standing where that noun would: μέγα φρονήσᾱς highly elated, δεινὰ ὑβρίζειν to maltreat terribly (§1573). Goodell has the same class with μέγα φρονεῖν, literally think large (Goodell §536).
- An accusative naming the thing the verb produced rather than the thing it acted on: ἕλκος οὐτάσαι to smite and thus make a wound, νόμισμα κόπτειν to coin money (§1578). That is the accusative of result.
- An accusative with a verb or adjective of state, naming what the statement is limited to: ὁ ἄνθρωπος τὸν δάκτυλον ἀλγεῖ the man has a pain in his finger, ποταμός, Κύδνος ὄνομα, εὖρος δύο πλέθρων a river, Cydnus by name, two plethra in width (§1601). That is the accusative of respect. Goodell calls it the specifying accusative and prints ἀποτμηθέντες τᾱ̀ς κεφαλᾱ́ς, beheaded (Goodell §537).
- An accusative of measure with a verb of motion or distance: ἐξελαύνει σταθμοὺς τρεῖς, παρασάγγᾱς εἴκοσι καὶ δύο he advances three stages, twenty-two parasangs (§1581).
- An accusative of duration: ἔμεινεν ἡμέρᾱς ἑπτά he remained seven days (§1582). Goodell prints Ἔμεινε τρεῖς ἡμέρᾱς, he remained three days, and ἔτη γεγονὼς ἑβδομήκοντα, seventy years old (Goodell §538).
- Two accusatives with one verb, of which one is a predicate to the other: στρατηγὸν αὐτὸν ἀπέδειξε he appointed him general, πατέρα ἐμὲ ἐκαλεῖτε you were wont to call me father (§1613).
- Two accusatives with one verb, one of a person and one of a thing: Κῦρον αἰτεῖν πλοῖα to ask Cyrus for boats, οὐδεὶς ἐδίδαξέ με ταύτην τὴν τέχνην nobody taught me this art (§1628).
- An accusative used as an adverb: τρόπον τινά in some way, τὴν ταχίστην in the quickest way, χάριν for the sake of (§1608).
- An impersonal participle in the accusative with nothing in the sentence to govern it, as ἐξόν, δέον, παρόν, τυχόν (§2076). That is the accusative absolute.

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Ask whether the accusative repeats the verb or receives it. That is Monro's division as well: the accusative either repeats, with more or less modification, the meaning given by the verb, or the action of the verb is limited or directed by an object wholly distinct from it, as in ἕλκος ὅ με οὔτασε, literally the wound which he wounded me (Monro §132).
2. If the same verb could take either, do not assume the class is fixed by the verb. Many verbs take an accusative either of the external or of the internal object, so that τέμνειν ῡ̔́λην is fell timber and τέμνειν τᾱ̀ς τρίχας is cut off the hair, while τειχίζειν χωρίον is fortify a place and τειχίζειν τεῖχος is build a wall (§1555).
3. If the noun repeats the verb, look for its attribute. An attributive word is usually necessary, but not in Homer, since otherwise adding the noun to the verb would be redundant, and it is omitted in five sorts of case, among them the specialized noun, as in φυλακᾱ̀ς φυλάττειν to stand sentry, and the noun restricted by the article, as in τὸν πόλεμον πολεμεῖν to wage the present war (§1570). The bare noun is rarely added to the verb as a more emphatic form of statement, though often in Euripides, as in λῆρον ληρεῖν to talk sheer nonsense (§1571), and the noun may drop out and leave the attribute alone, as in παῖσον διπλῆν strike twice (§1572).
4. Learn the legal phrases whole. δικάζειν δίκην is decide a case, νῑκᾶν δίκην is win a case, ὀφλεῖν δίκην is lose a case (§1576), and Goodell prints δικάζειν δίκην τινά, decide a suit, and φεύγειν δίκην τινά, be defendant in a suit (Goodell §536), where Smyth's φεύγειν δίκην τινός is be put on one's trial for something (§1576).
5. If the accusative names a part or a quality rather than a thing acted on, it is an accusative of respect. Verbs that denote a state, and adjectives, may take an accusative that names a thing in respect to which the verb or adjective is limited, and it usually expresses a local relation or the instrument (§1600). Smyth's three groups are parts of the body, qualities and attributes, and the sphere in general (§1601).
6. Do not confuse the accusative of respect with its neighbours. Smyth says that it is not to be confused with the accusative after intransitive adjectives or after certain passives (§1604), and that the instrumental dative is also available for it, as is εἰς, κατά or πρός, so that διαφέρειν ἀρετῇ is used as well as the accusative (§1603).
7. If the accusative measures, ask whether it measures space or time. The accusative denotes extent in space and time (§1580); it denotes the space or way over which an action is extended and the measure of the space traversed (§1581); and it denotes extent of time, as ξυμμαχίᾱν ἐποιήσαντο ἑκατὸν ἔτη they made an alliance for a hundred years (§1582).
8. For time, test whether the whole period is covered. The accusative of time implies that the action of the verb covers the entire period, and when uninterrupted duration is emphasized παρά with the accusative and διά with the genitive are used instead (§1583). For how long a situation has lasted or how much time has elapsed, an ordinal is used without the article, often with οὑτοσῑ́ added, as τὴν μητέρα τελευτήσᾱσαν τρίτον ἔτος τουτῑ́, my mother who died two years ago (§1585).
9. If there are two accusatives, decide which pattern you have before you translate either. Either the second is a predicate to the first (§1613), or the two are an internal and an external object together (§1620), or they are a person and a thing both governed by the verb (§1628), or one of them is the accusative of extent, which is freely used in the same sentence with other accusatives, as in ὑπερενεγκόντες τὸν Λευκαδίων ἰσθμὸν τᾱ̀ς ναῦς (§1633).
10. To sort the predicate accusative from the object, look at the article. The absence of the article generally distinguishes the predicate noun from the object (§1614), and a predicate accusative may also stand in apposition to the object, as ἔδωκα δωρειᾱ̀ν τὰ λύτρα, I gave them the price of their ransom as a free gift (§1616). Goodell adds the passive test: in the passive the direct object is made subject and the other accusative stays a predicate noun agreeing with it (Goodell §534).
11. To sort the internal from the external object in a pair, ask which one is a person. The external object refers to a person and the internal object refers to a thing, and the internal one stands in closer relation to the verb: τὸν ἄνδρα τύπτειν τᾱ̀ς πληγᾱ́ς to strike the man the blows (§1620). The verbs of doing anything to or saying anything of a person work the same way: πολλὰ ἀγαθὰ ῡ̔μᾶς ἐποίησεν he did you much good (§1622).
12. If the verb is one of asking, teaching, depriving or reminding, expect two outer objects. Both person and thing are equally governed by the verb (§1630 is where Smyth lists the alternatives), and Goodell names the same verb classes, ask, demand, teach, remind, hide, deprive, with τὰ ἡμέτερʼ ἡμᾶς ἀποστερεῖ, he is depriving us of our property (Goodell §535).
13. If a participle stands in the accusative with no noun to agree with, test for the accusative absolute. A participle stands in the accusative absolute, instead of the genitive, when it is impersonal or has an infinitive as its subject, and when impersonal such participles have no apparent grammatical connection with the rest of the sentence (§2076). Goodell states the same rule and prints Ἐξόν εἰρήνην ἔχειν αἱρεῖται πολεμεῖν, when it is possible to have peace, he chooses war (Goodell §591).
14. If the participle is personal and still absolute, look for ὡς or ὥσπερ in front of it. The participle of a personal verb may be used absolutely if it is preceded by ὡς or ὥσπερ, and cases without them are rare (§2078).

## 4. Translation guidance

- The external object has the same construction in English (§1554).
- The cognate accusative usually goes into English as an adverb or as an English cognate phrase. Smyth translates both ways: πολλὴν φλυᾱρίᾱν φλυᾱροῦντα is talking much nonsense (§1564), and Ὀλύμπια νῑκᾶν is to win an Olympian victory (§1570).
- The adjective in a cognate accusative is often what English needs on the verb. Monro says a cognate accusative is generally used to introduce the adjective or pronoun which really qualifies or defines the predication contained in the verb, and he renders ἄπρηκτον πόλεμον πολεμίζειν as to wage a war without result, comparing the adverbial use of a neuter adjective in ἄλληκτον πολεμίζειν, to war without ceasing (Monro §135). Goodell files μέγα φρονεῖν, be proud, literally think large, under the neuter adjective or pronoun and not under the noun with a modifying word (Goodell §536).
- Translate the accusative of result with an English verb that already contains the result: ἕλκος οὐτάσαι to smite and thus make a wound (§1578). With verbs that mean to effect anything the predicate is proleptic, so τοῦτον τρέφειν τε καὶ αὔξειν μέγαν is to nurse and exalt him into greatness (§1579).
- The accusative of respect takes an English "in" or a possessive. τυφλὸς τά τ᾽ ὦτα τόν τε νοῦν τά τ᾽ ὄμματ᾽ εἶ is blind art thou in ears, and mind, and eyes, and διαφέρει γυνὴ ἀνδρὸς τὴν φύσιν is woman differs from man in nature (§1601). Goodell's βέλτῑόν ἐστι σῶμά γʼ ἢ ψῡχὴν νοσεῖν is 'Tis better to be ill in body than in soul (Goodell §537).
- Monro calls the same thing the accusative of the part affected, or accusative of reference, and the Latin grammarians call it accusativus graecus (Monro §137).
- The accusative of extent takes a bare English measure phrase: ἀπέχει ἡ Πλάταια τῶν Θηβῶν σταδίους ἑβδομήκοντα is Plataea is seventy stades distant from Thebes (§1581).
- The adverbial accusative is simply an English adverb. τίνα τρόπον is in what way? and πάντα τρόπον is in every way (§1608); μέγα is greatly, πολύ is much, οὐδέν is not at all (§1609); τί is why? and τοῦτο is for this reason (§1610). Goodell's list overlaps it and adds οὐκ ἀρχήν not at all, πρῶτον at first and τὸ λοιπόν hereafter (Goodell §540).
- Some adverbial accusatives are best read as appositives to the whole clause. Goodell puts προῖκα as a gift, χάριν τινός as a favour to some one, and τὸ λεγόμενον as the saying is, under the accusative in apposition to a clause or sentence (Goodell §539).
- The predicate accusative takes an English "as" or nothing at all: Κῦρον σατράπην ἐποίησε he made Cyrus satrap (Goodell §534), and τὴν σῑγήν σου ξυγχώρησιν θήσω I shall consider your silence as consent (§1613).
- The double accusative of person and thing usually needs an English preposition on the thing: ἀναμνήσω ῡ̔μᾶς καὶ τοὺς κινδῡ́νους I will remind you of the dangers also, τούτων τὴν τῑμὴν ἀποστερεῖ με he deprives me of the value of these things (§1628).
- The accusative absolute takes an English clause with when, since or although, and the choice is the reader's: ἐξὸν τὸ ἔλᾱττον is when it is possible to choose the less, οὐ μετὸν αὐτῷ is though he has no right, and μέλον γέ σοι is because it concerns you (§2076). Goodell translates the same participle both ways, προσῆκον as since or though it is fitting (Goodell §591).
- Where the absolute participle is passive and impersonal, English has the same idiom. Smyth says γεγραμμένον, δεδογμένον, εἰρημένον and προσταχθέν answer to English granted this is so, this done, which said (§2076).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Hunting for the class before reading the sentence. Smyth says that it is impossible to apportion all cases among the varieties of the accusatives and that many may be placed under different heads (§1607), and Goodell says of the adverbial accusatives that they are special forms of the other uses and we can not always say which (Goodell §540).
- Taking the cognate accusative as the source of the other accusatives. Smyth derives most adverbial accusatives from the internal object (§1607) and thinks the accusative of respect is probably in origin, at least in part, an accusative of the internal object (§1605). Monro rejects that derivation: the cognate accusative is only a special form of the use of the accusative as a defining or qualifying word, and grammarians have explained other constructions by its help because it is familiar, thereby falling into the error of deriving the simple from the complex (Monro §136). On Monro's account ἄριστα βουλεύειν is simpler and earlier than ἀρίστην βουλὴν βουλεύειν, not the reverse (Monro §136).
- Expecting one principle to cover the internal object. Monro says no such principle can be laid down, and that the accusative seems originally to have had a very wide adverbial use which was encroached upon by the more specific uses of other cases (Monro §132).
- Reading the accusative of the part affected as an object. Monro says that in κάμνει χεῖρα the accusative limits the action and does not express the notion of the verb or an attribute of it, but merely denotes a thing by reference to which the verb is limited or characterized (Monro §137).
- Reading a poetic accusative of the goal as an object. In poetry after verbs of motion the accusative may be used without a preposition to express the goal, as ἄστυ Καδμεῖον μολών, having come to the city of Cadmus (§1588), and Goodell gives the same use with Ἥξεις Ὑβριστὴν ποταμὸν οὐ ψευδώνυμον (Goodell §533).
- Concluding from that use that the accusative is the case of the goal. Monro says there is no reason to infer it, that a verb of motion is naturally defined by a noun expressing place, and that the accusative is used with verbs denoting motion from as well (Monro §140).
- Taking a dative of the person as equivalent to the accusative. When the dative of the person is used, something is done for him and not to him: πάντα ἐποίησαν τοῖς ἀποθανοῦσιν they rendered all honours to the dead (§1624).
- Reading the accusative of swearing as an object. The accusative is used in asseverations with the adverbs of swearing μά, οὐ μά, ναὶ μά and νή, and μά is negative except when ναί precedes (§1596).
- Forgetting that verbs of emotion take an accusative in Greek. φοβεῖσθαι, αἰσχῡ́νεσθαι and ἐλεεῖν take it, though several of the class usually take the dative in prose (§1595). The verbs of doing or saying good and ill take it too, εὖ ποιεῖν and κακῶς λέγειν among them (§1591).
- Missing the verbs that take an accusative object though the English verb is intransitive or needs a preposition. φεύγειν is flee from, λανθάνειν is escape the notice of, μένειν is wait for, and ἐπιλείπειν is give out, fail (§1597).
- Reading two accusatives after a verb of dividing as object and predicate. Verbs of dividing take one accusative of the thing divided and one of its parts: Κῦρος τὸ στράτευμα κατένειμε δώδεκα μέρη, Cyrus divided the army into twelve divisions (§1626).
- Assuming a double accusative must be two independent objects. Monro shows that in some cases the two accusatives are not to be explained independently, but one construes with the phrase formed by the verb and the other, so that we cannot say ῥέζειν τινά, to do to a person, but we may have κακὸν ῥέζειν τινά, to do evil to a person or thing (Monro §141).
- Expecting the case of an object shared by two verbs to suit both. The case of an object common to two verbs is generally the one demanded by the nearer verb (§1634).
- Reading a genitive absolute where the participle is impersonal. The impersonal character of these expressions would not be shown by the genitive, since the participle in that case marks a distinction between masculine or neuter and feminine, and the accusative absolute occurs first in Herodotus and the Attic prose writers of the fifth century (§2077).
- Treating Homer's usage as Attic. Monro's accusative of time and space puts the word expressing duration of time in the accusative, ἕνα μῆνα a month, and he compares these accusatives with the neuter adjectives of quantity (Monro §138). Monro also finds an accusative of the person spoken to with εἰπὸν and αὐδάω, rare with the simple verbs and regular with the compounds (Monro §140).
