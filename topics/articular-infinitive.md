# Articular Infinitive

## 1. Quick answer

The article turns the infinitive into a noun without taking the verb away. The articular infinitive, while having the character of a substantive, retains the functions of a verb, and it may take dependent clauses (§2025). It admits the constructions of an ordinary substantive, so that the same verb yields a nominative τὸ ποιεῖν, a genitive τοῦ ποιεῖν, a dative τῷ ποιεῖν and an accusative τὸ ποιεῖν, each in any tense (§2026). The article is regularly used when the connection uniting the infinitive to another word has to be expressed by the genitive, the dative, or a preposition (§2030). So the case of the article, not the infinitive, tells you what the phrase is doing in the sentence. The negative is μή (§2028). Goodwin states the historical point behind all of this: by the substantial loss of its dative force the infinitive became verbalized, and by the assumption of the article it was substantivized again with a decided increment of its power (GMT §788).

## 2. Recognition

- A neuter singular article, in any of four cases, standing immediately before an infinitive: τὸ ποιεῖν making or to make, τοῦ ποιεῖν of making, τῷ ποιεῖν for making, by making (§2026).
- The article separated from its infinitive by the infinitive's own subject, object or adjuncts. The whole of that, dependent clauses included, forms one large substantival idea: τὸ μὲν γὰρ πόλλ᾽ ἀπολωλεκέναι κατὰ τὸν πόλεμον "the fact that we have lost much in the war" (§2037). Goodwin describes the same thing as the infinitive with its subject, object, or other adjuncts preceded by the article τό, the whole sentence standing as a single noun (GMT §806).
- An article before an infinitive after a preposition. The article cannot ordinarily be omitted when the infinitive follows a preposition, the only exception being a few cases of ἀντί with the simple infinitive in Herodotus (GMT §803). Smyth records the same omission after ἀντί in Herodotus (§2032 g).
- τοῦ μή before an infinitive, which may be negative purpose: τοῦ μὴ τὰ δίκαια ποιεῖν "in order not to do what was just" (§2032 e).
- τὸ μή or τὸ μὴ οὐ after a verb of hindering, preventing or denying, where the μή only strengthens the negative already in the leading verb (GMT §811).
- An article with an infinitive in apposition to a demonstrative: τοῦτό ἐστι τὸ ἀδικεῖν, τὸ πλέον τῶν ἄλλων ζητεῖν ἔχειν "injustice is this: to seek to have more than other people" (§2035). Goodwin lists the same example under apposition to a noun in the same case (GMT §804).
- An article with an infinitive in an exclamation: τῆς τύχης· τὸ ἐμὲ νῦν κληθέντα δεῦρο τυχεῖν "my ill-luck! that I should happen now to have been summoned hither!" (§2036). Goodwin gives the construction its own heading, the infinitive with τό in exclamations of surprise or indignation (GMT §805).
- ἄν standing inside the phrase. The articular infinitive may take ἄν (§2030), and Goodwin cites οὐ τῷ φεύγειν ἂν τὸ πλῆθος τὸ ὑμέτερον "not because I would avoid your people" (GMT §212).
- Not in Homer. The use of the infinitive as an indeclinable noun is subsequent to Homer and became possible with the later use of the article, and the use of the infinitive with an article in the genitive or dative is wholly post-Homeric (Monro §242).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Read the case of the article first. It has the value of a substantive as regards its constructions (§2030). Smyth treats the construction under the four cases, each in its own section: the nominative of the articular infinitive as subject (§2031), the genitive (§2032), the dative (§2033) and the accusative (§2034).
2. Nominative: look for a subject or a predicate noun. The articular infinitive is treated as subject, predicate noun, and object like the simple infinitive (§2027), and the plain infinitive is used as subject especially with quasi-impersonal verbs and expressions (§1984) and as a predicate noun with ἐστί in definitions (§1986). Smyth's example is νέοις τὸ σῑγᾶν κρεῖττόν ἐστι τοῦ λαλεῖν "in the young silence is better than speech" (§2031). Goodwin says the article may be prefixed to make the infinitive more prominent as a noun in the structure of the sentence (GMT §789), and most of his subject examples would admit the construction without the article by making the infinitive less prominent as a subject nominative (GMT §790).
3. Genitive: run Smyth's list. It limits the meaning of substantives, adjectives, and verbs (§2032 a). Adnominal: τοῦ πιεῖν ἐπιθῡμίᾳ "from desire to drink" (§2032 b). Partitive, and after comparatives: τοῦ θαρσεῖν τὸ πλεῖστον εἰληφότες "having gained the greatest amount of courage" (§2032 c). After verbs: ἐπέσχομεν τοῦ δακρῡ́ειν "we desisted from weeping" (§2032 d). Purpose, often a negative purpose (§2032 e). In a genitive absolute (§2032 f). After prepositions and adverbs (§2032 g). Goodwin's list runs the same way, an adnominal genitive, a genitive after verbs and adjectives and with comparatives, a partitive genitive, a genitive absolute, and a genitive expressing cause, purpose, or motive, and he adds that the final use appears first and chiefly in Thucydides (GMT §798).
4. Dative: expect cause, manner or means, or a word that governs the dative. The dative goes with verbs, adjectives, and adverbs (§2033 a) and after prepositions (§2033 b). Goodwin states the same range: the infinitive with τῷ may express cause, manner, or means, or it may follow verbs, adjectives, and adverbs which take the dative (GMT §799).
5. Accusative: object, or a preposition, or an accusative of respect. As object: δείσᾱς τὸ ζῆν "fearing to live" (§2034 a). After a preposition: μέγιστον ἀγαθὸν τὸ πειθαρχεῖν φαίνεται εἰς τὸ καταπρᾱ́ττειν τᾱ̓γαθά "obedience appears to be an advantage of the greatest importance with regard to the successful accomplishment of excellent objects" (§2034 b). The accusative after verbs and nouns is, in many cases, like an accusative of respect: τὸ δρᾶν οὐκ ἠθέλησαν "they refused to do it" (§2034 e). Goodwin reads those accusatives the same way, as coming nearest to the accusative of respect or limitation (GMT §795).
6. If a preposition stands before the article, do not look for a conjunction. The infinitive with the article, as genitive, dative, or accusative, very often follows prepositions, or adverbs used as prepositions (GMT §800). Goodwin also records which combinations do not occur, so that the infinitive is not found with ἀνά in any case, nor with κατά in the genitive, nor with ὑπέρ in the accusative (GMT §801). Smyth's genitive list after prepositions is ἀπό, πρό, διά, μετά, περί, ὑπό, ἕνεκα, χάριν, χωρίς, πλήν, μέχρι, and adverbs (§2032 g); his accusative list is διά, ἐπί, κατά, μετά, περί (§2034 b).
7. If the preposition is ὑπέρ, read the phrase as a purpose clause. To express purpose the genitive with ὑπέρ is very common: ὑπὲρ τοῦ μὴ τὸ κελευόμενον ποιῆσαι "in order not to do what was commanded" (§2032 g). Goodwin says the genitive of the infinitive with ὑπέρ is often equivalent to a final clause and prints the same sentence from Demosthenes (GMT §802).
8. The negative is μή and has no extra meaning. The infinitive not in indirect discourse has μή, and the articular infinitive has μή (§2711); παράδειγμα τοῦ μὴ ῡ̔μᾶς ἀδικεῖν is "a warning not to injure you" (§2712). Goodell states the same rule, that the negative with an infinitive is usually μή except when the infinitive represents an original finite verb which had οὐ (Goodell §564).
9. If the leading verb means hinder, prevent, deny or omit, expect the negative to be redundant. Verbs signifying to hinder take both the simple infinitive and the articular infinitive, and such verbs may take the strengthening but redundant negative μή (§2038). Smyth's summary of constructions numbers them, so that εἴργει με τὸ μὴ γράφειν is rather common and εἴργει με τοῦ μὴ γράφειν is not so common (§2744). Goodwin numbers the same set in reverse order, four forms with the simple infinitive and the genitive with τοῦ (GMT §807) and a fifth with τὸ μή (GMT §811). Goodell says that with verbs of hindering and the like the infinitive may also have the article τοῦ, as a from genitive (Goodell §572).
10. If the leading verb is itself negatived, expect μὴ οὐ. Instead of μὴ οὐ we find also μή, τὸ μή, τοῦ μή, τὸ μὴ οὐ, but not τοῦ μὴ οὐ (§2749). Goodwin says the same of the article: τὸ μὴ οὐ may be used with verbs and expressions which do not have a negative meaning, provided these are themselves negatived or are interrogative implying a negative (GMT §814), and he says that the nature of the leading verb will always make the force of the negative plain (GMT §813).
11. Read the tense as stage of action, not time, unless the phrase reports someone's words. The articular infinitive may indicate time after verbs of saying or thinking, or may be timeless (§2029). Where it does report, each tense keeps its own force: verbs of saying and thinking rarely take the articular infinitive, as ἐξομεῖ τὸ μὴ εἰδέναι; "wilt thou swear thou didst not know?" (§2034 g). Goodwin says of that class that each tense of the infinitive preserves its time, and even the infinitive with ἄν occurs (GMT §794). Goodell states the rule generally for an infinitive that represents a finite verb: the original tense and tense meaning are retained, the time being relative to that of the principal verb (Goodell §577). Gildersleeve prints the same construction tense by tense as the articular infinitive as the representative of the indicative, present, aorist and future (Gildersleeve §328).
12. If you cannot see why the article is there, ask whether the simple infinitive was available. The accusative of the infinitive with τό appears after many verbs and verbal expressions which usually take only the simple infinitive, as τὸ σπεύδειν δέ σοι παραινῶ "I commend speed to thee" (§2034 c), and some verbs take the articular infinitive as an object when the simple infinitive could not be used, as μόνον ὁρῶν τὸ παίειν τὸν ἁλισκόμενον "taking heed only to strike any one he caught" (§2034 f). Goodwin makes the same observation and gives the reason: the double character of the articular infinitive, as noun and verb, permits it to stand as an object wherever the object accusative of a noun would be allowed (GMT §792).
13. If the sense turns on the presence of the article, look the verb up. With verbs of fearing there is a difference: with the articular infinitive φοβοῦμαι means simply I fear, and with the infinitive without the article it commonly has the force of hesitate or feel repugnance, so that φοβοῦμαι τὸ ἀδικεῖν is I fear wrong-doing in general (§2238 a).
14. If the phrase is Homeric, it is probably not an articular infinitive at all. Smyth calls ἀνῑ́η καὶ τὸ φυλάσσειν the nearest approach to this use in Homer (§2025). Monro names the same line as the only instance which really comes near the later articular infinitive, and then reads its article as an article of contrast, adding that we need not take τὸ φυλάσσειν closely together (Monro §259). Goodwin says the construction appears first in Pindar, since τό in that Odyssey line can hardly be the article (GMT §788). Take the Homeric τό as a demonstrative unless something forces the other reading.

## 4. Translation guidance

- English has no one rendering, because the article is doing case work that English does with prepositions and with the word "fact". Gildersleeve states the transformation: ἡ τιμὴ τῆς πόλεως becomes τὸ τιμᾶσθαι τὴν πόλιν, and ἡ σωτηρία τῆς νήσου becomes τὸ σῶσαι τὴν νῆσον, because the article enables the infinitive to assume all the constructions of the substantive that are not inconsistent with its verbal nature (Gildersleeve §582).
- Nominative subject: an English gerund or an infinitive. τὸ Πελοποννησίους αὐτοῖς μὴ βοηθῆσαι παρέσχεν ῡ̔μῖν is "the fact that the Peloponnesians did not come to their assistance enabled you" (§2031). Gildersleeve's perfect examples need an English perfect: τὸ δίκης ἀφεῖσθαι μεγάλη δωρειὰ ἦν "to have been allowed to go free of punishment was a great boon" (Gildersleeve §319).
- Accusative object: an English gerund. τὸ ἑλληνίζειν παρὰ τούτων ἔγωγε ἔμαθον is "I learned speaking Greek from them" (Gildersleeve §321), and τλήσομαι τὸ κατθανεῖν is "I will endure the dying of the death" (Gildersleeve §323). Goodwin renders the same Aeschylean line "I shall dare to die" (GMT §791).
- Genitive of desire or hope: "of" with a gerund. πρὸς τὴν πόλιν προσβαλόντες ἐς ἐλπίδα ἦλθον τοῦ ἑλεῖν is "they attacked the city and entertained hopes of taking it" (§2032 b).
- Genitive of purpose: "in order to", "so as to", or a plain infinitive. ἐτειχίσθη Ἀταλάντη τοῦ μὴ λῃστᾱ̀ς κακουργεῖν τὴν Εὔβοιαν is "Atalante was fortified to prevent pirates from ravaging Euboea" (§2032 e).
- Dative of means or cause: "by". οὐδενὶ τῶν πάντων πλέον κεκράτηκε Φίλιππος ἢ τῷ πρότερος πρὸς τοῖς πρᾱ́γμασι γίγνεσθαι is "Philip has conquered us by nothing so much as by being beforehand in his operations" (§2033 a).
- Dative after a preposition: take the preposition's ordinary sense. ἐν τῷ φρονεῖν γὰρ μηδὲν ἥδιστος βίος is "for life is sweetest in being conscious of nothing", and πρὸς τῷ μηδὲν ἐκ τῆς πρεσβείᾱς λαβεῖν is "in addition to gaining nothing from the embassy" (§2033 b). Goodwin's list gives the same values for ἐκ, ἀντί, ἀπό, ἕνεκα, πρός, ἐν, ἐπί, διά, εἰς and παρά with the articular infinitive (GMT §800).
- Accusative after a preposition: again the preposition decides. πρὸς τὸ μετρίων δεῖσθαι πεπαιδευμένος is "schooled to moderate needs" (§2034 b).
- Render the genitive with τοῦ μή after verbs of hindering as "from", not "from not". πᾶς γὰρ ἀσκὸς δύο ἄνδρας ἕξει τοῦ μὴ καταδῦναι is "for each skin-bag will prevent two men from sinking" (§2744). Goodwin translates the same sentence "will keep two men from sinking" (GMT §807).
- τὸ μή after a verb of hindering is also "from": οἷοί τε ἦσαν κατέχειν τὸ μὴ δακρῡ́ειν "they were able to restrain their weeping" (§2744). Goodwin renders the same clause "to restrain their tears" (GMT §811).
- In apposition, use a colon or "namely". Smyth treats the plain infinitive in apposition the same way, εἷς οἰωνὸς ἄριστος, ἀμῡ́νεσθαι περὶ πάτρης "one omen is best, to fight for our country" (§1987), and the articular infinitive stands in apposition to a preceding word, especially a demonstrative (§2035).
- A genitive articular infinitive after an abstract noun often defines what the noun applies to: ἀμαθίᾱ ἡ τοῦ οἴεσθαι εἰδέναι ἃ οὐκ οἶδεν is "the ignorance of thinking one knows what one does not know" (§1322).
- Where the whole phrase is a clause, translate it as a clause and put the article's case into the English conjunction. Goodwin's Demosthenic examples run to several lines each and stand as a single noun (GMT §806); Smyth's τὸ μὲν γὰρ πόλλ᾽ ἀπολωλεκέναι κατὰ τὸν πόλεμον is "the fact that we have lost much in the war" (§2037).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Taking the neuter article as agreeing with a neuter noun. It is the article of the infinitive, and the article has the power to make substantival any word or words to which it is prefixed, infinitives included: καλοῦσί γε ἀκολασίᾱν τὸ ὑπὸ τῶν ἡδονῶν ἄρχεσθαι "they call intemperance being ruled by one's pleasures" (§1153 f).
- Taking τοῦ before an infinitive as a possessive genitive belonging to the noun before it. Read the whole phrase as one substantive in the genitive (§2032 a).
- Reading τοῦ μή after a verb of hindering as "from not". Smyth states the rule: observe that this idiom does not have the logical meaning "from not", which we should expect (§2744).
- Reading τὸ μὴ οὐ as two negatives that cancel. The μή merely strengthens the negative idea of the leading verb, and τὸ μὴ οὐ appears when that leading verb is itself negatived (GMT §811).
- Assuming every τοῦ μή is a verb of hindering. Goodwin separates them: the use of τοῦ μή with the infinitive as an ordinary negative expression is to be distinguished from the one that belongs with verbs of hindrance (GMT §813).
- Reading μή as conditional or as a sign of doubt. It is simply the negative that the articular infinitive takes (§2028), and the ordinary negative of the infinitive is μή because the infinitive was employed as early as Homer in an imperative sense (§2711 a).
- Expecting the tenses to mark time. The tenses of the object infinitive not in indirect discourse are timeless and denote only stage of action (§1989), and the articular infinitive is timeless except after verbs of saying or thinking (§2029).
- Looking for a conjunction to translate after a preposition. Greek has put the clause into a case instead, and in Thucydides the accusative, genitive, and dative with prepositions run to 163 cases against 135 without (GMT §788).
- Expecting the construction in Homer and reading a Homeric τό as an article. It marks contrast rather than definiteness there, and the Homeric article contrasts where the Attic article defines (Monro §259).
- Treating an articular infinitive after ἀμελέω or ἐπιμελέομαι as an error for the plain infinitive. A few verbs which govern the genitive of a noun allow also the genitive of the infinitive with τοῦ as well as the simple infinitive (GMT §793).
- Forgetting that it is still a verb. As a verb the infinitive has voice and tense, may take a subject in the accusative and an object, and is modified by adverbs and particles including ἄν; as a noun it may take the article and so be marked as having a noun construction (Goodell §562).
- Missing the construction after μέχρι and ἄχρι, where Smyth notes that they take the articular infinitive in Demosthenes (§2383).
