# Purpose Clauses

Status: drafted 2026-09-07 on the pattern of `topics/genitive-absolute.md`
and `topics/result-clauses.md`. Every claim carries a section citation;
`scripts/verify_topic_pages.py` checks them. `topic: purpose` names the
derived record in `data/smyth/topics.json`.

## 1. Quick answer

Final clauses denote purpose and are introduced by ἵνα, ὅπως, ὡς in order that, that, with the negative ἵνα μή, ὅπως μή, ὡς μή, and μή alone, lest (§2193). The verb stands in the subjunctive after a primary tense and in the optative after a secondary tense: γράφω ἵνα ἐκμάθῃς "I write (on this account) that you may learn", ἔγραψα ἵνα ἐκμάθοις "I wrote (on this account) that you might learn" (§2196). After a secondary tense the subjunctive may be used in place of the optative, and then it sets forth a person's previous purpose in the form in which he conceived it (§2197 a). A final clause stands in apposition to τούτου ἕνεκα or διὰ τοῦτο expressed or understood (§2195), and that is the test that separates it from an object clause after a verb of effort or of fearing, both of which stand in apposition to a demonstrative expressed or implied (§2207).

## 2. Recognition

- ἵνα, ὅπως or ὡς with a subjunctive after a present, perfect or future, or with an optative after an aorist or imperfect (§2196).
- μή alone, with no final conjunction, meaning lest: μὴ σπεῦδε πλουτεῖν μὴ ταχὺς πένης γένῃ "haste not to be rich lest thou soon become poor" (§2196).
- ὄφρα, strictly while, until, in Epic and Lyric, and ἕως in Epic (§2193 a). ὄφρα is the most common final particle in Homer, and is originally temporal, meaning while and then until (GMT §314).
- ὅπως with the subjunctive and ἄν in positive clauses: τοῦτ᾽ αὐτὸ νῦν δίδασχ᾽, ὅπως ἂν ἐκμάθω "tell me now this very thing, that I may learn" (§2201).
- ὡς ἄν and ὅπως ἄν with the optative, very rare in Attic prose and more frequent after secondary than after primary tenses (§2202).
- The future indicative after ὅπως, rarely after ὡς, ὄφρα and μή, in the same sense as the subjunctive: οὐδὲ δι᾽ ἓν ἄλλο τρέφονται ἢ ὅπως μαχοῦνται "nor are they maintained for any other single purpose than for fighting" (§2203).
- A secondary tense of the indicative after ἵνα, and sometimes after ὅπως or ὡς, when the purpose was not attained (§2185 c).
- A final clause with no principal clause: ἵν᾽ ἐκ τούτων ἄρξωμαι "to begin with this" (§2204), and the colloquial ἵνα τί, originally to what end: ἵνα τί ταῦτα λέγεις; "why do you say this?" (§2204).
- τούτου ἕνεκα or διὰ τοῦτο standing before the clause: ἐκκλησίᾱν τούτου ἕνεκα ξυνήγαγον ὅπως ὑπομνήσω "I have convened an assembly for this reason that I may remind you" (§2195).
- The negative is μή (§2705 a). After the final particles the regular negative is μή, but after μή, lest, οὐ is used (GMT §305).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Find the conjunction, and note which one it is. ἵνα is the chief final conjunction in Aristophanes, Herodotus, Plato and the orators; ὅπως is the chief one in Thucydides and in Xenophon; ὡς is rare in prose except in Xenophon, and is common in tragedy, especially in Euripides; μή alone is very rare in prose except in Xenophon and Plato (§2193). GMT sets out the same distribution: ἵνα appears in Homer as a fully developed final conjunction, is overshadowed by ὄφρα in epic and lyric poetry and by ὡς in tragedy, and in Plato and the orators has almost exterminated the other final particles (GMT §311).
2. If the conjunction is ὡς, decide first whether it is final at all. Relative ὡς as, how is originally an ablative from the relative stem, and all its uses represent that primitive meaning (§2989); it often shows the original meaning in which way, how, as (§2193). Goodell separates the three uses that matter here: ὡς purely relative in comparisons (Goodell §633), ὡς declarative meaning how, that (Goodell §635), and ὡς final, meaning that, in order that, frequent in poetry but rare in prose except in Xenophon (Goodell §636).
3. If the conjunction is ἵνα, check that it is not the local ἵνα. ἵνα ἄν is not final but local, wherever, and the original meaning of ἵνα was local and denoted the end to be reached (§2201 a). Goodell puts the two meanings side by side, relative where, still the prevailing meaning in poetry, and final in order that, the most frequent final conjunction in prose (Goodell §640), and gives ἐν ἀγορᾷ, ἵνα ῡ̔μῶν πολλοὶ ἀκηκόᾱσι "in the market-place, where many of you have heard me" for the local use (Goodell §641).
4. Now separate purpose from result. Final clauses denote purpose (§2193); a clause of result denotes a consequence of what is stated in the principal clause (§2249). The two constructions can apply in the same cases: a clause of intended result with ὥστε and the infinitive is often used where ὅπως might occur in an object clause after a verb of effort (§2267 b). They differ in the source of the idea of purpose. With ὥστε the infinitive expresses only the result, and the idea of purpose comes only from the general sense and especially from the meaning of the leading verb, whereas ἵνα μή would express only purpose (§2267 a). GMT states the value of the result construction in the same terms: it properly means only that one action or state is of such a nature as to be followed by another as a consequence, and that the second action actually does follow is often implied apart from the words (GMT §587).
5. Then separate a pure final clause from an object clause after a verb of effort. Test the apposition. A final clause stands in apposition to τούτου ἕνεκα or διὰ τοῦτο (§2195); object clauses after verbs of effort and after verbs of fearing both stand in apposition to a demonstrative expressed or implied, οὐδένα δεῖ τοῦτο μηχανᾶσθαι, ὅπως ἀποφεύξεται πᾶν ποιῶν θάνατον "no man ought to contrive (this) how he shall escape death at any cost" (§2207). GMT uses the same distinction, which it calls very strongly marked: an object clause can stand in apposition to a preceding τοῦτο, whereas a final clause would stand in apposition to τούτου ἕνεκα (GMT §304).
6. Ask what the leading verb is. Object clauses after verbs of effort are introduced by ὅπως, rarely by ὡς, scarcely ever by ἵνα, and the negative is μή (§2209). Verbs of effort are those denoting to take care or pains, to strive, and the same construction follows certain verbs of will signifying to ask, command, entreat, exhort and forbid (§2210). Smyth distinguishes three kinds: final clauses proper denote a purpose set forth in a definite action, object clauses after verbs of effort consider means to accomplish or avert a result, and object clauses after verbs of fearing deprecate an undesired result (§2208).
7. If a future indicative follows ὅπως, do not settle the question on the tense alone. Object clauses after verbs of effort take the future indicative with ὅπως after primary and secondary tenses (§2211), but the future indicative is also used, especially in poetry, after ὅπως in a pure final clause in the same sense as the subjunctive (§2203). GMT counts that second use as occasional, chiefly with ὅπως, very seldom with ὄφρα, ὡς and μή, never with ἵνα, and with only four undoubted examples in prose (GMT §324).
8. Read the mood against the tense of the leading verb, and expect the subjunctive to intrude. After a secondary tense the subjunctive may stand in place of the optative, and Thucydides and Herodotus prefer this vivid subjunctive while the poets, Plato and Xenophon prefer the optative (§2197 a). GMT explains the licence as the double construction of indirect discourse, since a final clause after a past tense expresses some person's previous purpose (GMT §318), and gives the counts writer by writer, 528 subjunctives to 894 optatives in all writers before Aristotle (GMT §320). Both moods may stand in one sentence after a secondary tense (§2199), and GMT warns against the common interpretation that the subjunctive marks the immediate and the optative the remote consequence (GMT §321).
9. If the optative stands after a primary tense, look for a reference to the past in the leading verb. The optative is very rare there except when the tense implies such a reference: οἴχονται ἵνα μὴ δοῖεν δίκην "they have gone away that they might not suffer punishment", where οἴχονται is practically equivalent to ἔφυγον (§2200). GMT keeps the two cases apart, calling the optative for the subjunctive after primary tenses a mere irregularity of construction for which no good reason appears (GMT §322), while allowing it where the leading verb implies a reference to the past as well as the present (GMT §323).
10. If ἄν stands in the clause, do not read a condition into it. ἄν and κέ do not appreciably affect the meaning, though originally these particles seem to have had a limiting and conditional force (§2201 b). ἵνα is the only purely final conjunction in that it does not limit the idea of purpose by the idea of time or of manner, and therefore never takes ἄν or κέν, since the purpose is regarded as free from all conditions (§2193 a). ἄν does stand with ὅπως and the subjunctive (§2201), and ὡς and ὄφρα take ἄν or κέ in poetry, especially in Homer (§2201 a). GMT gives the same reason and the same exception: the final particles of relative origin, ὡς, ὅπως and ὄφρα, sometimes take ἄν or κέ with the subjunctive, and ἵνα and μή never do (GMT §325). Monro reports the rule for Homer and credits it to Gildersleeve: ἵνα is the only purely final particle, the only one that does not limit the purpose by the notion of time or manner, and so clauses with ἵνα do not take κεν or ἄν, because the purpose as such is unconditional (Monro §286).
11. Having found ἄν, keep the final ἄν apart from two others. Final ὡς ἄν must not be confused with ὡς ἄν in conditional relative clauses (§2201 a), where the subjunctive with ἄν vividly anticipates the realization of a future event (§2565); and final ὡς ἄν must be distinguished from ὡς ἄν consecutive (§2202). When ἄν stands with the optative, read it as potential: after primary tenses that optative is certainly, after secondary tenses probably, potential, and its combination with the final conjunction produces a conditional relative clause (§2202 b).
12. If a secondary tense of the indicative follows the conjunction, read the purpose as unattained. The subordinate clause takes a past tense of the indicative in dependence on a past tense denoting unreality, and here the principal clause is an unfulfilled wish, an unfulfilled apodosis, or a question with οὐ, so that the indicative denotes that the purpose was not or cannot be attained (§2185 c). GMT sorts the tenses: the imperfect refers to present time or to continued or repeated action in past time, the aorist and pluperfect to past time (GMT §333). Two conditions must both hold, that the clause refers to present or past time and that the failure of the purpose is distinctly implied; otherwise the ordinary subjunctive or optative is used, and both constructions may occur in one sentence (GMT §336). Smyth states the same reservation, that assimilation does not take place when the final clause sets forth a real future purpose of the agent or does not show whether the purpose was realized (§2185 c).
13. Read the negative, and let it confirm rather than decide. Final clauses have μή (§2705 a), and so do object clauses with ὅπως after verbs of effort (§2705 b), so the negative does not separate the two. In order that no one is ἵνα (etc.) μηδείς or μή τις, in order that never is ἵνα (etc.) μήποτε, and in order that not is μηδέ after μή (§2193 b). μὴ οὐ after a final μή is very rare in Homer and in Attic (§2193), and GMT confines it after Homer to clauses after verbs of fearing, with three exceptions (GMT §306).
14. If the clause is temporal in form, ask whether it is final in force. Temporal conjunctions denoting limit as to duration or limit as to termination may imply purpose, so ἕως till, against the time when, in order that, and πρίν before, in order that not (§2418 a). In the Odyssey ἕως, usually with the aorist optative after a secondary tense, is almost a final conjunction (§2419). In Attic prose ἕως with the aorist optative after a secondary tense sometimes implies an expectation, hope or purpose on the part of the subject of the main verb (§2420). GMT reaches the same result from the meaning of until, whose idea is that the action of the leading clause continues to a time at which that of the dependent clause takes place (GMT §611), and adds that when such a clause implies a purpose and depends on a past tense it generally takes the optative, though the subjunctive may be used to retain the mood in which the purpose would be originally conceived (GMT §614).
15. If no conjunction is present at all, run through the equivalents before deciding there is no purpose in the sentence. Smyth translates they sent a herald to announce in seven ways: ἔπεμψαν κήρῡκα ἵνα (ὅπως) ἀπαγγέλλοιτο, ὅστις (ὃς) ἀπαγγελεῖται, ἀπαγγελοῦντα or rarely ἀπαγγέλλοντα, ὡς ἀπαγγελοῦντα, ἀπαγγέλλειν, τοῦ ἀπαγγέλλειν, and ὑπὲρ (ἕνεκα) τοῦ ἀπαγγέλλειν, and refers to ὥστε for an intended result (§2206). GMT lists the same equivalents: the relative with the future indicative, or in Homer with the subjunctive, the infinitive, the infinitive with ὥστε or ὡς, the future participle, and ὑπέρ with the genitive of the articular infinitive (GMT §338).
16. When the context does not decide, translate what the Greek says and add nothing. Smyth's own table shows the three constructions overlapping: παρακαλεῖ ῑ̓ᾱτρὸν ὅπως μὴ ἀποθάνῃ is common as a final clause, ἐπιμελεῖται ὅπως μὴ ἀποθανεῖται is common as an object clause after a verb of effort, and φοβεῖται ὅπως μὴ ἀποθανεῖται occurs occasionally after a verb of fearing, so that according to the form of expression employed the construction of the three kinds may differ in varying degree or be identical (§2208). Where the form is identical, translate the clause as the leading verb requires and do not press a distinction the Greek has not drawn.

## 4. Translation guidance

- The plain rendering is in order that, that, and for the negative lest (§2193). English commonly uses to and the infinitive for the same meaning: κατάμενε ἵνα καὶ περὶ σοῦ βουλευσώμεθα is "remain behind that we may consider your case also" (§2196).
- After a primary tense use may, after a secondary tense might: γράφω ἵνα μὴ ἐκμάθῃς "I write (on this account) that you may not learn", ἔγραψα ἵνα μὴ ἐκμάθοις "I wrote (on this account) that you might not learn" (§2196).
- Where a subjunctive stands after a secondary tense, the English is still might, but the Greek is a kind of quotation of the agent's own words: Ἀβροκόμᾱς . . . κατέκαυσεν ἵνα μὴ Κῦρος διαβῇ "Abrocomas burned the boats in order that Cyrus might (may) not cross", where the thought of Abrocomas was "I will burn the boats that Cyrus may not cross" (§2197 a).
- Where ὅπως ἄν or ὡς ἄν has the optative, English needs a conditional clause for the potential force: ἔδωκε χρήματα Ἀνταλκίδᾳ ὅπως ἂν πληρωθέντος ναυτικοῦ . . . οἵ τε Ἀθηναῖοι . . . μᾶλλον τῆς εἰρήνης προσδέοιντο "he gave money to Antalcidas in order that, if a fleet were manned, the Athenians might be more disposed to peace" (§2202).
- A past tense of the indicative in the clause needs a might have, and the English should show that the purpose failed: τί δῆτ᾽ οὐκ ἔρρῑψ᾽ ἐμαυτὸν τῆσδ᾽ ἀπὸ πέτρᾱς, ὅπως τῶν πάντων πόνων ἀπηλλάγην; "why indeed did I not hurl myself from this rock, that I might have been freed from all these toils?" (§2185 c). GMT gives the three tenses: ἵνα τοῦτο ἔπραττεν is in order that he might be doing this, but he is not doing it, ἵνα τοῦτο ἔπραξεν is that he might have done this, but he did not, and ἵνα τοῦτο ἐπεπράχει is that he might have done this, but he has not (GMT §333).
- ἵνα τί is to what end, and the ellipse is of a verb of happening: ἵνα τί (γένηται); "to what purpose?" (§946). GMT records the same colloquial idiom, ἵνα τί; that what?, where the answer usually supplies the final clause (GMT §331).
- Where the whole leading clause is missing, supply it. GMT reads ἵνα γε μὴ προλαβὼν χρήματα τῆς πόλεως ἢ πράξεις δρασμῷ χρήσῃ as "that you may not take to flight", after an implied refusal that the Greek leaves unwritten (GMT §332).
- A relative clause of purpose is best translated by an English infinitive of purpose: ἔδοξε τῷ δήμῳ τριᾱ́κοντα ἄνδρας ἑλέσθαι, οἳ τοὺς πατρίους νόμους συγγράψουσι "the people voted to choose thirty men who should codify the ancestral laws" (§2554). These clauses regularly take the future indicative even after past tenses, with the negative μή, and ὅς is commoner than ὅστις (§2554). GMT gives the same rule and adds that the antecedent may be either definite or indefinite, but that the negative is always μή because of the final force (GMT §566). Goodell explains the construction differently: ὅστις with an indicative often suggests cause, purpose or result by treating a definite antecedent as indefinite in order to characterise it, as πρεσβείᾱν πέμπειν ἥτις ταῦτ᾽ ἐρεῖ "to send an embassy to say this" (Goodell §619).
- The future participle carries purpose after verbs denoting to come, go, send, summon: προπέμψαντες κήρῡκα πόλεμον προεροῦντα "having sent a herald in advance to proclaim war"; the present participle is also used, ἔπεμπον . . . λέγοντας ὅτι "they sent men to say that" (§2065). GMT translates the type the same way, ἦλθε λυσόμενος θύγατρα "he came to ransom his daughter" (GMT §840).
- The bare infinitive of purpose is used in prose especially after verbs meaning to give, entrust, choose, appoint, take, receive (§2009), and it goes usually only with verbs taking the accusative: ταύτην τὴν χώρᾱν ἐπέτρεψε διαρπάσαι τοῖς Ἕλλησιν "he gave this land over to the Greeks to plunder" (§2008). Goodell reads that same infinitive as a to or for dative with a wide range of meaning, from simple purpose to cases where it merely defines the application of a word or phrase (Goodell §565).
- The articular infinitive in the genitive expresses purpose, often a negative purpose: τοῦ μὴ τὰ δίκαια ποιεῖν "in order not to do what was just", and the use with ὑπέρ or ἕνεκα is more common (§2032 e). With ὑπέρ: ὑπὲρ τοῦ μὴ τὸ κελευόμενον ποιῆσαι "in order not to do what was commanded" (§2032 g). GMT treats that genitive with ὑπέρ as often equivalent to a final clause (GMT §802). Smyth places the same genitive of purpose next to the genitive of cause (§1408).
- ὡς with a future participle is in the hope of, with the avowed intention of (§2086 b), and it reports the agent's declared aim without committing the writer: συλλαμβάνει Κῦρον ὡς ἀποκτενῶν "he seized Cyrus for the purpose (as he declared) of putting him to death" (§2086 c).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading a final ὡς as a comparative or a declarative ὡς. All three uses occur, and the final one is rare in prose outside Xenophon (Goodell §636); the relative meaning in which way is the source of them all (§2989).
- Calling the subjunctive after a past tense an error. After a secondary tense the subjunctive may be used in place of the optative (§2197), and it is the commoner of the two in Thucydides and Herodotus (§2197 a). GMT's counts of subjunctives against optatives after past tenses show more subjunctives in five writers, Herodotus, Thucydides, Lysias, Isocrates and Aeschines, equal numbers in Demosthenes, and more optatives everywhere else in the table (GMT §320).
- Treating a subjunctive and an optative in one sentence as a mixture of constructions. Both may be used after a secondary tense (§2199), and in some cases, especially when the subjunctive precedes, the subjunctive may express the immediate purpose while the optative expresses the less immediate one (§2199 a). GMT refuses that reading as a general rule, because it manifestly could not apply to the first example printed there (GMT §321).
- Making ἄν conditional. ἄν and κέ do not appreciably affect the meaning of a final clause (§2201 b); with ὅπως ἄν the final force is stronger than with ὡς ἄν (§2202 b).
- Expecting ἵνα ἄν. ἵνα never takes ἄν or κέ, since the purpose is regarded as free from all conditions (§2193 a), and where ἵνα ἄν does appear it is the local ἵνα (§2201 a). GMT reports the single Homeric case of κέ with ἵνα as a potential subjunctive with κέ, not a final ἵνα κε (GMT §325).
- Translating a past indicative in a final clause as a plain past. It marks a purpose that was not or cannot be attained (§2185 c), and ἄν cannot properly be added to the indicative in this construction (GMT §335).
- Assuming the future indicative after ὅπως proves an object clause after a verb of effort. It occurs in pure final clauses too (§2203), and its use there is an extension of the use after verbs of effort (§2203).
- Missing that the leading verb, not the conjunction, may decide. Some verbs take, by analogy, but in negative clauses only, the construction either of verbs of effort or of verbs of fearing (§2210 b).
- Reading a ὥστε clause as a purpose clause because the sense is purposive. The idea of purpose there comes only from the general sense and especially from the meaning of the leading verb (§2267 a).
- Forgetting that a temporal clause can express purpose. ἕως may mean against the time when, in order that (§2418 a), and the optative in such a clause is due to the principle of indirect discourse, so that the subjunctive with ἄν might have been used instead (§2420).
- Treating an implicit indirect discourse as a real quotation. The alternative construction of final clauses with subjunctive or optative is that of implicit indirect discourse (§2198), and clauses of purpose and object clauses after verbs of effort admit the alternative constructions of indirect discourse (§2622 f).
- Looking for a leading clause that was never written. The principal clause is sometimes omitted (§2204), and GMT gives an example where the omission is easily supplied (GMT §332).
- Missing the optative that stands by assimilation. Final clauses may take the optative without ἄν after an optative of the principal clause (§2186 c), rarely in prose but occasionally after an optative of wish in poetry (§2186 c). Smyth places that beside the past indicative of §2185 c as the two ways a final clause is assimilated in mood (§2205).
- Taking μή after a verb of fearing for a final μή. Final clauses were developed from original coördination, and ἀπόστιχε μή τι νοήσῃ Ἣρη "depart lest Hera observe aught" was originally let Hera not observe anything (§2194). GMT traces both final clauses and clauses with μή after verbs of fearing to an original parataxis, or coördination of two independent sentences (GMT §307).
- Expecting the optative to be usable after a primary tense. The optative is very rare there except when that tense implies a reference to the past as well as to the present (§2200), and GMT calls the optative for the subjunctive after primary tenses a mere irregularity of construction (GMT §322).
