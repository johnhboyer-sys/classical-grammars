# The Subjunctive

`topics/middle-voice.md`. Every claim carries a section citation;
`scripts/verify_topic_pages.py` checks them and

## 1. Quick answer

Mood designates by the form of the verb the mode or manner in which the speaker conceives of an assertion concerning the subject, and Greek has four moods proper, indicative, subjunctive, optative and imperative (§1759, §1760). The independent subjunctive refers to future time and has three main uses, the voluntative, which expresses the will of the speaker and is akin to the imperative, the deliberative, and the anticipatory, which anticipates an action as an immediate future possibility (§1796). Smyth's own list of the chief uses of the independent subjunctive is a different triad, the hortatory, the prohibitive and the deliberative (§1795): the hortatory expresses a request or a proposal (§1797), the prohibitive a prohibition (§1800), and the anticipatory is not on that list but has sections of its own (§1810). Gildersleeve says the subjunctive is the mood of anticipation, and it anticipates the realization of the predicate, which anticipation appears chiefly as an act of the will (Gildersleeve §369). Monro puts the same mood between two English auxiliaries, and says it of the subjunctive in a simple sentence or in the principal clause of a complex sentence: it may be said in general to express either the will of the speaker or his sense of the necessity of a future event, and like the English must and shall, by which it may usually be rendered, it is intermediate in meaning between an imperative and a future (Monro §274). In a dependent clause the mood has much less independent meaning, and the question is whether ἄν is there: conditional, relative and temporal clauses requiring the subjunctive must have ἄν, which is more closely attached to the conditional, relative and temporal word than it is to the subjunctive (§1768).

## 2. Recognition

- A first person plural with no subject pronoun, negatived by μή. Goodwin's minimal pair is the whole construction: Ἴωμεν, let us go; μὴ ἴωμεν, let us not go (GMT §256). Smyth's example continues with a second verb, νῦν ἴωμεν καὶ ἀκούσωμεν τοῦ ἀνδρός, let us go now and hear the man, with μήπω ἐκεῖσε ἴωμεν, let's not go there yet, for the negative (§1797). Goodell prints Ὦ παῖδες, ἱστώμεσθα, my children, let us rise (Goodell §472).
- A summoning particle in front of it. ἄγε, φέρε (δή), in Homer ἄγε (δή), sometimes precedes, as ἄγε σκοπῶμεν, come, let us consider, and ἴθι (δή) rarely precedes (§1797). Goodwin's list of them is longer: Ἄγε, ἄγετε, εἰ δ᾽ ἄγε, φέρε, ἴθι, δεῦρο, and δεῦτε may precede this subjunctive, and so sometimes ἔα, permit, let (GMT §255).
- A first person singular after one of those particles, which is the less common half of the same use: φέρε δὴ περὶ τοῦ ψηφίσματος εἴπω, let me now speak about the bill (§1797). Goodwin's is Φέρε ἀκούσω, come, let me hear (GMT §257), and Goodell's is φέρʼ ἐπʼ αὐτὴν ἴω, let me go to her (Goodell §472).
- A question in the first person that asks what the speaker is to do. εἴπωμεν ἢ σῑγῶμεν; is shall we speak or keep silence?, τί δρᾱ́σω; ποῖ φύγω; is what am I to do? whither shall I fly?, and the negative is μή, as in μὴ φῶμεν; shall we not say? (§1805). Goodwin's name for it is the interrogative subjunctive, in questions of appeal, where the speaker asks himself or another what he is to do: Εἴπω τοῦτο; shall I say this? (GMT §287).
- βούλει or βούλεσθε standing in front of such a question: βούλει σοι εἴπω; do you wish me to say to you? (§1806). Goodwin adds the poetic pair, sometimes θέλεις or θέλετε (GMT §287).
- μή with an aorist subjunctive in the second or third person, which is a prohibition: μηδὲν ἀθῡμήσητε, do not lose heart, and ὑπολάβῃ δὲ μηδείς, and let no one suppose (§1800). Gildersleeve's are μὴ θαυμάσῃς, do not wonder, and μηδεὶς . . . θαυμάσῃ, let no one wonder (Gildersleeve §376).
- μή with a present subjunctive and no verb of fearing anywhere, which is a doubtful assertion: μὴ ἀγροικότερον ᾖ τὸ ἀληθὲς εἰπεῖν, I suspect it's rather bad form to tell the truth, and with μὴ οὐ a doubtful negation, μὴ οὐκ ᾖ διδακτὸν ἀρετή, virtue is perhaps not a thing to be taught (§1801).
- οὐ μή in front of it, which is not two negatives cancelling but an emphatic denial: οὐ μὴ παύσωμαι φιλοσοφῶν, I will not cease from searching for wisdom (§1804).
- ἐάν, ὅταν or ἐπειδάν at the head of the clause. Those are the conditional and temporal words that ἄν has coalesced with, ἐᾱ́ν (ἤν, ᾱ̓́ν), ὅταν, ὁπόταν, ἐπήν (ἐπᾱ́ν), ἐπειδάν from εἰ, ὅτε, ὁπότε, ἐπεί, ἐπειδή with ἄν (§1768).
- In Homer, a subjunctive standing where Attic would want a future: οὐ γάρ πω τοίους ἴδον ᾱ̓νέρας, οὐδὲ ἴδωμαι, for never yet saw I such men, nor shall I see them (§1810).

## 3. The uses, and how to tell them apart

1. Ask first whether the clause is independent. The use of the moods in independent sentences and principal clauses is treated apart, and the dependent construction of the moods was developed from their independent use (§1769). If the clause is independent, begin with the chief uses of §1795, the hortatory, the prohibitive and the deliberative, and use steps 11 to 14 for the anticipatory, the doubtful assertion and the doubled negative; if it is dependent, go to step 7.
2. If the verb is first person and the sentence is not a question, it is hortatory. The hortatory subjunctive, present or aorist, is used to express a request or a proposal, with μή for the negative, usually in the first person plural (§1797). Goodwin explains why the mood is used here: the want of a first person in the imperative is supplied by the first person of the subjunctive, which expresses both positive and negative exhortations and appeals (GMT §255). Smyth says the same, that the hortatory use compensates for the absence of an imperative of the first person (§1799). Gildersleeve puts it under the same heading, the imperative of the first person, positive and negative, with μή as the negative particle, and gives σκοπώμεθα against σκεψώμεθα and μὴ διώκωμεν (Gildersleeve §373).
3. If it is first person singular and negatived, read the tone before the sense. The first person singular in negative exhortations is rare and poetic and may convey a warning or a threat, as μή σε, γέρον, κοίλῃσιν παρὰ νηυσὶ κιχείω, old man, let me not find thee by the hollow ships, and this use is often regarded as prohibitive (§1798). Monro reads the Homeric line the same way and prints it as direct forbidding with the first person singular, μή σε κιχείω, let me not catch you (Monro §278).
4. If it is second or third person with μή, it is prohibitive. The subjunctive in the second and third persons of the aorist is often used to express prohibitions, negative μή, usually in the second person, and the third person of the present subjunctive is rare (§1800). Watch the tense: for the aorist subjunctive the present imperative may be employed, so that μὴ ποιήσῃς or μὴ ποίει ταῦτα is do not do this, not μὴ ποιῇς (§1800). Goodwin states the rule and its reason together: in prohibitions, in the second and third persons, the present imperative or the aorist subjunctive is used with μή and its compounds, and the distinction of tense here is solely the ordinary distinction between the present and aorist and has no reference to the moods, so that Μὴ ποίει τοῦτο is do not go on doing this and μὴ ποιήσῃς τοῦτο is simply do not do this (GMT §259). Goodell sorts the same material by person: the prohibitive subjunctive is used in the first person, as Μή ἀναμένωμεν ἄλλους, let us not wait for others, and in the second and third persons in the aorist only, as Μή λίπῃς μʼ οὕτως μόνον, leave me not thus alone, and Μηδεὶς εἴπῃ, let no one say (Goodell §473).
5. If the sentence is a question and the speaker is asking what he is to do, it is deliberative. The deliberative subjunctive, present or aorist, is used in questions when the speaker asks what he is to do or say, negative μή, usually in the first person; the rare second person repeats a question, and the third person generally represents the first, commonly with τὶς, as τί τις εἶναι τοῦτο φῇ; how shall anyone say this is so? (§1805). Smyth notes that the subjunctive question does not refer to a future fact, but to what is, under the present circumstances, advantageous or proper to do or say (§1805). Gildersleeve puts the same test as a rule about the answer, that the subjunctive question expects an imperative answer, and gives the same restriction on person, chiefly the first, the second only when the speaker puts himself in the place of the person addressed, the third chiefly when it represents the first (Gildersleeve §379). Goodwin adds the third person's own examples, Τί τις εἶναι τοῦτο φῇ; and Ποῖ τις οὖν φύγῃ; and notes that it is used chiefly when a speaker refers to himself by τὶς (GMT §289). Goodell calls the same use dubitative and observes that such questions are often exclamatory, calling for no answer (Goodell §471).
6. If it is a question and μή is the negative, that settles the class, and the reason is worth having. The use of μή and not οὐ in questions is due to the fact that the deliberative construction is simply the interrogative form of the hortatory subjunctive: φῶμεν is let us say and μὴ φῶμεν; is are we not to say? (§1809). Goodwin says the same: if ἔλθωμεν; shall we go? is the interrogative of ἔλθωμεν, let us go, then μὴ ἔλθωμεν; shall we not go? is the interrogative of μὴ ἔλθωμεν, let us not go (GMT §291).
7. In a dependent clause, look for ἄν before anything else. Conditional, relative and temporal clauses requiring the subjunctive must have ἄν, and where the particle does not coalesce with the conditional, relative or temporal word, it is usually separated from it only by such words as μέν, δέ, τέ, γάρ (§1768). Goodwin's version of the rule names the exceptions as well: in Attic Greek ἄν is regularly used with the subjunctive in protasis and in conditional relative sentences, and sometimes in final clauses with ὡς and ὅπως, being always closely joined with the particle or the relative, but never in independent sentences (GMT §200).
8. If the clause is final, expect no ἄν, and expect ἵνα never to take it. The subjunctive without ἄν is used in dependent clauses of purpose, after verbs of fearing, and in the protasis of conditional and conditional relative sentences (§1812). Goodwin states which final particle may take it: the final particles which have a relative origin, ὡς, ὅπως and ὄφρα, sometimes have ἄν or κέ in final clauses with the subjunctive, and Ἵνα and μή never take ἄν or κέ in this way (GMT §325). Gildersleeve states the same division for dependent clauses: in prose the pure subjunctive is confined to the final sentence, which is necessarily imperative in its nature, and in other classes of sentences ἄν with the subjunctive is the rule, though there are many exceptions in the older language and in poetry (Gildersleeve §387). `topics/purpose-clauses.md` treats this clause by clause.
9. If the temporal word already carries ἄν, read the compound. With ὅτε, ὁπότε, ἐπεί and ἐπειδή, ἄν forms ὅταν, ὁπόταν, ἐπάν or ἐπήν, and ἐπειδάν, and in Homer, where κέ is generally used for ἄν, the same clauses appear as ὅτε κε and ἐπεί κε (GMT §522). The force of ἄν with the subjunctive cannot usually be expressed in English (§1768). Goodwin says the same, that in its second use, with the subjunctive, it generally has no force that can be made perceptible in translation (GMT §193). `topics/particle-an.md` treats the particle's other uses.
10. If the text is Homer or a poet, the ἄν rule has more exceptions and the mood has more uses. Gildersleeve marks the exceptions where he states the rule: subjunctive dependent clauses outside the pure final sentence regularly take ἄν except in the older language, and the omission is sometimes clerical error, sometimes perhaps euphony, sometimes a survival of the older construction (Gildersleeve §456). Smyth records one case of the same thing, that ἄν is more often absent in general conditions than in vivid future conditions (§2339). Monro adds instead the rule for the Homeric dependent subjunctive: the rule in Homer is that the subjunctive is not used in a subordinate clause to express a past purpose or condition, but it may be used when the governing verb is a gnomic aorist or an aorist expressing a general denial, or in a simile, or if the action of the subordinate clause is still future at the time of speaking (Monro §298).
11. If an independent subjunctive in Homer reads like a future, it is the anticipatory subjunctive. In Homer the subjunctive is often closely akin to the future indicative and refers by anticipation to a future event, with οὐ for the negative: καί νύ τις ὧδ᾽ εἴπῃσι, and one will say (§1810). Goodwin describes the same use and Gildersleeve the same again, that the Homeric subjunctive is often indistinguishable from a future and serves as a missing aorist future where prose would employ the optative with ἄν (GMT §284, Gildersleeve §386). Monro sorts it by person: in the first person the subjunctive supplies the place of an imperative, and a subjunctive of the second and third person in an affirmative sentence is usually an emphatic future (Monro §275).
12. If that Homeric subjunctive carries κέ or ἄν, it is still independent. The subjunctive with ἄν, more commonly κέ, is used in Homer in independent sentences and clauses, negative οὐ: ἐγὼ δέ κ᾽ ἄγω Βρῑσηίδα, but in that case I will take Briseis (§1813). Monro reads κέ there as marking the conditional nature of the second clause when two clauses are set against one another by δέ (Monro §275). Gildersleeve records the same construction and rules it out of Attic prose: the subjunctive with ἄν is not employed as a form of independent statement in Attic prose (Gildersleeve §451).
13. If μή stands with an independent subjunctive and no verb of fearing, decide between fear and cautious assertion by the tense and the author. In Homer μή with the independent subjunctive indicates fear and warning or suggests danger, usually with the aorist, as μή τι χολωσάμενος ῥέξῃ κακὸν υἷας Ἀχαιῶν (§1802). In Attic the present subjunctive with μή may express a doubtful assertion, and the use is chiefly Platonic and often ironical (§1801). Goodwin separates the two the same way, the Homeric independent subjunctive with μή expressing apprehension coupled with a desire to avert the object of fear (GMT §261), and the later construction expressing a suspicion that something may prove to be true, a cautious assertion (GMT §265). Goodell takes both as one growth: the subjunctive with μή expresses what one fears and desires to avert, with μὴ οὐ what one fears will not be, and this is also softened to a cautious assertion or made a question (Goodell §474).
14. If the sentence opens οὐ μή, do not add the negatives together. From the use in doubtful assertion is probably developed the construction of οὐ μή with the aorist, less often the present, subjunctive to denote an emphatic denial, as οὐκέτι μὴ δύνηται βασιλεὺς ἡμᾶς καταλαβεῖν, the king will no longer be able to overtake us (§1804). Goodwin distinguishes two uses under the one form: the subjunctive and the future indicative are used with the double negative οὐ μή in independent sentences, sometimes expressing a denial like the future indicative with οὐ, and sometimes a prohibition like the imperative or subjunctive with μή (GMT §294), so that οὐ μὴ τοῦτο γένηται means this surely will not happen (GMT §295). Smyth records the prohibitive half as a note, that οὐ μή with the subjunctive of the second person in the dramatic poets occasionally expresses a strong prohibition, οὐ μὴ ληρήσῃς, don't talk nonsense (§1800).
15. Whatever the use, do not read time out of the tense. The tenses of the moods except the indicative do not express time in independent sentences (§1859), and the subjunctive mood as such refers to the future, its tenses denoting only the stage of the action, continuance, simple occurrence, or completion with permanent result: τὰ αὑτῶν ἅμα ἐκποριζώμεθα is present, πορισώμεθα οὖν πρῶτον τὴν δαπάνην is aorist (§1860). Goodell states the same rule and adds the warning that the distinction often disappears in translating but is not to be overlooked (Goodell §475).

## 4. Translation guidance

- Hortatory: "let us", or "let me" in the singular. Ἴωμεν is let us go and μὴ ἴωμεν is let us not go (GMT §256); δεῦρο ἐξαναστῶμεν εἰς τὴν αὐλὴν is let us rise, go out here into the court (Goodell §472).
- Where a particle precedes, English often wants "come". ἄγε σκοπῶμεν is come, let us consider (§1797), and Φέρε ἀκούσω is come, let me hear (GMT §257). Smyth leaves it out of one of his own: φέρε δὴ περὶ τοῦ ψηφίσματος εἴπω is let me now speak about the bill (§1797).
- Prohibitive: a plain English negative imperative, with the tense carried in the aspect. μὴ φοβοῦ is don't be fearful and μὴ φοβηθῇς is don't be frightened; in maxims μή with the present imperative is preferred, μὴ κλέπτε being don't be a thief and μὴ κλέψῃς don't steal this or that (§1841).
- Third-person prohibitions take an English "let no one". μηδεὶς θαυμάσῃ is let no one be astonished, and μηδεὶς νομίσῃ is let no one think (§1840).
- Deliberative: "am I to", "are we to", "shall I". τί ποιῶμεν; or τί ποιήσωμεν; is what shall we do? (§1860); Τί εἴπω; is what shall I say? and τί βούλεσθε εἴπω; is what do you want me to say? (GMT §287); and Goodwin prints Ποῖ τράπωμαι; ποῖ πορευθῶ; among the same questions of appeal (GMT §287).
- τί πάθω; is its own idiom and not deliberative at all. It is what will become of me, what am I to do, literally what shall I undergo, and Smyth says the subjunctive there is not deliberative but refers to a future event (§1811). Goodwin gives the same rendering and adds a second sense, what harm will it do me, beside what will become of me (GMT §290). Gildersleeve reads the two sides apart: what am I to submit to in order to a certain end is one side, what am I to do is another (Gildersleeve §384). Goodell prints it first among the dubitative questions, τί πάθω; what shall I experience? what will happen to me? (Goodell §471).
- The doubtful assertion takes an English hedge, not a fear. μὴ ἀγροικότερον ᾖ τὸ ἀληθὲς εἰπεῖν is I suspect it's rather bad form to tell the truth, and ἀλλὰ μὴ οὐχ οὕτως ἔχῃ is but I rather think this may not be so (§1801). Goodwin's rendering of the same construction is I am afraid the truth may be too rude a thing to tell (GMT §265).
- The Homeric μή with the subjunctive takes a fear the English has to spell out. Smyth's rendering of μή τι χολωσάμενος ῥέξῃ κακὸν υἷας Ἀχαιῶν is "may he not (as I fear he may) in his anger do aught to injure the sons of the Achaeans" (§1802). Goodell's shorter one is Μὴ σοὺς διαφθείρῃ γάμους, where his gloss is that the speaker fears she will (Goodell §474).
- οὐ μή takes an emphatic English future, not a double negative. οὐ μὴ παύσωμαι φιλοσοφῶν is I will not cease from searching for wisdom (§1804), and Goodwin's Οὐ μὴ πίθηται is he will not obey (GMT §295).
- The anticipatory subjunctive takes an English future. οὐδὲ ἴδωμαι is nor shall I see them, and καί νύ τις ὧδ᾽ εἴπῃσι is and one will say (§1810). Monro's rendering of the negative use is the same: οὐ γάρ τίς με βίῃ γε ἑκὼν ἀέκοντα δίηται is no man shall chase me against my will (Monro §276).
- A dependent subjunctive's ἄν gets no English at all, and the tense decides the English tense instead. ἐπειδὰν ἅπαντ᾽ ἀκούσητε, κρῑ́νατε is when you have heard everything, decide (§1860).
- Where the deliberative subjunctive has been replaced by a periphrasis, translate the periphrasis. ἡμεῖς δὲ προσμένωμεν; ἢ τί χρὴ ποιεῖν; is and shall we wait? or what must we do?, and τί ποιητέον; is what are we to do? (§1807). Gildersleeve's τί δῆτα δεῖ σκοπεῖν; is the same substitution (Gildersleeve §383).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading the subjunctive as a subordinate mood because of its name. The name is due to the belief of the ancient grammarians that the mood was always subordinate, so that εἴπω, shall I speak?, was explained as due to the omission of a preceding βούλει (§1795). Gildersleeve records the same derivation, that such phrases as ἴωμεν, let us go, were explained by βούλει ἴωμεν; (Gildersleeve §370). Goodwin shows that for one Attic shape the ancient explanation is not wrong: in εἰ δ᾽ αὖ βούλεσθε καὶ φλεγμαίνουσαν πόλιν θεωρήσωμεν, βούλεσθε is not parenthetical but is felt to be the leading verb on which the subjunctive depends (GMT §287).
- Taking the two prohibitive forms as a choice of mood. They are a choice of tense across two moods, and Goodwin says that the distinction has no reference to the moods (GMT §259). Smyth reads the difference as virtually one of tenses, the present denoting an action continuing, the aorist an action concluded, and adds that the distinction is often immaterial, often a difference of tone rather than of meaning, and sometimes too subtle for dogmatic statement (§1841). Gildersleeve reports a third explanation and declines to settle it: some scholars have seen a certain urbanity in the change from the second person imperative to the second person subjunctive in the pungent aorist form, but it is noteworthy that a like limitation is found in Sanskrit (Gildersleeve §377).
- Expecting a present subjunctive to prohibit in the second person. It does not: the type μὴ γράφῃς is never used, and μὴ γράψον occurs rarely in poetry (§1840). Monro finds the rule already absolute in Homer for the second person, and notes that the third person is occasionally used when fear and not command is expressed (Monro §278).
- Expecting an affirmative subjunctive in the second or third person in Attic. In affirmative exhortations the second and third persons of the subjunctive are not regularly used, the imperative being the only recognised form (GMT §258). Goodell records the same and its single exception, once in the second person (Goodell §472).
- Reading μή with the subjunctive as always a prohibition. It may be prohibitive, and the prohibitive subjunctive in the second and third persons of the aorist is the common case (§1800). It may instead indicate fear and warning, or suggest danger, which is what it does in Homer (§1802). Or it may be a doubtful assertion, with μὴ οὐ for a doubtful negation (§1801). Goodwin divides them the same way, prohibitions with μή and its compounds (GMT §259), the Homeric independent subjunctive with μή implying fear (GMT §261), and μή with the subjunctive in cautious assertions (GMT §265).
- Reading the subjunctive with ἄν as a potential in Attic. It is not one: the subjunctive with ἄν is not employed as a form of independent statement in Attic prose (Gildersleeve §451), and Goodwin says ἄν is never used with the subjunctive in independent sentences (GMT §200). The construction is Homeric (§1813), and Goodwin says that in epic poetry the independent subjunctive with nearly the sense of the future indicative sometimes takes κέ or ἄν, forming a future potential expression (GMT §201).
- Carrying the Homeric uses into Attic. Monro states the distinction: in Attic the use of the subjunctive in independent clauses is either hortatory, deliberative, or prohibitive, and the use with ἄν, the use in affirmation, and the negative uses do not survive (Monro §279). Smyth says the same of one of them, that the futural subjunctive is retained in Attic only in subordinate clauses and in τί πάθω (§1810).
- Trusting a "must" that the other grammars qualify. Smyth's rule is that conditional, relative and temporal clauses requiring the subjunctive must have ἄν (§1768); Goodwin's is that it is regularly so used (GMT §200); Gildersleeve's is that such clauses regularly take ἄν except in the older language, with omissions from euphony or survival (Gildersleeve §456); and Smyth himself records that ἄν is more often absent in general conditions than in vivid future conditions (§2339). Read the strong form as Attic prose and the weak form as everything else. `topics/conditional-sentences.md` lists the protases where this applies.
- Deciding a Homeric subjunctive is deliberative because it is a question. Monro reads the interrogative subjunctive in Homer as expressing necessity, submission to some command or power, as in πῇ γὰρ ἐγώ, φίλε τέκνον, ἴω; where am I to go? (Monro §277). Smyth reaches the same reading for τί πάθω; in Attic, where the subjunctive is not deliberative but refers to a future event (§1811).
- Assuming the deliberative subjunctive has no substitute. Deliberation in the past is expressed by ἔδει, χρῆν (ἐχρῆν), ἔμελλον with the infinitive, and by the verbal adjective in -τέον with ἦν (§1808), and in the present it may be replaced by a periphrasis with δεῖ or χρή and the infinitive or by the verbal adjective in -τέον ἐστί (§1807). Gildersleeve lists the same substitutes and adds the future (Gildersleeve §383).
- Arguing from the mood's name to its origin. The theories do not agree. Smyth leaves the question open, saying whether the anticipatory is a form of the voluntative is uncertain (§1796). Gildersleeve argues the other way: in certain spheres of early Greek the subjunctive appears as a future with the negative of denial, οὐ, whence the theory that it was originally a simple future, but it is easier to make the futural subjunctive a deadened imperative than to get the imperative notion out of a simple future of prediction, and the fact that μή is the native negative seems decisive (Gildersleeve §371). Monro declines to choose and describes the mood as intermediate between an imperative and a future (Monro §274). The page reports all three; it does not decide.
