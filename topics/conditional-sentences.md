# Conditional Sentences

Status: drafted 2026-09-07 on the pattern of `topics/genitive-absolute.md`.
Every claim carries a section citation; `scripts/verify_topic_pages.py`
checks them. `topic: conditional` names the derived record in
`data/smyth/topics.json`.

## 1. Quick answer

A condition is a supposition on which a statement is based, and a conditional sentence commonly consists of a protasis, the conditional clause expressing the supposed case, and an apodosis, the principal clause expressing what follows if the condition is realized (§2280). The protasis is introduced by εἰ; with the subjunctive εἰ commonly takes ἄν, which yields the three forms ἐᾱ́ν, ἤν, ᾱ̓́ν (§2282, §2283). In the apodosis ἄν goes with the optative to denote possibility, and with the past tenses of the indicative to denote either the non-fulfilment of the condition or, occasionally, repetition (§2284). The negative of the protasis is μή, and of the apodosis οὐ (§2286). Smyth classifies the ordinary types primarily according to time, and his table gives the simple, the unreal and the general form for present and for past time, and for future time the more vivid, the emotional and the less vivid (§2297). The form of the apodosis alone distinguishes the special kind of general condition from the two forms of future conditions (§2336).

## 2. Recognition

- εἰ with the present, perfect, imperfect, aorist or pluperfect indicative, and any form of the simple sentence in the apodosis: a simple present or past condition, stating a supposition with no implication as to its reality or probability (§2298).
- εἰ with the imperfect and ἄν with the imperfect, or εἰ with the aorist and ἄν with the aorist: a present or past unreal condition, εἰ ταῦτα ἐποίεις, καλῶς ἂν ἐποίεις and εἰ ταῦτα ἐποίησας, καλῶς ἂν ἐποίησας (§2304, §2305).
- ἐᾱ́ν (ἤν, ᾱ̓́ν) with the subjunctive and, in the apodosis, the future indicative or any other form referring to future time: a more vivid future condition, ἐᾱ̀ν ταῦτα ποιῇς, καλῶς ποιήσεις (§2323).
- ἐᾱ́ν with the subjunctive and the present indicative in the apodosis: a present general condition, σὲ ἐπαινῶ "I always praise you" (§2337). A gnomic aorist in the apodosis is equivalent to a present indicative there (§2338).
- εἰ with the optative and ἄν with the optative: a less vivid future condition, εἰ πρᾱ́σσοις καλῶς (§2329).
- εἰ with the optative and the imperfect indicative in the apodosis: a past general condition, εἴ πού τι ὁρῴη βρωτόν, διεδίδου "if ever he saw anything to eat anywhere, he always distributed it" (§2340). An iterative imperfect or aorist with ἄν belongs here too: ἐπὶ τὴν ὑπόθεσιν ἐπανῆγεν ἂν πάντα τὸν λόγον (§2341).
- εἰ with the future indicative: an emotional future condition, εἰ ταῦτα λέξεις "if thou speakest thus" (§2328); or, where a present intention or necessity is meant, a present condition, εἰ μαχεῖ "if you mean (are going) to fight" (§2301).
- No εἰ at all. A participle, often in the genitive absolute, an adverb, a prepositional phrase, a relative clause, or some other single word or phrase may replace the protasis: δίκης οὔσης for εἰ δίκη ἐστίν (§2344). The two members may also be simply coördinated, the protasis having the form of an independent clause (§2288).
- ἄν standing alone in the apodosis, the verb being omitted, especially when the protasis has the same verb (§2351).
- The fixed phrases: εἰ μή "except", εἰ μὴ ὀλίγους τούτους ἀνθρώπους (§2346); εἰ μὴ διά "except for", εἰ δὲ μή "otherwise", πολεμήσειν ἔφη αὐτοῖς (§2346).
- A relative or temporal clause doing the work of the protasis, ὅς (ὅστις) answering to εἴ τις and ὅς ἄν to ἐᾱ́ν τις, with μή for its negative (§2560).
- Concessive καὶ εἰ (κεἰ), καὶ ἐᾱ́ν (κᾱ̓́ν), εἰ καί, ἐᾱ̀ν καί (§2369).
- In Homer, εἴ κε or εἴ κεν rather than ἐᾱ́ν (§2283); εἰ alone with the subjunctive, εἴ περ γάρ σε κατακτάνῃ (§2327); εἴ κε with the optative, οὐδ᾽ εἴ κεν τοῦ πατρὸς ἀποφθιμένοιο πυθοίμην (§2334).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Cut the sentence in two. Find εἰ, ἐᾱ́ν, ἤν or ᾱ̓́ν (§2282, §2283) and take the clause it heads as the protasis; the rest is the apodosis (§2280). The protasis usually precedes, but may follow, the apodosis (§2281).
2. Read the apodosis before you name the condition. The form of the apodosis alone distinguishes the special kind of general condition from the two forms of future conditions (§2336), and the same ἐᾱ̀ν ταῦτά σοι δοκῇ, ποίει may be particular or general (§2324). GMT states it the same way: in external form the general present condition coincides with the more vivid future condition, both being expressed by ἐάν and the subjunctive (GMT §397).
3. Look for ἄν in the apodosis and read what it is doing there. With the optative it denotes possibility; with the past tenses of the indicative it denotes either the non-fulfilment of the condition or, occasionally, repetition (§2284).
4. If the protasis has εἰ with a past tense of the indicative and the apodosis has ἄν with a past tense, the condition is unreal (§2303). Then read the tenses for time: the imperfect refers to present time or sometimes to a continued or habitual past act (§2304), the aorist to a simple occurrence in the past (§2305), the rare pluperfect to an act completed in past or present time (§2306). Protasis and apodosis may have different tenses, and the six combinations are set out with examples (§2310). There is nothing in the form of either clause that denotes unreality; in the combination the unreality of the protasis is always, and that of the apodosis generally, implied (§2308). GMT gives the same tense rule and says the context must decide, in each case, to which time the imperfect refers (GMT §410).
5. Before you conclude that a sentence is not unreal because ἄν is missing, check the four exceptions. ἄν may be omitted when the apodosis is an imperfect indicative denoting unfulfilled obligation, possibility or propriety, such as ἔδει, χρῆν, ἐξῆν, εἰκὸς ἦν, καλὸν ἦν with the infinitive (§2313). It is regularly omitted in an apodosis formed by the imperfect of μέλλω and the infinitive, denoting an unfulfilled past intention (§2318). It may be omitted with the aorist of κινδῡνεύω when the emphasis falls on the dependent infinitive (§2319). And some expressions containing a secondary tense of the indicative without ἄν, and not followed by a dependent infinitive, are virtually equivalent to the apodosis of an unreal condition (§2320). That last limitation distinguishes these from the impersonals, where ἔδει and ἐξῆν are auxiliaries and the emphasis is on the infinitive (§2313 a). GMT treats the first of these as a potential indicative without ἄν and glosses ἔδει τοῦτον ἀποθανεῖν as "he ought to have perished (but did not)" (GMT §415), noting that these are originally expressions of past necessity involving no reference to any condition (GMT §417). Gildersleeve gives the reason the idiom exists: Greek expresses possibility, power, obligation and necessity as facts, whereas our translation often implies the failure to realize, as in ἃ ἔδει αὐτὸν ἀπολῦσαι (Gildersleeve §363).
6. With those impersonals, ἄν shows what is denied. With ἄν it is implied that the obligation does or did not exist; without ἄν it is implied that the action of the dependent infinitive is or was not realized (§2316).
7. If the protasis has ἐᾱ́ν with the subjunctive, go back to the apodosis; there are more than two possibilities. The future indicative or any other form referring to future time gives a more vivid future condition (§2323); a present indicative or its equivalent gives a present general condition (§2337), and a gnomic aorist counts as a present indicative there (§2338). Neither name fits every ἐᾱ́ν clause. ἐᾱ́ν may instead set forth the motive for the apodosis, on the chance that, in the hope that: ἄκουσον καὶ ἐμοῦ, ἐᾱ́ν σοι ἔτι ταὐτὰ δοκῇ (§2354). A subjunctive clause of that shape may also be implied indirect discourse, ἐπιβουλεύουσιν ἐξελθεῖν, ἢν δύνωνται βιάσασθαι, where the direct form was ἐξέλθωμεν, ἢν δυνώμεθα (§2354 a; §2622 e).
8. If the protasis has εἰ with the optative, go back to the apodosis again; more than one possibility remains. ἄν with the optative gives a less vivid future condition (§2329); the imperfect indicative or an equivalent gives a past general condition (§2340). The optative refers distinctly to the past only in this past general use, and when the optative in indirect discourse represents a past indicative (§2336). But an imperfect apodosis is not by itself proof of a past general condition: πρὸς τὴν πόλιν, εἰ ἐπιβοηθοῖεν, ἐχώρουν is εἰ with the optative on the chance that the citizens should make a sally (§2354), and GMT files that very sentence under clauses containing a protasis whose apodosis is implied in the past leading verb, which is implied indirect discourse (GMT §696). Smyth files the same kind of εἰ clause there too, τὰ χρήματα τῷ δήμῳ ἔδωκεν, εἴ πως τελευτήσειεν ἄπαις (§2622 a). And εἰ with the optative is not infrequent with a primary tense of the indicative, a subjunctive, or an imperative in the apodosis (§2359).
9. Do not convert the two future forms into degrees of probability. The difference depends on the mental attitude of the speaker, and is not an inherent difference between probable realization in the one case and possible realization in the other; the same thought may often be expressed in either form without any essential difference in meaning (§2322). Impossibilities may be expressed by ἐᾱ́ν with the subjunctive (§2322 c), and anything physically impossible may be represented as supposable by εἰ with the optative (§2329 a). GMT agrees that the distinction between the two is less marked and that it is sometimes of slight importance which is used (GMT §456).
10. If the protasis has εἰ with the future indicative, decide between two readings. When the protasis expresses strong feeling, the future indicative with εἰ is commonly used instead of ἐᾱ́ν with the subjunctive; the protasis commonly suggests something undesired or feared, and the apodosis commonly conveys a threat, a warning, or an earnest appeal to the feelings (§2328). But if the protasis expresses a present intention or necessity, the future has a modal force, is equivalent to μέλλεις μαχεῖσθαι, and is not equivalent to ἐᾱ̀ν μάχῃ (§2301). GMT makes the same point: such a future indicative could not be changed to a subjunctive with ἐάν without an entire change of sense and time (GMT §407, GMT §408).
11. If the clause with εἰ or ἐᾱ́ν is not a condition, test the other uses. εἰ or ἐᾱ́ν may set forth the motive for the action or feeling expressed by the apodosis, with the force of on the chance that, in case that, in the hope that: εἴ πως πείσειαν αὐτόν (§2354). εἰ or εἴπερ, when it expresses the real opinion of the writer or speaker, may have a causal force (§2246). Goodell records the same two uses and adds two more: after expressions of wonder or indignation εἰ with the indicative describes, in the milder form of a supposition, the fact that causes the emotion (Goodell §654), and after expressions of doubt or inquiry εἰ becomes an indirect question (Goodell §655).
12. If there is no εἰ, look for the protasis substitute before deciding the sentence is defective. The substitutes are listed at §2344, and the tense rule there needs care: Smyth writes that the present participle represents the imperfect and the perfect the pluperfect, yet his own first example, δίκης οὔσης for εἰ δίκη ἐστίν, stands for a present, so take the tense from the context (§2344). Sometimes the protasis has to be supplied from what precedes, or from a main clause with ἀλλά which follows (§2344 a); sometimes the verb of the protasis is omitted because the apodosis has the same verb (§2345); and with the potential optative or the indicative with ἄν in an independent sentence a protasis may be supplied from the context, or there may have been no conscious ellipsis at all (§2349). GMT states it in the form that does not fail, that the tense of the participle is always the one the verb itself would have stood in, with the present including the imperfect, and adds that the future participle is not used to represent the future indicative in future conditions (GMT §472, GMT §473).
13. If the apodosis is missing instead, read the standard cases: the conclusion is omitted when it is "it is well" or the like, or when English would say "know that" (§2352), and the apodosis may be expressed in a participle or an infinitive with or without ἄν as the construction requires (§2350). GMT collects the same ellipses, including ἄν without a verb standing for a whole apodosis (GMT §479, GMT §483).
14. If a relative or temporal clause has conditional force, resolve it. Conditional relative clauses may be resolved into if clauses, ὅς (ὅστις) corresponding to εἴ τις and ὅς ἄν to ἐᾱ́ν τις, with μή for the negative (§2560), and the correspondence with conditional and temporal sentences is set out form by form, τι ἔχει δίδωσι against ἐᾱ́ν τι ἔχῃ δίδωσι (§2561). The six forms answer one for one to the six conditional forms. Simple present and past conditional relative clauses have the present or past indicative (§2562). Present and past unreal conditional relative clauses have a secondary tense of the indicative, with a secondary tense and ἄν in the main clause (§2564). Those that vividly anticipate a future event take the subjunctive with ἄν (§2565); those that set it forth less vividly take the optative (§2566). Present general conditional relative clauses have ἄν with the subjunctive and a present indicative main clause (§2567), and past general ones the optative with an imperfect main clause (§2568).
15. If the mood of a relative or temporal clause inside the sentence looks wrong, suspect assimilation. A subordinate clause takes a past tense of the indicative in dependence on a past tense denoting unreality (§2185); when an optative of the principal clause refers to future time, a conditional relative clause regularly takes the optative by assimilation (§2186); and a conditional relative or temporal clause referring to future or general present time takes the subjunctive if it depends on a subjunctive (§2188).
16. Do not require protasis and apodosis to match a table. Greek shows many other combinations expressing distinct shades of feeling, most of them no less regular than the ordinary forms, and departure from the ordinary type is not to be counted as violation of rule (§2355). The potential optative may answer a simple present or past protasis, an emotional future protasis, a less vivid future protasis, or a subjunctive protasis (§2356), and as the apodosis of an unreal protasis it is Homeric, for in Attic such combinations are rare and some are suspected, the sentence being better read as a simple condition with a potential optative (§2312); the subjunctive of exhortation, the optative of wish and the imperative may all serve as apodosis (§2357); the unreal indicative may follow a simple present protasis (§2358); and εἰ with the optative is not infrequent with a primary tense of the indicative, a subjunctive, or an imperative in the apodosis (§2359). The same period may show different forms of conditional sentences according to the exigency of the thought (§2343), and one sentence may carry several protases or several apodoses (§2366, §2368).
17. If ἄν stands inside the protasis with εἰ, it is not out of place. The potential optative with ἄν or the unreal indicative with ἄν standing in the clause with εἰ is itself the apodosis of another protasis expressed or understood, and such clauses form simple present conditions, "if it is true that we would accept" (§2353). GMT reads them the same way, but keeps the two apart: a present condition may be expressed by a potential optative in the protasis, and a present or a past condition by a potential indicative (GMT §409, GMT §458).
18. Confirm with the negative. μή negatives the conditional clause as a whole (§2286). οὐ is sometimes found in clauses introduced by εἰ (ἐᾱ́ν), in seven cases: adherescent οὐ, an emphatic assertion of fact or probability, εἰ used instead of ὅτι after verbs of emotion, εἰ approaching the idea of ἐπεί, a bimembered protasis introduced by a single εἰ whose μέν clause and δέ clause may have οὐ, a bimembered clause holding a negative clause directly opposed to a positive one, εἰ δὲ τῷ μέν, τοῖς δ᾽ οὔ, and εἰ whether in simple and alternative indirect questions, which takes either οὐ or μή (§2698).
19. If the condition is inside indirect discourse, undo that first. After a secondary tense all subordinate verbs in the present, future or perfect indicative, and all subjunctives, are usually changed to the corresponding tenses of the optative or are retained, and subjunctives with ἄν lose ἄν, so ἐᾱ́ν becomes εἰ (§2619). Smyth's table at §2621 then shows where, after εἶπεν ὅτι or ἔφη, the optative and the infinitive may be substituted for the indicative or subjunctive in conditional sentences in indirect discourse. GMT warns that an optative in a protasis after a past tense may merely represent the same tense of the subjunctive or indicative of the direct discourse (GMT §457).
20. If the passage is Homer, do not apply the Attic table. In Homer the imperfect in unreal conditions refers only to past time, and the apodosis may have κέ or ἄν with the optative: καί νύ κεν ἔνθ᾽ ἀπόλοιτο (§2311). εἰ alone with the subjunctive occurs with no appreciable difference from εἴ κε (§2327), and εἴ κε with the optative has the same force as εἰ alone (§2334). GMT sets out the whole Homeric range of forms in protasis and apodosis together (GMT §399). Monro adds that the pure subjunctive with εἰ is used in general sayings and in similes (Monro §292), that the apodosis to an optative protasis is generally given by the optative with κεν (Monro §311), and that a past tense with κεν or ἄν naturally came to be used where the event in question had not happened (Monro §324).

## 4. Translation guidance

- Simple present and past: εἰ ταῦτα ποιεῖς, καλῶς ποιεῖς "if you do this, you do well" (§2298). Nothing is implied about the truth of the supposition; the truth of the conclusion depends solely on the truth of the condition (§2298 a). GMT puts the English side by side with the Greek for every tense of this form (GMT §390, GMT §402).
- The simple condition is used even where the writer knows the supposition is physically impossible, and even where he holds it to be true, in which case εἴπερ is often used for εἰ (§2298 b). GMT allows the same latitude: this form of condition may be used even when the supposition is notoriously contrary to fact, if the speaker does not wish to imply this by the construction (GMT §404). Goodell does not go that far with εἴπερ: he puts εἴπερ γε Δᾱρείου ἐστὶ παῖς under the simple or logical condition, which presents the supposition simply, without implying anything as to its reality or probability. His remark that the case is not merely assumed but real belongs to another example, φήσουσι δή με σοφὸν εἶναι, εἰ καὶ μή εἰμι, where καί suggests it (Goodell §647).
- Unreal present: "if you were now doing this, you would be doing well". Unreal past with the aorist: "if you had done this, you would have done well" (§2304, §2305). The implied opposite shows which: for the imperfect it is a present or an imperfect, for the aorist an aorist, for the pluperfect a perfect or a pluperfect (§2304, §2305, §2306). GMT states the implied opposites as a rule (GMT §413).
- Do not add a denial of the apodosis that the Greek does not carry. Denial of the apodosis is not an essential character of the construction, as appears once the apodosis is itself negative (GMT §412).
- More vivid future: ἐᾱ̀ν ταῦτα ποιῇς, καλῶς ποιήσεις "if you do this, you will do well" (§2323). The form corresponds to shall and will in older English, "if ye shall ask ... I will do it", while modern English substitutes the present (§2324). GMT gives Ἐάν τι λάβω, δώσω σοι, "if I shall receive anything, I will give it to you" (GMT §444).
- Less vivid future: "should ... would" (§2329). English would is ambiguous, used both for ἄν with the optative and for ἄν with the past indicative, and "if I were" may translate both εἰ with the optative and εἰ with the past indicative (§2332). GMT gives the pair Εἰ ἔλθοι, πάντ᾽ ἂν ἴδοι against the unreal form and warns that we often translate both εἴη ἄν and ἦν ἄν by the same English (GMT §455, GMT §456).
- Present general: "if ever you do this, I always praise you", the conclusion holding true of any time or of all time (§2337). Past general: "if ever you did this, I always praised you" (§2340). GMT defines a general supposition as one referring indefinitely to any act or acts of a given class which may be supposed to occur or to have occurred at any time (GMT §394), and notes that only in present and past conditions which do not imply non-fulfilment does Greek distinguish general from particular in construction (GMT §395). Its examples use "if ever" and "always" in the English: ἢν ἐγγὺς ἔλθῃ θάνατος, οὐδεὶς βούλεται θνῄσκειν (GMT §462). The gnomic aorist and other iterative expressions may stand in the apodosis (GMT §466).
- Greek may also put a general condition in the indicative, and it is still a general condition in English. The protasis usually has εἴ τις, εἴ τι with the indicative, and the apodosis the present or the imperfect (§2342). GMT explains the form as stating the general supposition as if it were particular (GMT §467), and observes that Latin and English commonly do not recognise the distinction at all (GMT §396).
- Emotional future: "if thou speakest thus, thou wilt be hated by me", and the εἰ may often be rendered by shall (§2328). When εἰ with the future is directly contrasted with ἐᾱ́ν with the subjunctive, the former usually presents the unfavourable alternative (§2328 a). GMT calls it the stronger form of expression, especially common when the condition contains a strong appeal to the feelings or a threat or warning (GMT §447), and Goodell labels it the Emotional Future Condition (Goodell §648).
- ἔδει, χρῆν and the rest without ἄν: "one ought to blame him", with the action of the infinitive understood not to have taken place (§2313). The present infinitive there generally expresses what would necessarily, possibly, or properly be done now; what would have been so done in the past is expressed by the aorist infinitive, and sometimes by the present, so that §2314 b is headed present infinitive of past time (§2314).
- εἰ μή is "except", εἰ μὴ διά is "except for", and εἰ δὲ μή is "otherwise" and introduces a supposition opposed to something just said (§2346). εἰ δὲ μή often occurs even where the preceding clause is negative and we expect εἰ δέ (§2346 d). GMT confirms that εἰ δὲ μή is much more common here than ἐὰν δὲ μή, even when ἐὰν μέν with the subjunctive precedes (GMT §478), and Goodell notes the same (Goodell §656).
- Conditional relatives will not always take an English relative. English cannot always, without obscurity, use a relative to translate ὅτε or ὅ τι with an unreal indicative, and there "whenever" or "whatever" are best rendered by "if ever" (§2561).
- εἰ or ἐᾱ́ν of motive: "on the chance that", "in case that", "in the hope that", "if haply" (§2354). Goodell translates the same construction "on the chance that, in the hope that, if perchance" (Goodell §652).
- Goodell summarises: what is stated not as a fact but as a supposition, assumed in order to base upon it another statement, is a condition, and English marks it not only by if and unless but by inversion, "Were I Brutus, or Should you ask me" (Goodell §645).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading ἐᾱ́ν with the subjunctive as always future. It is equally the present general condition (§2337), and only the apodosis shows which (§2336).
- Reading εἰ with the optative as always "should". It is equally the past general condition, "if ever" (§2340), and the optative refers distinctly to the past only there (§2336).
- Turning the less vivid future into a weaker claim about probability (§2322). GMT observes that it is often nearly indifferent in English whether we say if we shall go it will be well or if we should go it would be well (GMT §456).
- Translating an unreal imperfect as past when it is present, or the reverse. The imperfect refers to present time or to a continued or habitual past act, and only the context shows which (§2304; GMT §410).
- Concluding that a sentence is not unreal because the apodosis has no ἄν (§2313, §2318, §2319, §2320).
- Treating ἔδει ἄν and ἔδει alike. With ἄν the obligation is denied; without ἄν the action of the infinitive is denied (§2316).
- Taking an iterative imperfect or aorist with ἄν for the apodosis of an unreal condition. Do not confuse these cases with unreal apodoses (§2341).
- Rewriting εἰ with the future indicative as ἐᾱ́ν with the subjunctive. The emotional future has its own force (§2328), and the future of present intention cannot be so changed at all (§2301; GMT §408).
- Reading the aorist subjunctive in a protasis as past. Neither tense of the subjunctive has any time of itself, and the aorist subjunctive may mark the action of the protasis as completed before the action of the principal clause (§2325).
- Looking for a fulfilled-condition form. Greek has no special forms to show that an action is or was fulfilled, however clearly the context may imply it (§2292). GMT says the same and adds that it is hardly conceivable that any language should find such a form necessary (GMT §391).
- Deciding a sentence has no condition because it has no εἰ. The protasis may be a participle, an adverb, a prepositional phrase, a relative clause, or a single word (§2344), or the two members may simply be coördinated (§2288).
- Emending a sentence whose protasis and apodosis do not match the table. Most of these combinations are no less regular (§2355).
- Taking εἰ with ἄν inside the protasis as an error (§2353; GMT §409).
- Reading every εἰ as conditional. εἰ or εἴπερ, when it expresses the real opinion of the writer or speaker, may have a causal force (§2246); it may set forth the motive for the action, on the chance that (§2354); it may stand after a verb of emotion (§2698); or it may introduce an indirect question (Goodell §655).
- Treating a conditional relative clause as an ordinary one. The antecedent of conditional relative clauses is indefinite, and ὃς ἄν is always generic (§2560).
- Supplying ἄν in a Homeric protasis. εἰ alone with the subjunctive occurs with no appreciable difference from εἴ κε (§2327), and Monro finds the pure subjunctive in general sayings and similes (Monro §292).
- Reading Homeric unreal conditions by the Attic rule. There the imperfect refers only to past time, and the apodosis may have κέ or ἄν with the optative (§2311).
- Forgetting that the future optative is not used in conditional sentences except in indirect discourse (§2287, §2331).
- Reading an optative protasis inside reported speech as a real optative. It may only represent the subjunctive or indicative of the direct form (GMT §457; §2619).
