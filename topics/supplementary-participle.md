# Supplementary Participle

Status: drafted 2026-09-07 on the pattern of `topics/genitive-absolute.md`
and `topics/result-clauses.md`. Every claim carries a section citation;
`scripts/verify_topic_pages.py` checks them. `topic: supplementary-participle`
names the derived record in `data/smyth/topics.json`.

One section on this page is cited inside Smyth's syntax more often than any other. Eighteen sections of Part IV Syntax cite §2106, on the supplementary participle in indirect discourse, and no other section in the book has more citations from Part IV. The next most cited is §1392, the genitive of separation, with nine. The counts are the ones recorded in `data/smyth/topics.json`, and they are why a translator meets this construction more often than its two pages of Smyth would suggest.

## 1. Quick answer

The supplementary participle completes the idea of the verb by showing that to which its action relates (§2088). It agrees with the subject of the main verb when that verb is intransitive or passive, and with the object when the verb is transitive: οὔποτ᾽ ἐπαυόμην ἡμᾶς οἰκτῑ́ρων "I never ceased pitying ourselves", τοὺς πένητας ἔπαυσ᾽ ἀδικουμένους "I put a stop to the poor being wronged" (§2089). Smyth divides its uses into two, not in indirect discourse and in indirect discourse (§2093). In the second, the participle represents a dependent statement, so that ἤκουσε Κῦρον ἐν Κιλικίᾳ ὄντα "he heard that Cyrus was in Cilicia" has ὄντα standing for ἐστί, and the sentence might have run ἤκουσεν ὅτι Κῦρος ἐν Κιλικίᾳ εἴη; with verbs not introducing indirect discourse there is no such statement, as in ἐπαύσαντο μαχόμενοι "they ceased fighting" (§2092). Which of the two you have decides how the tense is read, and the leading verb decides which of the two you have.

## 2. Recognition

- A participle that cannot be removed without leaving the construction incomplete. The attributive and circumstantial participles are commonly not necessary to the construction, but the removal of a supplementary participle may make the construction incomplete (§2048).
- τυγχάνω (poetic κυρῶ), λανθάνω and φθάνω, the verbs denoting being in some modified way (§2095), where the participle contains the main idea and is often represented in translation by the finite verb with an adverbial phrase: παρὼν ἐτύγχανε "he happened to be there" (§2096 a).
- Verbs of continuing: διάγω, διαγίγνομαι, διατελῶ, διαμένω. διάγουσι μανθάνοντες "they are continually (they spend their time in) learning"; διατελεῖ μῑσῶν "he continues to hate" (§2097).
- Verbs signifying to begin, cease, endure, grow weary of an action: παῦσαι λέγουσα "stop talking"; μὴ κάμῃς φίλον ἄνδρα εὐεργετῶν "do not grow weary of doing good to your friend" (§2098).
- Verbs of coming and going, where the participle specifies the manner and contains the main idea: οἴχονται διώκοντες "they have gone in pursuit"; οἴχεται θανών "he is dead and gone" (§2099).
- Verbs of emotion, where the participle often denotes cause: χαίρω διαλεγόμενος τοῖς σφόδρα πρεσβῡ́ταις "I like to converse with very old men"; οὐ γὰρ αἰσχῡ́νομαι μανθάνων "for I am not ashamed to learn" (§2100).
- Verbs signifying to do well or ill, to surpass or be inferior, where the participle specifies that in which the action consists: εὖ γ᾽ ἐποίησας ἀναμνήσᾱς με "you did well in reminding me"; ἀδικεῖτε πολέμου ἄρχοντες "you do wrong in being the aggressors in the war" (§2101).
- περιορῶ and similar verbs, signifying overlook, allow: μείζω γιγνόμενον τὸν ἄνθρωπον περιορῶμεν "we allow the man to grow greater" (§2103).
- Verbs of knowing and showing, which take the participle in indirect discourse: οὐ γὰρ ᾔδεσαν αὐτὸν τεθνηκότα "for they did not know that he was dead"; ἔγνω τὴν ἐσβολὴν ἐσομένην "he knew that the invasion would take place" (§2106). Smyth lists them from οἶδα, γιγνώσκω and ἐπίσταμαι through μέμνημαι, δηλῶ and φαίνομαι to ἀγγέλλω (§2106).
- The personal constructions δῆλός εἰμι and φανερός εἰμι: δῆλος ἦν οἰόμενος "it was clear that he thought" (§2107).
- σύνοιδα or συγγιγνώσκω with the dative of the reflexive: ἐμαυτῷ ξυνῄδη οὐδὲν ἐπισταμένῳ "I was conscious of knowing nothing" (§2108).
- Verbs of perception, ὁρῶ, αἰσθάνομαι, ἀκούω, πυνθάνομαι (§2111), in either of the two constructions (§2112).
- Verbs of finding and detecting, εὑρίσκω, (κατα)λαμβάνω, and the passive ἁλίσκομαι (§2113).
- ὡς standing before the participle in indirect discourse, marking the mental attitude of the subject of the main verb: ὡς μηδὲν εἰδότ᾽ ἴσθι με "be assured that I know nothing" (§2120).
- A predicate adjective or substantive with no participle at all, where ὤν is understood: ὁρῶ μέγαν (ὄντα) τὸν ἀγῶνα "I see that the contest is important" (§2119).
- Goodell sorts the same material by what the participle agrees with: a predicate participle agreeing with the subject, with verbs that express existence in some particular way and with verbs meaning begin, cease, endure, grow weary (Goodell §585), and one agreeing with the object, with verbs meaning perceive, remember, know and with verbs meaning show, make known (Goodell §586); with some verbs it may agree with either (Goodell §587).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. First decide that the participle is supplementary at all. The participle has three main uses, attributive, circumstantial and supplementary (§2046), and the circumstantial and supplementary participles are both predicate participles (§2047). The test is to remove the participle: an attributive or circumstantial participle is commonly not necessary to the construction, while removing a supplementary participle may leave the construction incomplete (§2048).
2. Accept that the test does not always answer. A participle may be both circumstantial and supplementary, as ἀδικούμενοι ὀργίζονται "they are enraged at being wronged" or "because (when, if) they are wronged", and circumstantial and supplementary participles often cannot be sharply distinguished, particularly with verbs signifying to be angry, ashamed, content, pleased, inferior to, do wrong, endure, and come and go (§2048). GMT reaches the same verdict from the other side, filing most of the participles of emotion under the circumstantial participle of cause as well (GMT §882).
3. Where the two overlap, let the English carry both. ἀδικῶ ταῦτα ποιῶν is "I do wrong in doing this" or "I am guilty in doing this", and in the first rendering ταῦτα ποιῶν is appositive to the subject (§2048).
4. Find what the participle agrees with. It agrees with the subject when the verb is intransitive or passive, and with the object when the verb is transitive (§2089). When the object is the same as the subject it is commonly suppressed and the participle agrees with the subject, as in ὁρῶ ἐξαμαρτάνων "I see that I err" (§2089 a); for emphasis or contrast the object may be expressed by the reflexive pronoun, as in οἶδα ἐμαυτὸν δικαίως κεχρημένον αὐτοῖς "I know that I have presented my case honestly" (§2089 b). GMT states that when one of these verbs has for its object an accusative of the reflexive referring to its subject the participle generally agrees with the reflexive, so that we may have either δείξω ἐμαυτὸν τοῦτο πεποιηκότα or δείξω τοῦτο πεποιηκώς (GMT §905).
5. Now settle the second question, whether the participle is in indirect discourse. The substitution test is Smyth's own. If the participle represents a dependent statement, the sentence could have been written with ὅτι and a finite verb according to the principles of indirect discourse; if the verb does not introduce indirect discourse, there is no such statement (§2092). From that point of view the uses of the supplementary participle are two (§2093).
6. Read the tense according to the answer. Not in indirect discourse, the supplementary participle is often like an object infinitive, and the tenses denote only stage of action and not difference of time (§2094). In indirect discourse each tense has the same force as the corresponding tense of the indicative or optative with ὅτι or ὡς, the present including also the imperfect and the perfect including also the pluperfect (§2106). Smyth spells that out tense by tense: present for present indicative, future for future, aorist for aorist, perfect for perfect (§1874). GMT states the same correspondence, that each tense of the participle represents the corresponding tense of the indicative or optative (GMT §904).
7. If the leading verb is ἀκούω or πυνθάνομαι, let the case of the participle decide. Not in indirect discourse, of physical perception, the participle stands in the genitive with those two verbs, and usually in the accusative with αἰσθάνομαι, as with ὁρῶ (§2112 a). In indirect discourse, of intellectual perception, the participle stands in the accusative with ἀκούω and πυνθάνομαι as it does with ὁρῶ and αἰσθάνομαι (§2112 b). GMT states the rule as a diagnostic: with some of these verbs the construction of the participle is generally shown by its case, ἀκούω and πυνθάνομαι in Attic Greek regularly taking the genitive in the one construction and the accusative in indirect discourse, while other verbs, as ὁρῶ, have the accusative regularly in both constructions, where the context generally makes the meaning plain (GMT §886). Goodell adds the third member: ἀκούω τινός with a participle is I hear with my own ears something going on, ἀκούω τι with a participle is I hear that, and ἀκούω with the accusative and infinitive is I hear as report or tradition that (Goodell §588).
8. Do not expect a clean answer with verbs of finding. Verbs of finding and detecting take the participle in either construction, of the act or state in which a person or thing is found, or of the fact that it is found in that act or state (§2113). It is often difficult to distinguish the two, and καταλαμβάνουσι νεωστὶ στάσει τοὺς τῶν Ἀθηναίων ἐναντίους ἐκπεπτωκότας may mean either that they found that the anti-Athenian party had been recently expelled, or that they found them recently expelled (§2114). Smyth allows the same indecision in general: it is sometimes impossible to decide whether a participle stands in indirect discourse or not, and after verbs of perceiving the difference may be of no great importance to the sense (§2093 a).
9. If ὡς stands before the participle, read it as a report of someone's thought and not as a comparison. ὡς with a participle in indirect discourse marks the mental attitude of the subject of the main verb or of some other person mentioned prominently in the sentence, and sometimes only adds emphasis when that attitude is already clear (§2120). A participle with ὡς may follow a verb of thinking or saying that does not take the participle in indirect discourse without ὡς (§2121), and the genitive or accusative absolute with ὡς may stand in the same place: ὡς πολέμου ὄντος παρ᾽ ῡ̔μῶν ἀπαγγελῶ; "shall I report from you (on the assumption) that there is war?" (§2122). GMT works through the same three steps, the participle with ὡς (GMT §916), the absolute participle with ὡς in its place (GMT §917), and the extension of that absolute to verbs that would not take the participle at all (GMT §918), adding a fourth class of verbs of saying and thinking where the construction shades back into the circumstantial participle (GMT §919). Goodell records the same use of ὡς with a predicate participle after verbs of knowing, thinking and saying, and notes that in some cases a circumstantial participle with ὡς stands where a predicate participle would be expected (Goodell §594).
10. If a predicate adjective or substantive stands where you expected a participle, supply ὤν. ὤν may be omitted with verbs taking a supplementary participle, so with verbs meaning to perceive, know, show, announce, find and discover, and especially with φαίνομαι, τυγχάνω, διατελῶ and διαγίγνομαι (§2119). GMT records the same omission for the constructions not in indirect discourse (GMT §902) and separately for indirect discourse (GMT §911).
11. If an infinitive stands where the participle might have stood, expect a change of meaning and go to Smyth's list. Some verbs admit either the supplementary participle or the infinitive, sometimes with only a slight difference (§2123), and the two differ greatly when the infinitive expresses purpose or result, but little when the infinitive shows only its abstract verbal meaning (§2124). The differences that matter are listed verb by verb (§2125), and the largest of them are these: αἰσχῡ́νομαι with the participle is I am ashamed of doing something which I do, with the infinitive I am ashamed to do something which I have refrained from doing (§2126); ἄρχομαι with the participle is begin to do something and continue with something else, with the infinitive begin and continue with the same thing (§2128); μανθάνω with the participle is learn that something is, with the infinitive learn how to do something (§2136); οἶδα and ἐπίσταμαι with the participle are know that something is, with the infinitive know how to do something (§2139); παύω with the participle is stop what is taking place, with the infinitive prevent something from taking place (§2140); and φαίνομαι with the participle is I am plainly, with the infinitive I seem or it appears, though that last distinction is not always maintained (§2143). Goodell prints the same distinctions in two columns, participle against infinitive, for φαίνομαι, ἄρχομαι, αἰδοῦμαι, γιγνώσκω, ἐπίσταμαι, οἶδα, μανθάνω, ἐπιλανθάνομαι and μέμνημαι (Goodell §588).
12. When neither the participle nor the infinitive settles anything, check whether the verb is one of those for which they do not differ. Smyth lists the verbs that take either with no difference or only a slight one, among them αἰσθάνομαι, ἀκούω, πυνθάνομαι, ἀγγέλλω, παρασκευάζομαι, ὁμολογῶ, πειρῶμαι, ἐπιτρέπω, νομίζω, θαυμάζω and τίθημι (§2144). Verbs of intellectual perception take ὅτι or ὡς as well (§2145). GMT agrees for the perception verbs, where the infinitive differs little if at all from the participle (GMT §914), and notes that the verbs of its indirect-discourse list may also be followed by a clause with ὅτι or ὡς (GMT §912).
13. If the verb is λανθάνω, τυγχάνω or φθάνω, do not read the aorist participle as past. The action of φθάνω and λανθάνω usually coincides with that of the participle, present with present and aorist with aorist, and with tenses other than the present and imperfect an aorist participle with these verbs refers to an action coincident in time (§2096 b). GMT states the coincidence as a rule, with English equivalents: ἔλαθον ἀπελθόντες is they went away secretly, οὐκ ἔφθησαν ἀπελθόντες is no sooner were they gone, ἔτυχον εἰσελθόντες is they came in by chance (GMT §144). With a present or imperfect of these verbs the rare aorist participle refers to an action or state anterior to that of the present or imperfect (§2096 b), which is why ἄριστα τυγχάνουσι πρᾱ́ξαντες is "they happen to have fared the best" (§2096 d); GMT prints the same example under the same rule (GMT §146). The perfect participle is the ordinary way of marking completion here (GMT §147).
14. If the leading verb is a verb of physical perception, expect the present participle whatever the English idiom wants. ὁρῶ especially and ἀκούω regularly take the present participle in Attic prose, which usually does not distinguish between I see a house burning and I see a house burn; the complexive aorist does occur (§2112 a). GMT reads the same aorist participles as marking the occurrence of the act against the present, which marks its progress (GMT §884).
15. If the construction is impersonal, or a dative appears where you expected a nominative, look at the class Smyth files last. The participle stands with some impersonal expressions taking the dative, such as those signifying the advantage or consequence of an action and those implying confidence or fear, though the personal construction is often preferred (§2104). GMT gives the same class its own heading and adds that the dative of any participle may be so used (GMT §901), and files beside it the datives βουλόμενος, ἡδόμενος, ἄσμενος, ἀχθόμενος and the rest that agree with a dative depending on εἰμί or γίγνομαι (GMT §900).
16. When the context still does not decide, say what the Greek is doing and let the ambiguity stand. Smyth twice writes the indecision into the rule itself, at §2093 a for indirect discourse and at §2048 for the boundary with the circumstantial participle. Neither is a defect in the analysis; both state that Greek did not make the distinction the analysis needs.

## 4. Translation guidance

- With τυγχάνω, λανθάνω and φθάνω, put the participle into the English main verb and the Greek verb into an adverb or an auxiliary: παρὼν ἐτύγχανε is "he happened to be there", or "he was there by chance" (§2096 a). τυγχάνω often loses the idea of chance and denotes mere coincidence in time, I am just now, I was just then, or simply I am (§2096 c).
- With verbs of continuing, English wants an adverb: διάγουσι μανθάνοντες "they are continually (they spend their time in) learning"; θρηνοῦντες διετελοῦμεν "we lamented continually" (§2097).
- With verbs of coming and going the participle is the verb: βῆ φεύγων "he took to flight (went fleeing)"; οὐ τοῦτο λέξων ἔρχομαι "I am not going to say this" (§2099).
- With verbs of emotion English usually wants at or a because clause. Smyth notes that verbs of emotion also take ὅτι or ὡς, by which construction the object is simply stated, while with the participle the connection is closer: οὐκ ἂν ἀχθοίμην μανθάνων "I should not be annoyed at learning" (§2100).
- With verbs of doing well or ill, English wants in doing: καλῶς ἐποίησεν οὕτως τελευτήσᾱς τὸν βίον "he did well in ending his life thus"; ὀνήσεσθε ἀκούοντες "you will profit by hearing" (§2101).
- With περιορῶ, the present participle is view with indifference and the aorist participle is shut one's eyes to, while the infinitive is let something happen through negligence or simply permit (§2141). Smyth's example carries both: περιεῖδε τὸν αὑτοῦ πατέρα καὶ ζῶντα τῶν ἀναγκαίων σπανίζοντα καὶ τελευτήσαντ᾽ οὐ τυχόντα τῶν νομίμων "he looked on with indifference while his own father was in want of necessities when alive and (shut his eyes) to his failure to receive the customary rites after he had passed away" (§2141).
- With verbs of knowing and showing, English wants a that clause, and the tense of the participle sets the tense of the English verb: οὐ γὰρ ᾔδεσαν αὐτὸν τεθνηκότα "for they did not know that he was dead" (§2106), κατέμαθε πόλεις ἕνδεκα ἢ δώδεκα ἔχουσαν "he learned that Chersonesus contained eleven or twelve cities" (§2106).
- The personal construction stays personal in Greek and turns impersonal in English: δῆλος ἦν οἰόμενος is "it was clear that he thought", and θῡ́ων φανερὸς ἦν πολλάκις is "it was evident that he often sacrificed" (§2107). GMT prefers the same personal Greek to an impersonal expression (GMT §907).
- With σύνοιδα the English is conscious that: συνειδὼς αὐτὸς αὑτῷ ἔργον εἰργασμένος "conscious (to himself) that he had done the deed" (§2108).
- Distinguish the two constructions of perception in English. εἶδε Κλέαρχον διελαύνοντα is "he saw Clearchus riding through", of the act seen (§2112 a); ὁρῶμεν πάντα ἀληθῆ ὄντα ἃ λέγετε is "we see that everything you say is true", of the fact perceived (§2112 b).
- With verbs of finding the English keeps the found in: κῆρυξ ἀφικόμενος ηὗρε τοὺς ἄνδρας διεφθαρμένους "the herald, on his arrival, found the men already put to death"; ἢν ἐπιβουλεύων ἁλίσκηται "if he be detected in plotting" (§2113 a).
- ὡς with the participle wants be assured, understand, on the assumption that: ὡς μηδὲν εἰδότ᾽ ἴσθι με "be assured that I know nothing"; δῆλος ἦν Κῦρος ὡς σπεύδων "Cyrus was plainly bent on haste" (§2120). GMT gives the difference between the two English versions: ἴσθι ταῦτα οὕτως ἔχοντα is know that this is so, ἴσθι ὡς ταῦτα ἔχοντα is know that (as you may assume) this is so (GMT §916).
- Keep Smyth's pair for φαίνομαι distinct in English: φαίνεται τᾱ̓ληθῆ λέγων is "he is evidently speaking the truth", φαίνεται τᾱ̓ληθῆ λέγειν is "he appears to be speaking the truth (but he may be lying)" (§2143).
- Keep the pair for παύω distinct too: ἔπαυσαν φοβουμένους πλῆθος νεῶν "they stopped their terror at the number of ships", against παύσαντες τὸ μὴ προσελθεῖν ἐγγὺς τὴν ὁλκάδα "preventing the merchantman from drawing near" (§2140).
- ἐπιστάμενος νεῖν is "knowing how to swim", because with οἶδα and ἐπίσταμαι the infinitive is know how to do something (§2139); with the participle the same verbs would give know that something is (§2139). GMT translates the parallel case for μέμνημαι, μεμνήσθω ἀνὴρ ἀγαθὸς εἶναι "let him remember to be a brave man", and notes that with ὤν it would mean let him remember that he is a brave man (GMT §915).
- Where ὤν is omitted, restore it in the English but not in the Greek: ᾱ̓̀ν ἐν Χερρονήσῳ πύθησθε Φίλιππον (ὄντα) is "if you learn that Philip is in Chersonesus" (§2119).
- A present or perfect participle used as a simple predicate adjective with εἰμί or γίγνομαι takes an English relative clause or a plain finite verb: ἦσαν ἀπιστοῦντές τινες φιλίππῳ "there were some who distrusted Philip"; ἐγὼ τὸ πρᾶγμ᾽ εἰμὶ τοῦθ᾽ ὁ δεδρᾱκώς "I am the one who has done this deed" (§2091).
- Monro notes for Homer that the aorist participle almost always represents an action as past at the time given by the verb, unlike the aorist infinitive, which generally conveys no notion of time (Monro §244); and that the aorist participle may nevertheless express a fact coincident in time with the verb, especially when the time of the fact is the important point (Monro §245).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading the participle as circumstantial and translating it as an adverbial clause when the verb needs it. The removal of a supplementary participle may make the construction incomplete (§2048).
- Reading a supplementary participle as indirect discourse after a verb that does not introduce it. With verbs not introducing indirect discourse there is no dependent statement (§2092).
- Reading the tense as time when the participle is not in indirect discourse. There the tenses denote only stage of action and not difference of time (§2094).
- Reading the tense as stage when the participle is in indirect discourse. There each tense has the same force as the corresponding tense of the indicative or optative with ὅτι or ὡς (§2106).
- Taking an aorist participle with τυγχάνω, λανθάνω or φθάνω as past. With tenses other than the present and imperfect it is coincident in time (§2096 b), and GMT's English equivalents show how to translate it (GMT §144).
- Missing that λανθάνω needs an object. GMT points out that λανθάνω is active and means to escape the notice of, so that it must have an object expressed or understood; when none is expressed, sometimes πάντας is understood and sometimes a reflexive referring to the subject, so that one and the same clause may mean either he did this without any one's knowing it or he did this unconsciously (GMT §892).
- Overlooking the reversed construction, where λανθάνω or φθάνω itself becomes the participle. GMT collects the cases, among them ἄψ ἀπὸ τείχεος ἆλτο λαθών for ἔλαθεν ἄλμενος (GMT §893).
- Forgetting the idiom οὐκ ἂν φθάνοις with a participle, which is an exhortation meaning the sooner the better: ἀποτρέχων οὐκ ἂν φθάνοις "the sooner you run off the better" (GMT §894).
- Taking the genitive after ἀκούω for a genitive of the thing heard when it marks the construction. The genitive there is the participle not in indirect discourse (§2112 a), and the accusative is the participle in indirect discourse (§2112 b).
- Assuming the participle after a verb of finding must be indirect discourse. It is often difficult to distinguish the two constructions there (§2114), and in the meaning discover, find, καταλαμβάνω does not take the aorist participle (§2114).
- Reading ὡς with the participle as a comparison. It marks the mental attitude of the subject of the main verb (§2120), and it may be added purely for emphasis when that attitude is already clearly marked (§2120).
- Treating the accusative or genitive absolute with ὡς after a verb of knowing as a separate circumstantial clause. After verbs admitting the supplementary participle in indirect discourse the absolute with ὡς may stand instead of the participle or a clause with ὅτι or ὡς (§2122).
- Expecting the participle and the infinitive to be interchangeable. They differ greatly when the infinitive expresses purpose or result (§2124), and Smyth lists the verbs where the difference is marked (§2125).
- Missing that some verbs take both with no real difference, so that no distinction should be pressed. Smyth names the verbs that take either the participle or the infinitive with no difference, or only a slight one, in meaning (§2144).
- Missing an omitted ὤν and looking for a verb that is not written. ὤν is often omitted (§2116), including after ἅτε, οἷα, ὡς and καίπερ with predicate adjectives (§2117), and with the verbs that take a supplementary participle (§2119). GMT records the same omission of ὤν in the constructions of the supplementary participle that it has listed (GMT §902).
- Taking a future participle after a verb of knowing as a participle of purpose. The future participle expresses an idea of will and shows that an action is purposed, intended or expected, and its nearest approach to mere futurity appears in general only after verbs of knowing and perceiving (§2044).
- Assuming Homer behaves like Attic here. Smyth notes that the Homeric usage of the perception verbs is less strict (§2110), and Monro treats the whole class under implied predication, where the noun or pronoun takes the place in the construction of the whole participial clause (Monro §245).
- Forgetting that the participle is a verbal adjective, so that its agreement is the first point to check (Monro §243). Goodell states the same starting point, that as a verb the participle has voice and tense and may take an object, and as an adjective it is declined and agrees with a noun or pronoun (Goodell §580).
