# Genitive Case

## 1. Quick answer

One Greek genitive does the work of two older cases. Since the genitive has absorbed the ablative it includes the genitive proper, denoting the class to which a person or thing belongs, and the ablatival genitive (§1289). Goodell says the same and adds that in great part the two sets of uses are fairly distinct, but some uses are puzzling and must be learned mainly through reading (Goodell §505). The genitive most commonly limits the meaning of substantives, adjectives and adverbs, and less commonly that of verbs (§1289). So the first question is never what the genitive means. It is whether it limits a noun or a verb, and then whether the relation is "of" or "from".

## 2. Recognition

- A substantive in the genitive limiting another substantive (§1290). The genitive limits for the time being the scope of the substantive on which it depends by referring it to a particular class or description, or by regarding it as a part of a whole, and it is akin in meaning to the adjective: στέφανος χρῡσίου beside χρῡσοῦς στέφανος (§1291).
- Possession, ownership or belonging: ἡ οἰκίᾱ ἡ Σίμωνος the house of Simon, ὁ Κῡ́ρου στόλος the expedition of Cyrus (§1297). Origin belongs here too: οἱ Σόλωνος νόμοι the laws of Solon (§1298). So does the relation of child to parent, wife to husband, and of inferior to superior: Θουκῡδίδης ὁ Ὀλόρου Thucydides the son of Olorus (§1301).
- Material or contents: κρήνη ἡδέος ὕδατος a spring of sweet water, σωροὶ σῑ́του heaps of corn (§1323).
- Measure of space, time or degree: ὀκτὼ σταδίων τεῖχος a wall eight stades long, πέντε ἡμερῶν σῑτία provisions for five days (§1325).
- Value: ἱερὰ τριῶν ταλάντων offerings worth three talents (§1336).
- Quality, and chiefly in the predicate: ἐὼν τρόπου ἡσυχίου being of a peaceful disposition (§1320). Outside the predicate the genitive of quality corresponding to the Latin genitive occurs only when age or size is exactly expressed by a numeral, and the Latin type mulier mirae pulchritudinis comes out in Greek as γυνὴ θαυμασίᾱ κάλλος (§1321). The attributive use occurs in poetry: λευκῆς χιόνος πτέρυξ a wing white as snow (§1320).
- The genitive of the divided whole, used with any word that expresses or implies a part (§1306). It stands before or after the word denoting the part: τῶν Θρᾳκῶν πελτασταί targeteers of the Thracians, οἱ ἄποροι τῶν πολῑτῶν the needy among the citizens (§1307). With comparatives and superlatives: οἱ πρεσβύτατοι τῶν στρατηγῶν the oldest of the generals (§1315). With a country named as a known place: τῆς Ἀττικῆς ἐς Οἰνόην to Oenoë in Attica (§1311).
- A genitive with a verbal noun, which may be its subject or its object. τῶν βαρβάρων φόβος is the fear the barbarians feel (§1330), and φόβος τῶν Εἱλώτων is the fear felt towards them (§1331).
- A genitive that means "from". With verbs signifying to cease, release, remove, restrain, be distant from, the genitive denotes separation: λήγειν τῶν πόνων to cease from toil, εἴργεσθαι τῆς ἀγορᾶς to be excluded from the forum (§1392).
- A genitive of comparison after a comparative: ἤττων ἀμαθὴς σοφοῦ an ignorant man is inferior to a wise man (§1431).
- A bare genitive of time: ἡμέρᾱς by day, νυκτός at or by night, θέρους in summer (§1444). And, mostly in poetry, of place: ἰέναι τοῦ πρόσω to go forward (§1448).
- A genitive after an adverb: πλησίον Θηβῶν near Thebes (§1437), and τῆς ἡμέρᾱς ὀψέ late in the day (§1439).
- A genitive noun with a genitive participle, and nothing in the main construction of the sentence to account for either. That is the genitive absolute (§2070), and it has its own page.

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Ask whether the genitive limits a noun or a verb. A substantive in the genitive limits the meaning of a substantive on which it depends (§1290); or the genitive may serve as the immediate complement of a verb, or appear as a secondary definition beside an accusative which is the immediate object of the verb (§1339).
2. If it limits a noun, do not force it into a class. The genitive with substantives denotes in general a connection or dependence between two words, and that connection must often be determined by the meaning of the words, by the context, and by the facts presupposed as known (§1295). Monro adds that the variety of relations expressed by the genitive often eludes this kind of analysis, and classifying risks the fallacy of supposing the distinctions of thought to be prior to the language which embodies them (Monro §147). Monro also states the historical ground for the two halves, that the Greek genitive stands for the original or true genitive and also for the ablative, and that the ablative belongs with the predicate while the true genitive properly qualifies a noun (Monro §146).
3. If a verb governs it, ask whether the object is affected in part or as a whole. A verb may be followed by the partitive genitive if the action affects the object only in part, and if the entire object is affected the verb takes the accusative: τῶν πώλων λαμβάνει he takes some of the colts, against τὴν γῆν πᾶσαν ἔτεμον they ravaged the whole land (§1341).
4. Go through the verb classes. Sharing: πάντες μετεῖχον τῆς ἑορτῆς all took part in the festival (§1343). Touching and taking hold of: ἀντιλάβεσθε τῶν πρᾱγμάτων take our public policy in hand (§1345). Beginning: τοῦ λόγου ἤρχετο ὧδε he began his speech as follows (§1348). Aiming at and desiring: πάντες τῶν ἀγαθῶν ἐπιθῡμοῦσιν all men desire what is good (§1349). Reaching and obtaining: σπονδῶν ἔτυχε he obtained a truce (§1350). Tasting and enjoying: ὀλίγοι σῑ́του ἐγεύσαντο few tasted food (§1355). Remembering and caring for: τῶν ἀπόντων φίλων μέμνησο remember your absent friends (§1356). Hearing and perceiving: ἀκούσαντες τῆς σάλπιγγος hearing the sound of the trumpet (§1361). Filling: τριήρης σεσαγμένη ἀνθρώπων a trireme stowed with men (§1369). Ruling and commanding: τῆς θαλάττης ἐκράτει he was master of the sea (§1370). Buying and selling: ἀργυρίου πρίασθαι ἢ ἀποδόσθαι ἵππον to buy or sell a horse for money (§1372). Judicial action, where the genitive is the crime and the accusative the person accused: δώρων ἐκρίθησαν they were tried for bribery (§1375). Emotion, where the genitive is the cause: ἐθαύμασα τῆς τόλμης τῶν λεγόντων I wondered at the hardihood of the speakers (§1405).
5. If the sense is "from", work down the ablatival half. The genitive denotes separation with verbs of ceasing and removing (§1392); the genitive with verbs signifying to want and to lack may be classed with it (§1396); with verbs of differing it marks the thing differed from (§1401); with verbs signifying to surpass or be inferior to it denotes that with which anything is compared (§1402); and with verbs of hearing from it is probably ablatival rather than partitive (§1411).
6. Where the two halves cannot be separated, say so rather than choosing. Smyth records the doubt: it is uncertain whether verbs signifying to miss take a partitive or an ablatival genitive (§1352), and in many cases it is difficult to decide whether the genitive was originally the true genitive or the ablatival genitive, or whether the two have been combined (§1391). Goodell prints οὐδεὶς ἡμάρτανεν τοῦ ἀνδρός under the From genitive with no hesitation at all (Goodell §509), and Monro lists ἁμαρτάνω among the ablatival uses (Monro §152).
7. Adjectives and adverbs follow the verbs they answer to. The genitive is used with many adjectives corresponding in derivation or meaning to verbs taking the genitive (§1412), and the adjective often borrows the construction with the genitive from that of the corresponding verb (§1413). Adverbs do the same: τὰ τούτου ἑξῆς what comes after this, ἀξίως ἀνδρὸς ἀγαθοῦ in a manner worthy of a good man (§1437).
8. For time, ask how much of the period is meant. As contrasted with the accusative of time the genitive denotes a portion of time, and the genitive of time is hence partitive (§1444). The genitive may or may not denote a definite part of the time during which anything takes place; the dative fixes the time explicitly, and the accusative expresses the whole extent of time from beginning to end (§1447).

## 4. Translation guidance

- Adnominal genitives usually take an English of-phrase or a possessive, and sometimes an adjective, because the genitive is akin in meaning to the adjective and may often be translated by an epithet (§1291).
- The objective genitive normally takes some other English preposition than "of": ὁ θεῶν πόλεμος war with the gods, ἀδικημάτων ὀργή anger at injustice, θεῶν εὐχαί prayers to the gods (§1332).
- The genitive of the divided whole is "of" or "among": οἱ ἄδικοι τῶν ἀνθρώπων the unjust among men (§1312). It can be the subject of a finite verb or of an infinitive, and English then has to supply "some": ἐπιμειγνύναι σφῶν πρὸς ἐκείνους they said that some of their number associated with them (§1318).
- A predicate genitive of measure becomes an English adjective phrase: τὰ τείχη ἦν σταδίων ὀκτώ the walls were eight stades long (§1327).
- The genitive of price takes "for": πόσου διδάσκει for how much does he teach, πέντε μνῶν for five minae (§1372).
- The genitive of cause after an interjection is an English exclamation: φεῦ τοῦ ἀνδρός alas for the man (§1407).
- Goodell's rendering of the genitive with adverbs of place is the most literal: πόρρω τοῦ βίου θανάτου δὲ ἐγγύς far on in life and near death (Goodell §518).
- Where the genitive is the standard of comparison, English uses "than". Greek often prefers the genitive where ἤ would have to be followed by some other case than the nominative or accusative: ταῦτα τοῖς ὁπλῑ́ταις οὐχ ἧσσον τῶν ναυτῶν παρακελεύομαι I address these exhortations to the hoplites not less than to the sailors (§1069 c).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading πάντες with a genitive as partitive. When all are included there is no partition, and οὗτοι πάντες is simply all of these (§1308).
- Making the genitive with ἀκούειν the thing heard. The person or thing whose words or sound are perceived by the senses stands in the genitive, and the words or sound generally stand in the accusative: ἀκούσαντες τὸν θόρυβον hearing the noise (§1361).
- Reading every genitive with a verb of hearing the same way. ἀκούειν, αἰσθάνεσθαι and πυνθάνεσθαι meaning to become aware of or learn take the accusative (§1363); in the meaning heed, hearken, obey, verbs of hearing generally take the genitive (§1366); and ἀκούειν, ἀκροᾶσθαι and πυνθάνεσθαι meaning to hear from or learn from take the genitive of the actual source (§1364).
- Forgetting that the accusative is often available beside the genitive. Many of these verbs also take the accusative, and neuter pronouns must stand in the accusative (§1358).
- Taking every genitive after a compound verb as governed by the whole verb. Many verbs compounded with ἀπό, πρό, ὑπέρ, ἐπί and κατά take the genitive when the compound may be resolved into the simple verb and the preposition without change in the sense (§1384), and it is often difficult to determine whether the genitive depends on the compound verb as a whole or on the preposition contained in it (§1382).
- Reading κατηγορεῖν and related verbs as ordinary genitive verbs. The verbs of accusing and condemning containing κατά in composition take a genitive of the person and an accusative of the penalty, and κατηγορεῖν, καταγιγνώσκειν and καταψηφίζεσθαι take a genitive of the person and an accusative of the crime (§1385).
- Treating the genitive of comparison and ἤ as interchangeable. The genitive is usual if two subjects would have the same verb in common (§1069 a); but where two objects have the same verb in common and stand in the genitive, ἤ is preferred for the sake of euphony (§1069 b).
- Confusing the genitive of time with the dative. The genitive of time is less common than the dative of time with ordinals, or with ὅδε, οὗτος, ἐκεῖνος (§1447 a).
- Reading a genitive of place as a genitive of separation. The genitive denotes the place within which or at which an action happens, and is more frequent in poetry than in prose, though Smyth marks λαιᾶς χειρὸς οἰκοῦσι they dwell on the left hand as possibly ablatival (§1448).
- Expecting the genitive absolute to follow from the other uses. The grammars disagree about where it came from. Smyth derives it from cases like Γλαύκου ἀπιόντος, a genitive properly dependent on ἄχος γένετο which ceased to be felt as dependent and was extended to cases in which the governing expression did not take the genitive (§2075). Monro holds instead that it must have begun as an extension of one of the ordinary uses of the genitive, most probably the genitive of time (Monro §246), the use that expresses a period of time to which the action belongs without implying anything as to its duration (Monro §150).
