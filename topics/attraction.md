## 1. Quick answer

Attraction is one of Smyth's two explanations for an apparent violation of the concords: a word does not have its natural construction because of the influence of some other word or words in its clause, and the principle extends to moods and tenses as well as to cases (§926). Around a relative clause it takes three shapes, and telling them apart is the whole job. A relative pronoun is often attracted from its proper case into the case of its antecedent, especially from the accusative into the genitive or dative, and the demonstrative to whose case it is attracted is usually omitted if unemphatic (§2522). The antecedent taken up into the relative clause is said to be incorporated, and the relative and antecedent then stand in the same case, the relative agreeing adjectively with its antecedent (§2536). Inverse attraction goes the other way: an antecedent nominative or, oftener, accusative may be attracted to the case of the relative, and the attracted antecedent is often prefixed for emphasis to the relative clause (§2533). None of the three is compulsory. Attraction is not necessary, and takes place only, but not always, when the relative clause is essential to complete the meaning of the antecedent (§2524).

## 2. Recognition

- A relative in the genitive or dative whose own verb takes an accusative: ἄξιοι τῆς ἐλευθερίᾱς ἧς κέκτησθε "worthy of the freedom which you possess", and φοβοίμην ἂν τῷ ἡγεμόνι ᾧ δοίη ἕπεσθαι "I should fear to follow the leader whom he might give" (§2522 a, §2522 b). Goodell states the same rule, that the relative is often attracted from its proper case to the case of the antecedent, especially from the accusative to the genitive or dative (Goodell §613).
- A relative with no expressed antecedent: ἀφ᾽ ὧν ἴστε "from what you know", and ἐπαινῶ σε ἐφ᾽ οἷς λέγεις "I commend you for what you say" (§2522 a, §2522 b). When the omitted antecedent is genitive or dative, the relative, if standing in a different case, is usually attracted into the genitive or dative (§2531).
- ὅσος and οἷος are attracted just as ὅς is (§2524): Μήδων ὅσων ἑώρᾱκα "of all the Medes I have seen", and μὴ ὑποκειμένων οἵων δεῖ θεμελίων "if the foundations were not as they ought to be" (§2522 a).
- A noun standing inside the relative clause, in the relative's case and usually without an article: οὐκ ἀπεκρύπτετο ἣν εἶχε γνώμην "he did not conceal the opinion he had", and εἰς δὲ ἣν ἀφῑ́κοντο κώμην μεγάλη ἦν "the village at which they arrived was large" (§2537, §2537 a). Goodell describes the same construction as the antecedent taken up into the subordinate clause, with ὅς agreeing with it adjectively (Goodell §613).
- An accusative or nominative in front of the relative clause, in the relative's case rather than its own: τᾱ́σδε δ᾽ ᾱ̔́σπερ εἰσορᾷς . . . χωροῦσι "but the women whom thou seest are coming", and ἔλεγον ὅτι Λακεδαιμόνιοι ὧν δέονται πάντων πεπρᾱγότες εἶεν "they said that the Lacedaemonians had gained all they asked for" (§2533).
- οὐδεὶς ὅστις οὐ inflected as a unit, οὐδενὸς ὅτου οὐ, οὐδενὶ ὅτῳ οὐ, οὐδένα ὅντινα οὐ (§2534).
- θαυμαστὰ ὅσα and its kind, where the subject of the relative clause is identical with that of the main clause and is omitted together with the copula: χρήματα ἔλαβε θαυμαστὰ ὅσα "he received a wonderful amount of money" (§2535).
- A subject in the accusative after ὥσπερ, where the comparative clause omits its verb: οὐδαμοῦ γὰρ ἔστιν Ἀγόρατον Ἀθηναῖον εἶναι ὥσπερ Θρασύβουλον "it is in no wise possible for Agoratus to be an Athenian as Thrasybulus is" (§2465).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. First decide the direction of the influence. In ordinary attraction the relative leaves its own case for the antecedent's (§2522); in inverse attraction the antecedent leaves its own case for the relative's (§2533); in incorporation the antecedent stands inside the relative clause, and the two agree (§2536).
2. Restore the case the verb of the relative clause takes, and see whether the sentence works. Smyth prints the unattracted form beside his examples, so πρὸ τῶν κακῶν ὧν οἶδα is read as the genitive standing for an accusative (§2522 a), and οἷς ηὐτυχήκεσαν ἐν Λεύκτροις as the dative standing for an accusative (§2522 b).
3. Check the pronoun against the list before calling it attraction. The pronouns subject to attraction are ὅς, οἷος, ὅσος, but not ὅστις, except in the one construction at §2534 (§2524).
4. Ask what the relative clause is doing. An attracted relative clause virtually has the force of an attributive adjective, and when the relative clause is added merely as a remark, attraction does not take place (§2524).
5. The direction of attraction is not free. It runs especially from the accusative into the genitive or dative (§2522), and a relative in the nominative or dative is very rarely attracted (§2523).
6. Look for the demonstrative, which may be missing or late. A demonstrative pronoun to whose case the relative is attracted is usually omitted if unemphatic (§2522), and an omitted antecedent to which the relative has been attracted may afterward be supplied in the main clause, as in ἀφ᾽ ὧν . . . προσαιτεῖ καὶ δανείζεται, ἀπὸ τούτων διάγει "from what he begs and borrows, from that he lives" (§2526).
7. The omitted antecedent has its own rule, which is not the same rule. When the omitted antecedent is nominative or accusative, the relative retains its own case (§2530); when it is genitive or dative, the relative is usually attracted, but a relative in the nominative masculine or feminine, sometimes in the neuter, or one depending on a preposition, retains its own case (§2531). In Smyth's contrasting pair, ὧν ἐντυγχάνω πολὺ μάλιστα ἄγαμαι σέ has the attraction, while εἰδέναι τὴν δύναμιν ἐφ᾽ οὓς ἂν ἴωσιν keeps the relative's own case after the preposition (§2531 a).
8. The same rules apply to adverbs. Attraction takes place also in the case of relative adverbs, as in διεκομίζοντο ὅθεν ὑπεξέθεντο παῖδας "they conveyed their children from the places where they had deposited them" (§2528), and inverse attraction likewise, καὶ ἄλλοσε ὅποι ἂν ἀφίκῃ ἀγαπήσουσί δε "and elsewhere, wherever you go, they will love you" (§2533 c).
9. If a predicate noun is in the clause, expect it to follow. Predicate nouns follow the case of the relative attracted to an antecedent expressed or omitted (§2525).
10. If the relative is οἷος, ὅσος or ἡλίκος and the clause omits its copula, treat the whole clause as attracted. These relatives and a following nominative with the copula may be attracted to the case of the antecedent, so that χαριζόμενος τοιούτῳ ἀνδρὶ οἷος σὺ εἶ is commonly condensed to χαριζόμενος οἵῳ σοι ἀνδρί (§2532). The construction is common with superlatives, ὄντος πάγου οἵου δεινοτάτου "when the frost was tremendous" (§2532 a), takes the article when the relative clause is treated like a substantive, τοῖς οἵοις ἡμῖν "to such as we are" (§2532 b), and rarely keeps the subject in the nominative, κιναίδους οἵουσπερ σύ "rascals just like you", which occurs only when the number of the subject differs from that of the attracted relative (§2532 c).
11. Read θαυμαστὰ ὅσα as one expression, not as an adjective and a clause. The subject of the relative clause is identical with that of the main clause and is omitted with the copula, as in μετὰ ἱδρῶτος θαυμαστοῦ ὅσου "with an astonishing amount of sweat", and θαυμασίως ὡς belongs with them (§2535).
12. For incorporation, ask what case the antecedent would have had. A nominative, accusative or vocative antecedent, when incorporated, usually conforms to the case of the relative (§2537), and an accusative antecedent is incorporated in the accusative when the verb of the relative clause takes the accusative (§2537 a). An accusative antecedent may instead be incorporated as nominative, genitive or dative, as in κατασκευάζοντα ἧς ἄρχοι χώρᾱς (§2537 b). A genitive or dative antecedent, on the other hand, usually attracts the relative to its own case, as in ἐπορεύετο σὺν ᾗ εἶχε δυνάμει "he advanced with what force he had" (§2538); even when the antecedent is omitted, that attraction takes place (§2538). An antecedent in the nominative or accusative is more frequently incorporated than one in the genitive or dative, and an incorporated substantive often stands at the end of the relative clause and commonly has no article (§2536).
13. Watch the two exceptions to that. A genitive or dative antecedent, when incorporated, is attracted into the case of a nominative relative (§2538 a), and the place of such an antecedent is usually taken by a demonstrative pronoun in the genitive or dative, as in οὐδέ νυ τῶν περ μέμνηαι, ὅσα δὴ πάθομεν κακά "nor do you remember all the evils we suffered" (§2538 b).
14. If the case in front of the relative clause belongs to no verb, suspect inverse attraction rather than a corrupt text. The attracted antecedent is often prefixed for emphasis to the relative clause, which thus separates it from the verb it governs or by which it is governed (§2533), and the main clause may contain a resumptive demonstrative pronoun (§2533 a). Smyth notes the limits: the rare cases of the inverse attraction of the dative are suspected or admit another explanation (§2533 b).
15. Treat οὐδεὶς ὅστις οὐ as a pronoun. It stands for οὐδείς ἐστιν ὅστις οὐ, commonly shows inverse attraction, is treated like a single pronoun, and is inflected (§2534), as in οὐδενὶ ὅτῳ οὐκ ἀποκρῑνόμενος "replying to every one" (§2534). Goodell explains the same phrase the same way, through omission of the verb and the attraction of the antecedent to the case of the relative, and prints Οὐδἐνα ὅντινʼ οὐ κατέκλασε "He broke down every one" (Goodell §614).
16. In a comparison, look for a subject in the wrong case rather than a missing verb. The subject of a comparative clause with ὡς or ὥσπερ, the verb of which is omitted, is often attracted into the case, usually the accusative, of the other member of the comparison, and attraction into the dative is less common, as in ὥσπερ σκύλακι γενναίῳ ἀνακλάζοντι (§2465). Usually, however, the nominative stands with the verb omitted, πέπεισμαί σε μᾶλλον ἀποθανεῖν ἂν ἑλέσθαι ἢ ζῆν ὥσπερ ἐγώ (§2465 a).
17. Attraction is not limited to cases. The principle of attraction extends to moods and tenses (§926), where Smyth calls it assimilation: the mood of a subordinate clause intimately connected with the thought of the clause on which it depends is often assimilated to the mood of that clause (§2183). GMT states the same for relative clauses in both directions: a conditional relative clause referring to the future and depending on a subjunctive or optative regularly takes by assimilation the same mood as its leading verb (GMT §558), and one depending on a past tense of the indicative implying non-fulfilment regularly takes a past tense of the indicative (GMT §559).
18. Where the concord fails and there is no relative, look for the other kinds of attraction in Smyth's list. A demonstrative pronoun may agree in gender with a substantive predicated of it, αὕτη ἀρίστη διδασκαλία "this is the best manner of learning" (§1239), and when the subject of the infinitive is the same as the object in the genitive or dative of the governing verb, it is often omitted and a predicate noun is either attracted into the genitive or dative or stands in the accusative (§1978, §1060). Gildersleeve treats these under concord: the demonstrative pronoun is commonly attracted into the gender of the predicate (Gildersleeve §127), the attraction is sometimes pretermitted, especially in definitions in which the pronoun is the predicate (Gildersleeve §129), the superlative predicate may agree in gender with the subject rather than with the genitive (Gildersleeve §134), and the copula itself may be attracted by the predicate, as in χρυσὸς δὲ καὶ ἄργυρος, "Gold and silver is an invidious possession" (Gildersleeve §476).
19. If a relative does not agree with its antecedent, check the concord rules before treating it as attraction. Variations from the law of agreement are in general the same as with other pronouns, and Smyth lists them: construction according to sense, a plural relative after a singular antecedent denoting a whole class, a singular relative with collective force after a plural antecedent, a neuter relative agreeing with the notion implied in the antecedent, and agreement with a following predicate noun (§2502).
20. If the passage is Homer, the Attic attraction does not occur. Monro states that the later attraction of the relative into the case of the antecedent is not found in Homer (Monro §271). Homer has a different attraction, from the omission of the verb to be, found chiefly with ὅσος τε, as τὴν δὲ γυναῖκα εὗρον ὅσην τʼ ὄρεος κορυφήν, equivalent to τόσην ὅση ἐστὶ κορυφή (Monro §271). Inverse attraction occurs there: an antecedent may follow the case of the relative if it is separated from its own clause, νῆες ὅσαι πρῶται εἰρύαται ἄγχι θαλάσσης ἕλκωμεν, and Monro places it with the forms in which the antecedent is wanting, because it can only arise when the original construction of the antecedent has been forgotten (Monro §267).

## 4. Translation guidance

- An attracted relative is translated as if it had its own case, so English does not show the attraction: ἄξιοι τῆς ἐλευθερίᾱς ἧς κέκτησθε is "worthy of the freedom which you possess" (§2522 a).
- Where the antecedent is omitted, English usually needs what: ἀφ᾽ ὧν ἴστε is "from what you know" (§2522 a), and ἐμμένομεν οἷς ὡμολογήσαμεν δικαίοις οὖσιν ἢ οὔ; is "do we abide by what we agreed was just, or not?" (§2531 b). Goodell renders the same sentence the same way, "Do we abide by what we agreed was just, or not?" (Goodell §614).
- An incorporated antecedent comes back out in English: οὐκ ἀπεκρύπτετο ἣν εἶχε γνώμην is "he did not conceal the opinion he had" (§2537 a), and Goodell's Τούτους ἄρχοντας ἐποίει ἦς κατεστρέφετο χώρᾱς is "These he made rulers of the territory which he subdued" (Goodell §613).
- Inverse attraction usually needs an English relative clause with the antecedent restored: πολῑτείᾱν οἵᾱν εἶναι χρή παρὰ μόνοις ἡμῖν ἐστιν is "we alone have an ideal constitution", literally such as ought to be (§2533).
- οὐδεὶς ὅστις οὐ is English every one, and the negative is not to be rendered twice: οὐδενὸς ὅτου οὐχὶ ἀλογώτερον is "than which there is nothing more irrational" (§2534).
- θαυμαστὰ ὅσα is an amount, not a clause: χρήματα ἔλαβε θαυμαστὰ ὅσα is "he received a wonderful amount of money" (§2535).
- The οἷος construction is English such a man as you are, and the full form is χαριζόμενος τοιούτῳ ἀνδρὶ οἷος σὺ εἶ (§2532). With superlatives it is a plain intensive, ὄντος πάγου οἵου δεινοτάτου "when the frost was tremendous" (§2532 a).
- A comparison whose subject has been attracted keeps the English verb: ὥσπερ Θρασύβουλον is "as Thrasybulus is" (§2465).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading an attracted genitive as a genitive required by its own clause. In ἄξιοι τῆς ἐλευθερίᾱς ἧς κέκτησθε the relative is in the genitive not from its own clause, but from the case of its antecedent (§2522 a).
- Treating attraction as a rule that always applies. It is not necessary, and takes place only, but not always, when the relative clause is essential to complete the meaning of the antecedent (§2524).
- Attracting ὅστις. The pronouns subject to attraction are ὅς, οἷος, ὅσος, but not ὅστις (§2524), and the exception is the fixed phrase at §2534.
- Supplying the antecedent in the relative's case. When the omitted antecedent is nominative or accusative the relative retains its own case (§2530), so an unattracted relative does not prove there was no antecedent.
- Forgetting the preposition. A relative depending on a preposition retains its own case even when the omitted antecedent is genitive or dative (§2531).
- Emending an inverse attraction. An antecedent nominative or, oftener, accusative may be attracted to the case of the relative, and Smyth compares Latin urbem quam statuo vestra est (§2533).
- Taking an incorporated noun as the object of the main verb. The antecedent taken up into the relative clause stands in the relative's case and often at the end of that clause, commonly without an article (§2536), and a nominative, accusative or vocative antecedent so incorporated usually conforms to the case of the relative (§2537).
- Expecting the genitive and dative to behave like the accusative under incorporation. A genitive or dative antecedent, when incorporated, usually attracts the relative to its own case instead (§2538).
- Reading θαυμαστὰ ὅσα as an ordinary neuter plural adjective with a relative clause after it. The copula is omitted with the subject, and the phrase is a quantity (§2535).
- Looking for the Attic attraction in Homer. It is not found there (Monro §271), and Homer has inverse attraction instead (Monro §267).
- Calling every failure of agreement attraction. Smyth's other explanation is construction according to sense, where the agreement is with the real gender or number (§926), and the relative pronoun has its own list of such variations (§2502).
