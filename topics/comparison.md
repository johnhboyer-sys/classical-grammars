# Comparison

`topics/indirect-discourse.md`. Every claim carries a section citation;
`scripts/verify_topic_pages.py` checks them. `topic: comparison` names the

## 1. Quick answer

Greek compares in two ways at once, by ending and by construction. The usual endings are -τερος for the comparative and -τατος for the superlative, added to the masculine stem of the positive (§313); a smaller class adds -ῑων and -ιστος to the root, θᾱ́ττων beside τάχιστος and μείζων beside μέγιστος (§318, §319). Instead of the endings the adverbs μᾶλλον and μάλιστα may be used with the positive, and that is the only way of comparing participles and words that do not take the comparative and superlative endings (§323). Then the construction. The comparative degree may be followed by the genitive or by ἤ than, σοφώτερος ἐμοῦ or σοφώτερος ἢ ἐγώ (§1069); with ἤ the persons or things compared usually stand in the same case, and always so when they are connected by the same verb (§1069). The genitive here is ablatival: it denotes the standard or point of departure from which the comparison is made (§1431). The superlative is either relative, the highest degree of a quality, or absolute, a very high degree, and only the relative takes the article and the genitive of the person or thing surpassed (§1085). A superlative is strengthened by prefixing ὅτι or ὡς, ὅτι πλεῖστοι "as many men as possible" (§1086).

## 2. Recognition

- The plain endings, with the stem vowel lengthened before a short penult. Adjectives in -ος with a short penult lengthen ο to ω, and an undue succession of short syllables is thus avoided (§314). Goodell states the same rule for stems in -ο with a short penult (Goodell §177).
- The -ῑων and -ιστος class, where the vowel or the syllable ρο standing before ς of the nominative is lost, and which is much commoner in poetry than in prose (§318). Goodell describes the same class, a few common adjectives that add those endings to the root of the positive, or to a different form of the stem (Goodell §181).
- Suppletive sets that are not irregular so much as several words under one positive: ἀγαθός with ἀμείνων, ἄριστος, βελτῑ́ων, βέλτιστος, κρείττων, κράτιστος, and κακός with κακῑ́ων, κάκιστος, χείρων, χείριστος, ἥττων (§319). Goodell prints the same groups and explains them the same way, that words quite different have come to be grouped together (Goodell §182).
- Comparatives and superlatives with no positive at all: πρότερος and πρῶτος from πρό, ὕστερος and ὕστατος (§320). Goodell's list of the adjectives that lack a positive is the same, πρότερος and πρῶτος, ὑπέρτερος and ὑπέρτατος, ὕστερος and ὕστατος (Goodell §183).
- An adverb in the comparative or superlative, which is the adjective's neuter: σοφῶς gives σοφώτερον and σοφώτατα, καλῶς gives κάλλῑον and κάλλιστα (§345). Goodell states the rule as the neuter accusative singular for the comparative and the neuter accusative plural for the superlative (Goodell §234).
- μᾶλλον or μάλιστα with the positive, which is what compound adjectives, adjectives with a prepositional prefix, verbal adjectives in -τός and adjectives in -ιος generally take (§323 a). Goodell adds ἧττον and ἥκιστα to the pair and notes that participles are compared only in this way (Goodell §180).
- A bare genitive with no preposition and no ἤ: ἤττων ἀμαθὴς σοφοῦ, δειλὸς ἀνδρείου "an ignorant man is inferior to a wise man, a coward to a brave man" (§1431). Goodell calls it the From Genitive and reads it as the starting-point of comparison, οὑτοσῑ̀ σοφώτερος ἐμοῦ "This man here is wiser than I" (Goodell §517).
- A genitive after a verb rather than an adjective. With verbs signifying to surpass, be inferior to, the genitive denotes that with which anything is compared, ἡττῶντο τοῦ ὕδατος "they were overpowered by the water" (§1402). Goodell's list of the same verbs runs προέχω, προέστηκα, ὑπερέχω, λείπομαι, νῑκῶμαι (Goodell §509).
- ὅσῳ or ὅσον answered by τοσούτῳ or τοσοῦτον, the Greek for the more, the more (§2468, §2469).
- ὡς, ὥσπερ, καθάπερ opening a clause with no verb of its own (§2463, §2464).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Read the degree before the construction, and do not assume the comparative compares. It is sometimes used merely as an intensive and does not differ essentially from the positive (§1067), it may stand alone with the second part implied (§1082), and it is often used where English requires the positive, οὐ γὰρ χεῖρον πολλάκις ἀκούειν "for 'tis not a bad thing to hear often" (§1083).
2. Ask what the comparative is contrasted with when nothing follows it. That which is exceeded may be indicated by the sense only, so that οἱ σοφώτεροι is the wiser, those wiser than the rest (§1082 a); the comparative may denote excess (§1082 c); and it is used to soften an expression, ἀγροικότερον "somewhat boorishly" (§1082 d).
3. Consider the positive as well. The positive, used to imply that something is not suited or inadequate for the purpose in question, is especially common before an infinitive with or without ὥστε, νῆες ὀλίγαι ἀμῡ́νειν "ships too few to defend" (§1063). Goodell places the same construction under the infinitive with ὡς or ὥστε after adjectives in the comparative or implying comparison, βραχύτερα ἢ ὡς ἐξικνεῖσθαι "too short to reach" (Goodell §566).
4. Choose between the genitive and ἤ; this is a real choice, not a stylistic one. The genitive is usual if two subjects would have the same verb in common (§1069 a). When two objects have the same verb in common, the genitive is preferred if the object stands in the accusative, is frequent if it stands in the dative, and is very rare if it stands in the genitive, where ἤ is preferred for the sake of euphony (§1069 b). And the genitive is often used where ἤ would be followed by some other case than nominative or accusative, or by a preposition (§1069 c).
5. If ἤ is there, look at what follows it. The word following ἤ may be the subject of a new verb, expressed or understood, ἡμεῖς ὑπὸ κρείττονος διδασκάλου πεπαιδεύμεθα ἢ οὗτοι "we have been educated by a better teacher than they", but it is more often attracted into the case of the preceding word (§1070). Gildersleeve notes what happens when two subjects are joined that way: the common predicate of two subjects compared by means of the particle ἤ agrees with the last (Gildersleeve §474).
6. ἤ may be omitted in statements of number. In statements of number and measure ἤ may be omitted after the adverbial comparatives πλέον (πλεῖν) and ἔλᾱττον (μεῖον), which do not alter their case and number, πόλις πλέον πεντακισχῑλίων ἀνδρῶν "a city of more than 5000 men" (§1074). Even when ἤ is kept, πλέον remains unchanged (§1074), and the adjectival forms may be used instead, with or without ἤ or with the genitive (§1074 a).
7. If both the genitive and ἤ are present, both may stand. The genitive sometimes occurs together with ἤ, when the genitive has a separate construction, or is a pronoun to which the ἤ clause stands as an appositive or of which it is explanatory (§1075).
8. If neither is there, look for a preposition. Instead of the genitive or ἤ, ἀντί and πρό with the genitive, or πρός and παρά with the accusative, are sometimes used with the comparative, μὴ παῖδας περὶ πλείονος ποιοῦ πρὸ τοῦ δικαίου "do not consider children of more account than justice" (§1073). Smyth states the same point, that the object compared may be expressed by πρό or ἀντί with the genitive, or by παρά or πρός with the accusative (§1404).
9. Read the dative in the sentence as the measure, not as a second comparison. The dative of manner is used with comparative adjectives and other expressions of comparison to mark the degree by which one thing differs from another, κεφαλῇ ἐλᾱ́ττων "a head shorter", and with the superlative, μακρῷ ἄριστα "by far the best" (§1513, §1513 a).
10. Watch for the four idiomatic comparatives before translating the genitive literally. Compendious comparison puts the possessor, rather than the object possessed, in the genitive after a comparative, μὴ χεῖρον τούτων "not inferior to theirs" (§1076). Reflexive comparison measures the increase against the subject itself, πλουσιώτεροι ἑαυτῶν γιγνόμενοι "becoming richer than they were before" (§1078), and the same happens with the superlative, ἀμβλύτατα αὐτὸς αὑτοῦ ὁρᾷ "his sight is at its dullest" (§1093). Proportional comparison uses ἢ κατά with the accusative, or ἢ ὥστε and ἢ ὡς with the infinitive, to denote too high or too low a degree, ὅπλα ἔτι πλείω ἢ κατὰ τοὺς νεκροὺς ἐλήφθη "more arms were taken than there were men slain" (§1079). Double comparison puts both adjectives in the comparative and always uses ἤ, ἡ εἰρήνη ἀναγκαιοτέρᾱ ἢ καλλῑ́ων "a peace inevitable rather than honourable" (§1080).
11. If one member of the comparison is a whole idea rather than a thing, expect it to be abridged. When one person or thing is compared with an entire idea expressed by a clause, that clause may be abridged into a substantive or a participle, πρᾶγμα ἐλπίδος κρεῖσσον "an event beyond our expectations" (§1077).
12. Ask whether the superlative is relative or absolute, because the article settles it. ὁ σοφώτατος ἀνήρ is the wisest man, ἀνὴρ σοφώτατος a very wise man, and the relative superlative is followed by the genitive of the person or thing surpassed (§1085). The class an individual belongs to may be designated by a genitive of the divided whole (§1085 a), and Smyth places the same genitive under the partitive, οἱ πρεσβύτατοι τῶν στρατηγῶν "the oldest of the generals" (§1315).
13. The genitive with a superlative has two functions. It is both partitive and ablatival, so that σοφώτατος ἀνθρώπων means wisest among men and wiser than any other single man, and the partitive idea is the stronger (§1434). Gildersleeve states that the superlative predicate may agree in gender with the subject rather than with the genitive (Gildersleeve §134).
14. The strengtheners mark the degree. ὅτι or ὡς, rarely ᾗ, prefixed to the superlative gives as . . . as possible, and ὅτι or ὥς is always added when a preposition precedes, ὡς εἰς στενώτατον "into as narrow compass as possible" (§1086); with ὡς a form of δύναμαι or οἷός τέ εἰμι may be used (§1086 a). οἷος may strengthen the superlative (§1087), and so may εἷς ἀνήρ in apposition (§1088), ἐν τοῖς before it in all genders and numbers (§1089), and μάλιστα, πλεῖστον or μέγιστον with it (§1090). καί, πολλῷ, μακρῷ, πολύ, παρὰ πολύ and the correlative ὅσῳ also strengthen it (§1091), and in poetry a superlative may be strengthened by the genitive of the same adjective in the positive, ὦ κακῶν κάκιστε "oh, vilest of the vile" (§1092).
15. Do not read μᾶλλον beside a comparative as a doubled error. The comparative may be strengthened by ἔτι, πολλῷ, μακρῷ, πολύ, and μᾶλλον is sometimes used with the comparative (§1084). μᾶλλον ἤ may also be used though a comparative precedes, and there it is to be taken with the verb (§1072). A double comparative form, on the other hand, occurs sometimes to produce a comic effect, and a double superlative is πρώτιστος (§322).
16. Where two clauses are compared, find the correlative pair first. Comparative clauses of quantity or degree are introduced by ὅσῳ, ὅσον in proportion as, and the principal clause usually contains τοσούτῳ, τοσοῦτον (§2468). ὅσῳ with τοσούτῳ and a comparative or superlative is the more, the more, and the less, the less (§2469). The demonstrative antecedent may be omitted, especially when its clause precedes (§2470), one member may contain a comparative and the other a superlative (§2471), and ὅσῳ may be used without a comparative when the correlative clause has one (§2472). The clauses may even be fused, when both correlatives are dropped and the predicate of the subordinate clause is a comparative or superlative with a form of εἶναι (§2473).
17. For a comparative clause of quality, look for the missing verb rather than supplying a new one. Such clauses are introduced by ὡς, ὥσπερ, καθάπερ, ὅπως, ᾗ, ὅπῃ, ᾗπερ, and the principal clause may contain a demonstrative adverb such as οὕτως or ὧδε (§2463); the verb of the comparative clause is commonly omitted if it is the same as the verb of the leading clause (§2464). Goodell says the same of ὥσπερ, a strengthened ὡς used only in a relative and comparative sense, whose clauses are therefore like simple sentences (Goodell §637).
18. If the subject of such a clause is in the accusative, this is attraction, not a new construction. The subject of a comparative clause with ὡς or ὥσπερ whose verb is omitted is often attracted into the case, usually the accusative, of the other member of the comparison, though the nominative with the verb omitted is usual (§2465, §2465 a).
19. Read the mood in a comparative clause by the conditional rules. The moods there are used with the same meaning as in conditional clauses or other conditional relative clauses (§2474): the indicative in assertions and statements of fact (§2475), with ἄν for the potential or unreal (§2475 a); the subjunctive with ἄν of future time (§2476 a) and of present time in general conditions (§2476 b); the optative with ἄν as potential (§2477 a) and as in less vivid future conditions (§2477 b), and without ἄν in indirect discourse representing ἄν with the subjunctive (§2477 c) or of past time in general conditions (§2477 d).
20. If ὥσπερ has εἰ after it, take the two together. ὥσπερ εἰ and ὥσπερ ἂν εἰ, just as if, form a combination of a comparison and a condition, used with the imperfect or aorist indicative or with the optative, and ὥσπερ there represents the suppressed apodosis to the condition with εἰ (§2478). ὥσπερ ἂν εἰ is the commoner of the two (§2480). GMT explains the same forms as an unconscious suppression of the verb of the apodosis in similes and comparisons (GMT §485), and separately as a suppression of the verb of the protasis when ὡς εἰ or ὡς εἴ τε stands with a noun, an adjective or a participle (GMT §475). Smyth adds that in some cases the ellipsis may easily be supplied, but that it was usually unconscious (§2478).
21. If the passage is poetry, expect the comparison to be a simile and the mood to follow other rules. ὡς, ὡς εἰ, ὡς εἴ τε, ὡς ὅτε and ὡς ὁπότε are often used in poetry in similes and comparisons, with the present and aorist indicative and subjunctive regularly, and the optative only with ὡς εἰ or ὡς εἴ τε (§2481, §2481 a). ὡς is common in Homer with the subjunctive without ἄν (§2483), ὡς εἰ is commonly ὡς εἴ τε there and usually stands without any finite verb (§2484), and ὡς ὅτε and ὡς ὁπότε take the indicative or the subjunctive, generally without ἄν in Homer (§2486). Attic poetry does not use the Epic and Lyric ὡς εἴ τε for ὡς εἰ, and in Attic ὡς εἰ is practically equivalent to ὡς (§2485). GMT states the same construction for Homer: similes may be expressed by the subjunctive with ὡς ὅτε, rarely ὡς ὁπότε, sometimes by ὡς or ὥς τε, and except in a few cases of ὡς ὅτ᾽ ἄν neither ἄν nor κέ is found in these expressions (GMT §543).
22. If the passage is Homer, a comparative does not always compare degrees. Monro reads the Homeric suffix as contrastive: the meaning is often not that an object has more of a quality than some other object, but that it has the quality in contradistinction to objects which are without it, so that κουρότεροι and ὁπλότεροι mark the class of youths (Monro §122). Smyth says the same of Attic when the force is only contrast, since δεξίτερος is right in contrast to its opposite ἀριστερός (§1066). He reports the Homeric words in the same terms: θηλύτεραι γυναῖκες implies a comparison with men (§1082 b), and originally -τερος had no other force than to contrast one idea with another (§313 b). Monro also states that the comparative is sometimes a softened way of expressing the notion of the positive (Monro §122).
23. Read the Homeric genitive as an ablative. Monro places the genitive with adjectives of comparison under the ablatival genitive, where it expresses the point from which the higher degree of a quality is separated, and groups it with the genitive with verbs of excelling and falling behind (Monro §152). Monro also records ἠέ and ἤ as the particle used after comparatives, and after verbs implying comparison such as βούλομαι and φθάνω (Monro §340).
24. Where ἤ is not comparing degrees, read it as marking difference. Comparative ἤ stands after comparatives where the genitive or a preposition is not used, and after words indicating difference or diversity or having a comparative force, ἄλλος, ἕτερος, ἄλλως, διάφορος, διαφέρειν, ἐναντίος, διπλάσιος, πρίν (§2863). After τί or a negative, ἤ may be used without ἄλλος (§2863 a); it is often used after verbs of willing and choosing (§2863 b); and if two clauses connected by ἤ have the same verb it may be omitted in the clause following ἤ (§2863 c). Smyth places these comparative clauses with ἤ among the disjunctive coördinated sentences (§2462).

## 4. Translation guidance

- A comparative with the genitive is English than: σοφώτερος ἐμοῦ is "wiser than I" (§1069), and Goodell renders the same shape "This man here is wiser than I" (Goodell §517).
- The genitive often has to be expanded into an English clause: πλείοσι ναυσὶ τῶν Ἀθηναίων is "with more ships than the Athenians" (§1433).
- A comparative of degree of difference takes by in English, or nothing at all: κεφαλῇ ἐλᾱ́ττων is "a head shorter", literally by the head (§1513).
- ἢ κατά and ἢ ὥστε are English too . . . to: φοβοῦμαι μή τι μεῖζον ἢ ὥστε φέρειν δύνασθαι κακὸν τῇ πόλει συμβῇ is "I fear lest there should befall the State an evil too great for it to be able to bear" (§1079). Goodell's ἐλάττω ἔχοντα δύναμιν ἢ ὥστε τοὺς φίλους ὠφελεῖν is "having too little force to aid his friends", literally less than as for aiding (Goodell §566).
- A positive with an infinitive can carry the same too: μακρὸν ἂν εἴη μοι λέγειν is "it would take too long for me to state" (§1063).
- A softening comparative is English rather or somewhat: ἀμελέστερον ἐπορεύετο is "he proceeded rather carelessly" (§1082 d).
- A superlative with ὅτι or ὡς is as as possible: ὅτι τάχιστα is "as quickly as possible" (§1086), and ἤγαγον συμμάχους ὁπόσους πλείστους ἐδυνάμην is "I brought the very largest number of allies I could" (§1087).
- ἐν τοῖς with a superlative is very: ἐν τοῖς πλεῖσται δὴ νῆες is "the very largest number of ships" (§1089), and μάλιστα with it gives οἱ μάλιστα ἀνοητότατοι "the very stupidest" (§1090).
- A reflexive comparison needs than before or than usual in English: αὐτοὶ αὑτῶν εὐμαθέστεροι γίγνονται is "they learn more easily than before" (§1078).
- ὅσῳ with a comparative is the more, the more: καὶ χαλεπώτεροι ἔσονται ὅσῳ νεώτεροί εἰσιν is "and they will be the more severe the younger they are" (§2470).
- A superlative with the genitive may need both halves in English: ἀνὴρ ἐπιεικὴς υἱὸν ἀπολέσᾱς οἴσει ῥᾷστα τῶν ἄλλων is "a reasonable man will bear the loss of a son more easily than other men", and most easily of all men (§1434).
- The positive with οὕτω and ὥσπερ expresses equality, and ἧττον before the positive expresses inferiority: οὕτως ἀγαθὸς ὥσπερ καὶ καλός is as good as handsome (§324).
- μᾶλλον ἤ after a positive is rather than: προθῡ́μως μᾶλλον ἢ φίλως is "more prompt than kindly" (§1065).
- A positive with the genitive of the same adjective is a superlative in poetry: κακὰ κακῶν is "woe of woe" (§1064).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading every comparative as a comparison. It may be used merely as an intensive and not differ essentially from the positive (§1067), it may stand alone with what is exceeded indicated by the sense only (§1082 a), and in Homer the suffix is often contrastive rather than gradual (Monro §122).
- Taking μᾶλλον for a separate word of degree when it is the comparison itself. When either form can be used, the one with μᾶλλον or μάλιστα is more emphatic, and Thucydides sometimes uses πλέον or τὸ πλέον instead of μᾶλλον (§1068). When the positive precedes, μᾶλλον alone may stand for the comparative (§1066 a).
- Expecting ὡς to mean than. ὡς for ἤ is rare, and suspected by some (§1071).
- Reading a genitive after a comparative as possessive. It is the standard or point of departure from which the comparison is made, and often expresses a condensed comparison when actions are compared (§1431); the same genitive follows multiplicatives in -πλοῦς and -πλάσιος (§1432).
- Missing the compendious comparison and comparing the wrong two things. The possessor, rather than the object possessed, may be put in the genitive after a comparative (§1076).
- Supplying an ἤ that Greek left out before a numeral, and then declining πλέον to match. It does not alter its case and number (§1074).
- Treating πρότερος and ὕστερος as though they had positives. They are defectives, derived from prepositions or adverbs (§320), and with two the comparative exhausts all the degrees of comparison, so πρότερος and πρῶτος, ἑκάτερος and ἕκαστος are carefully to be distinguished (§1085 b).
- Reading a superlative without an article as the highest degree. Without the article it is the absolute superlative, a very high degree of the quality (§1085).
- Reading a comparative in the neuter as an adjective when it is an adverb. In adverbs derived from adjectives the comparative is the same as the neuter singular of the comparative adjective and the superlative the same as the neuter plural of the superlative adjective (§345), and adverbs of place in ω keep ω, ἄνω giving ἀνωτέρω and ἀνωτάτω (§345 a).
- Forgetting that comparatives are made from nouns and pronouns too. In poetry and sometimes in prose they are formed from substantives and pronouns, βασιλεύτερος "more kingly" and κύντερος "more doglike" (§321), and Monro reads such words as ones where the stem is that of a substantive, so that the adjectival character is given by the suffix (Monro §122).
- Reading a ὥσπερ clause as though its verb were missing by accident. The verb is commonly omitted when it is the same as the verb of the leading clause (§2464), and the subject may be attracted into the case of the other member of the comparison (§2465).
- Taking ὥσπερ ἂν εἰ as two constructions to be resolved separately. It is one combination of comparison and condition, and the ellipsis was usually unconscious (§2478); with a participle it is sometimes used with much the same force as ὥσπερ alone, the εἰ being added by a confusion of constructions (§2480 a).
- Looking for ἄν in a Homeric simile. ὡς is common in Homer with the subjunctive without ἄν (§2483), and GMT records that except in a few cases of ὡς ὅτ᾽ ἄν neither ἄν nor κέ is found there (GMT §543).
- Reading ἤ after ἄλλος as a comparison of degree. It marks difference, ἄλλα ἢ τὰ γενόμενα "things different from what occurred" (§2863).
