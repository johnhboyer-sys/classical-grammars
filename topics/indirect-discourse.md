# Indirect Discourse

Status: drafted 2026-09-07 on the pattern of `topics/genitive-absolute.md`.
Every claim carries a section citation; `scripts/verify_topic_pages.py`
checks them. `topic: indirect` names the derived record in
`data/smyth/topics.json`; that record is the index headword "Indirect",
which covers indirect discourse, indirect questions and the indirect
reflexive together.

## 1. Quick answer

The words or thoughts of a person may be quoted in direct or indirect form after verbs, or other expressions, of saying or thinking (§2589). In an indirect quotation the words or thoughts are given at second hand with certain modifications to indicate that they are reported (§2591). The leading verb decides the construction: verbs of saying take either ὅτι or ὡς and a finite verb, or the infinitive; most verbs of thinking and believing take the infinitive; most verbs of knowing, perceiving, hearing, showing take the participle (§2592). After primary tenses the original mood and tense are retained, except that the person of the verb may be changed (§2598). After secondary tenses, primary tenses of the indicative and all subjunctives may be changed to the same tense of the optative, but an indicative denoting unreality is retained (§2599). The change from direct to indirect discourse is almost always a change of mood, not of tense (§2606). The tense of the reported verb is not always the tense the speaker used: a present optative may represent the imperfect as well as the present (§2611 b), the imperfect passes into the present optative and the pluperfect into the perfect optative when there is no doubt that a past tense stood in the direct form (§2623 b), and a past tense of historical narration may be inserted as the writer's statement of fact where the direct discourse had a present or perfect (§2624).

## 2. Recognition

- ὅτι or ὡς with the indicative or optative after verbs of saying, knowing, perceiving, showing, and after verbs of emotion (§2577). The other conjunctions are διότι, ὅπως (rarely), and the poetic οὕνεκα and ὁθούνεκα (§2578).
- ὅτι introducing what is in fact a direct quotation, with the value of quotation marks: οἱ δὲ εἶπον ὅτι ἱκανοί ἐσμεν "but they said (that) 'we are ready'" (§2590 a). GMT collects the same use and notes that ὅτι or ὡς here cannot be expressed in English (GMT §711).
- An infinitive, with or without an accusative, after φημί, λέγω, ὁμολογῶ, ὑπισχνοῦμαι, ὄμνῡμι and the rest (§2017), or after ἡγοῦμαι, οἴομαι, δοκῶ, νομίζω, ἐλπίζω (§2018). The finite verb of the quoted sentence becomes the infinitive, and Κῦρος νῑκᾷ becomes φησὶ Κῦρον νῑκᾶν (§2016).
- A participle after verbs of knowing and showing, οἶδα, γιγνώσκω, μέμνημαι, δηλῶ, ἀγγέλλω: οὐ γὰρ ᾔδεσαν αὐτὸν τεθνηκότα "for they did not know that he was dead" (§2106).
- A participle in the accusative after ὁρῶ, αἰσθάνομαι, ἀκούω, πυνθάνομαι, where the case is itself the clue: in indirect discourse ἀκούω and πυνθάνομαι take the accusative, not the genitive (§2112).
- ὡς with a participle, marking the mental attitude of the subject of the main verb: δῆλος ἦν Κῦρος ὡς σπεύδων (§2120). A participle with ὡς may also follow a verb of thinking or saying that does not take the participle in indirect discourse without ὡς, ὡς τὰ βέλτιστα βουλεύοντες ῑ̓σχῡρίζοντο (§2121). And after verbs that do admit the supplementary participle in indirect discourse, the genitive or accusative absolute with ὡς may stand instead of the participle or of a clause with ὅτι or ὡς (§2122).
- An optative without ἄν in a subordinate clause where a subjunctive with ἄν belongs, because ἐᾱ́ν, ὅταν, ἐπειδάν, ἕως ἄν become εἰ, ὅτε, ἐπειδή, ἕως (§2607).
- An infinitive inside a temporal or relative clause, by attraction to an infinitive standing in the principal clause after a verb of saying (§2631).
- Indirect questions, which have the constructions of indirect discourse (§2596): the interrogatives of the direct question may be retained, but it is more common to use the indefinite relatives, which in interrogative sentences are used only in indirect questions, ἠρώτων ὅ τι ἐστὶ τὸ πρᾶγμα, ἠρώτων αὐτὸν τὸ στράτευμα ὁπόσον εἴη, ᾔδει δὲ οὐδεὶς ὅποι στρατεύουσιν (§2664), and simple questions are introduced by εἰ whether, less often by ἆρα (§2671).
- The pronouns. οἷ and σφίσι are only indirect reflexives (§1228 b); οὗ, σφίσι and the oblique cases of αὐτός appear when the subordinate clause does not form part of the thought of the principal subject (§1229).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Find the leading verb and read the construction off it (§2592). A verb of saying may take ὅτι or ὡς or the infinitive, and in most cases the choice is optional with the writer (§2579). A verb of thinking almost always takes the infinitive, ὡς occurs, and ὅτι is very rare (§2580). A verb of intellectual perception usually takes ὅτι or ὡς, less often the participle, which is normal after verbs of physical perception (§2581).
2. Ask whether that verb is primary or secondary, because nothing else is decided until you know. After primary tenses the mood and tense of the direct form stand unchanged (§2598, §2602). The historical present counts as a secondary tense (§2627 a).
3. Reconstruct the direct form with three rules held together. No verb ever becomes subjunctive by reason of indirect discourse (§2609). No verb must become optative, since even after a secondary tense indicatives and subjunctives may be retained for vividness (§2610). The optative may represent either the indicative or the subjunctive of the direct form (§2611 a). A present optative can stand for the present indicative, the imperfect, the present subjunctive with or without ἄν, or the present optative (§2611 b).
4. Read ἄν before you read the mood. ἄν of the direct form is retained in indirect discourse except when a dependent subjunctive with ἄν becomes optative after a secondary tense (§2607). GMT states the rule from both sides: ἄν is never joined with a verb in indirect discourse unless it stood also in the direct form, and never omitted if it was used there (GMT §667). So an optative with ἄν in the text was an optative with ἄν in the direct form, and it is regularly retained after ὅτι or ὡς (§2625).
5. Handle the past tenses separately, because the ambiguity is in those tenses. The potential indicative with ἄν and the indicative of unreality always remain unchanged, to prevent confusion with the optative of the direct form (§2623 a). The imperfect and pluperfect in simple sentences usually remain unchanged to prevent ambiguity, and in subordinate clauses both tenses are retained unaltered (§2623 b). The aorist indicative in a principal clause may become aorist optative, but in a subordinate clause it remains unchanged to avoid ambiguity with the aorist optative, which usually represents the aorist subjunctive (§2623 c). GMT agrees and adds the exception: where no ambiguity can arise, chiefly in causal sentences, the aorist indicative may follow the general principle (GMT §693).
6. When you meet an imperfect that makes no sense as an imperfect, suspect an inserted statement of fact. Where the present or perfect indicative would have stood in the direct discourse, a past tense of historical narration is often used as a statement of fact by the writer from his own point of view (§2624). Such inserted statements are often difficult to distinguish from indicatives in indirect discourse, and the two forms may occur in the same sentence (§2624 b). GMT documents the tense usage itself, the imperfect and pluperfect standing for the present and perfect after past tenses in Homer and, with the context always making the represented tense clear, in Attic too (GMT §674). GMT §691 and GMT §701 judge that such clauses are really, or simply, not included in the indirect discourse.
7. If the moods are mixed, do not emend and do not force one reading over the sentence. For the sake of variation a mood of the direct form may be used in the same sentence with a mood of the indirect: the main verb may stay direct while the subordinate verb becomes optative, or the reverse (§2632). GMT allows the dependent verbs to be changed to the optative even when the leading verb retains the indicative, and says this may give rise to a great variety of constructions in the same sentence (GMT §690).
8. Work the subordinate clauses by their own rule. After a primary tense all subordinate verbs retain the original mood and tense (§2618). After a secondary tense all subordinate verbs in the present, future, or perfect indicative, and all subjunctives, are usually changed to the corresponding tenses of the optative, or they are retained, and subjunctives with ἄν lose ἄν (§2619). Subordinate verbs in the imperfect, aorist, or pluperfect indicative, and all optatives, remain unchanged (§2620). Smyth's table at §2621 sets out the conditional sentences case by case after εἶπεν ὅτι and after ἔφη.
9. If no verb of saying is present, ask whether indirect discourse is implied. It is implied in the case of any subordinate clause which, though not depending formally on a verb of saying or thinking, contains the past thought of another person and not a statement of the writer or speaker, and it appears only after secondary tenses (§2622). Six kinds are listed there: conditional clauses whose conclusion is implied in the leading verb (§2622 a), temporal clauses implying purpose or expectation (§2622 b), causal clauses (§2622 c), ordinary relative clauses (§2622 d), clauses depending on an infinitive after a verb of will or desire (§2622 e), and clauses of purpose and object clauses after verbs of effort, which admit the alternative constructions of indirect discourse (§2622 f). GMT states the same principle for any single dependent clause which indirectly expresses the past thought of any person, even that of the speaker himself (GMT §694). Its list, though, runs in a different order and its kinds do not match Smyth's one for one. GMT's first head is clauses depending on the infinitive after verbs of wishing, commanding and advising, which is Smyth's last (GMT §695); its second is a clause containing a protasis whose apodosis is implied in the past leading verb, which is Smyth's first (GMT §696); its third, a protasis depending on a past verb of emotion like θαυμάζω or αἰσχύνομαι, is in Smyth too, under his first head rather than as a separate kind: §2622 a opens after a verb of emotion with οἱ δ᾽ ᾤκτῑρον εἰ ἁλώσοιντο, which is the sentence GMT prints for that head (GMT §697; §2622 a). The temporal (GMT §698), the past causal (GMT §699) and the ordinary relative sentences (GMT §700) follow after that.
10. Read the negative last, and let it correct you. The same negative used in the direct discourse is commonly kept (§2608), and after ὅτι or ὡς it is retained (§2710). But with the infinitive and participle μή sometimes takes the place of οὐ (§2608): verbs of saying and thinking take μή in emphatic declarations and expressions of thought which involve a wish that the utterance may hold good (§2723), and verbs of hoping, expecting, promising, trusting, agreeing, testifying and swearing take μή to indicate strong assurance, confidence, and resolve (§2725). GMT lists the same verbs and notes that even φημί, λέγω, νομίζω, ἡγοῦμαι occasionally take μή (GMT §685), and that the participle shows the same exceptions (GMT §688).
11. Decide whether the infinitive is in indirect discourse at all. GMT's test has two conditions, both required; if either fails, the test includes cases it is meant to exclude. The infinitive must depend on a verb implying thought or the expression of thought, one of the class of verba sentiendi et declarandi; and the thought as originally conceived must also have been expressed by some tense of the indicative, with or without ἄν, or of the optative with ἄν, so that it can be transferred without change of tense. βούλεται ἐλθεῖν is therefore not in indirect discourse while φησὶν ἐλθεῖν is, and GMT grants that one might possibly make ἐλθεῖν in βούλομαι ἐλθεῖν represent ἔλθοιμι, which is exactly what the leading verb and the ἄν keep out (GMT §684). Take the line as a working convenience rather than a firm boundary: GMT closes by saying that it is impossible to say where a Greek would have drawn it, or to be sure that he would have drawn any line at all (GMT §684). In Smyth the same boundary appears as the rule that the infinitive expressing command, warning, or wish after a verb of will or desire is not itself in indirect discourse, and that its negative is μή (§2622 e).
12. If the direct form must have been an imperative, look for the periphrasis. The imperative is commonly replaced in indirect discourse by a periphrasis with χρῆναι (§2612), and the idea of an imperative or hortatory subjunctive may be set forth in the infinitive as a statement about what ought to be (§2633).
13. If the passage is Homer, drop the Attic rule. Except in indirect questions, the optative of indirect discourse is unknown to Homer, who uses in the dependent clause the same past tense that would have been used in an independent clause, from the point of view of the speaker (§2624 c). GMT documents the same stage of the language (GMT §671, GMT §674), and Monro reports that the mood in clauses with ὅ, ὅτι, ὅ τε is the indicative, not the optative as in some Attic uses (Monro §270). Homeric alternative clauses with ἠέ . . . ἦε and the optative are the translation of a subjunctive into indirect discourse, a doubt or deliberation thrown back into the past (Monro §302).
14. Expect the construction to change in a long quotation. Long sentences, or a series of sentences, in indirect discourse depending on a single verb of saying are uncongenial to the animated character of Greek, which resists the formal regularity of Latin (§2634). The devices are named: ἔφη is repeated (§2634 a), the indirect form is abandoned for the direct (§2634 b), ἔφη χρῆναι or ἐκέλευσε is inserted or repeated (§2634 c). Indirect discourse may also be introduced by ὅτι or ὡς and then pass into the infinitive as if the introductory verb had required the infinitive (§2628). Goodell states the same preference for varying a long quotation (Goodell §663) and the same shift into the infinitive when the introductory verb of saying is no longer near (Goodell §659).

## 4. Translation guidance

- English does not have the optative of indirect discourse. Put back the tense of the direct form and apply the English sequence of tenses: ἔγνωσαν ὅτι κενὸς ὁ φόβος εἴη "they recognized that their fear was groundless", where the direct form was ἐστί (§2615 a).
- A retained indicative does not mean more certainty. διῆλθε λόγος ὅτι διώκει αὐτοὺς Κῦρος is "a report spread that Cyrus was pursuing them" (§2615 b); the retention of the mood of direct discourse lies solely in the option of the writer, and the vivid form reproduces the time and situation in which the quoted words were used (§2613). GMT says that the indicative here is merely a more vivid form of expression than the optative, with no difference in meaning (GMT §670).
- The tenses of the infinitive in indirect discourse denote the same time relative to that of the leading verb as the corresponding tenses of the indicative in direct discourse (§1866). Present for present, and also for imperfect; perfect for perfect, and also for pluperfect (§1866 a). So ἔφη ἢ ἄξειν Λακεδαιμονίους ἢ αὐτοῦ ἀποκτενεῖν is "he said that he would either bring the Lacedaemonians or kill them on the spot", direct ἄξω, ἀποκτενῶ (§1867 c).
- The participle carries the same values: οὐ γὰρ ᾔδεσαν αὐτὸν τεθνηκότα "for they did not know that he was dead", direct ὅτι τέθνηκε (§1874 d). Its English is a that-clause, and the whole construction represents a dependent statement (§2106).
- ὡς with a participle becomes "be assured that", "in the belief that", "on the assumption that": ὡς μηδὲν εἰδότ᾽ ἴσθι με "be assured that I know nothing" (§2120); ὡς πολέμου ὄντος παρ᾽ ῡ̔μῶν ἀπαγγελῶ; "shall I report from you that there is war?" (§2122). GMT gives the same reading and its reason: ὡς implies that the thought of the participle is expressed as that of the leading subject (GMT §916, GMT §917).
- ὡς rather than ὅτι after a verb of saying is often a signal to the translator. ὡς is apparently preferred when a writer wishes to mark a statement as an opinion, a pretext, as untrue, and so when the main clause or the subordinate clause is negative (§2579).
- An imperative of the direct form comes through as an infinitive, and English restores the command: ἀπηγόρευε μηδένα βάλλειν "he forbade any one to shoot", direct μηδεὶς βαλλέτω (§2633 a); ἔφη χρῆναι πλεῖν ἐπὶ Συρᾱκούσᾱς "he said that they ought to sail to Syracuse" (§2633 b).
- δῆλον ὅτι, οἶδ᾽ ὅτι, εὖ ἴσθι ὅτι are so often parenthetical that they become mere formal expressions and ὅτι loses all conjunctive force: render "evidently", "surely", "be assured" (§2585). GMT translates οἶδ᾽ ὅτι the same way, "I am sure" (GMT §705).
- Indirect questions take the English of an ordinary subordinate question: ἠρώτων αὐτοὺς τίνες εἶεν "they asked them who they were", direct τίνες ἐστέ; (§2664). The moods and tenses follow the same rules as govern clauses in indirect discourse, and a past indicative with ἄν always remains unchanged (§2677).
- εἰ is "whether" (§2671). After verbs expressing uncertainty or doubt it can carry a negative force, "whether ... not": εἰ μὲν δὴ δίκαια ποιήσω, οὐκ οἶδα "I don't know whether I shall do what is right" (§2671 a).
- Dependent exclamations are best translated with a participle in English. Verbs of emotion are often followed by a clause with οἷος, ὅσος, ὡς, and English generally introduces such clauses by considering, thinking, upon the reflection how (§2687).
- Goodell says that a long quotation carried through strictly in the indirect form, as is so often done in Latin, would have seemed in Greek monotonous and dull, and English resembles Greek rather than Latin in this (Goodell §663).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading every optative as potential. All optatives with or without ἄν of the direct form are retained (§2611), so an optative with ἄν stood in the direct form as it stands here. An optative without ἄν, on the other hand, has three possible sources: after a secondary tense it may represent the indicative, or it may represent the subjunctive (§2611 a), or it may be an optative of the direct form retained unchanged (§2620).
- Making the retained indicative mean something the optative would not. The retention of the mood of direct discourse lies solely in the option of the writer or speaker (§2613). GMT calls the indicative merely a more vivid form of expression, with no difference in meaning (GMT §670).
- Translating an imperfect as an imperfect when the direct form had a present. That is the inserted statement of fact, used by the writer from his own point of view (§2624). GMT gives the Attic examples of the imperfect and pluperfect standing for the present and perfect of the direct form (GMT §674).
- Turning an aorist optative in a subordinate clause into an aorist indicative. There the aorist indicative is normally retained, precisely to avoid ambiguity with the aorist optative that represents an aorist subjunctive (§2623 c).
- Expecting the optative after every secondary tense. No verb must become optative by reason of indirect discourse (§2610), and after secondary tenses the indicative is freely retained (§2615 b).
- Restoring or deleting ἄν on your own judgment. ἄν of the direct form is retained except when a dependent subjunctive with ἄν becomes optative (§2607), and it is occasionally retained even then, in temporal and relative sentences, though many scholars expel it (§2626). GMT records that ἄν is occasionally retained with relatives and temporal particles even when the subjunctive to which it belonged has been changed to the optative (GMT §702).
- Treating every infinitive after a verb as a reported statement. Verbs of will or desire take an infinitive that is not in indirect discourse and whose negative is μή (§2622 e). GMT's test has two halves and both must hold: the infinitive depends on a verb implying thought or the expression of thought, and the thought as first conceived would have been an indicative with or without ἄν, or an optative with ἄν (GMT §684).
- Reading μή with an infinitive as proof that the clause is not a statement. Verbs of hoping, promising, swearing, agreeing, trusting and testifying take μή where οὐ would be expected, and the μή marks strong assurance (§2725); verbs of saying and thinking take it in emphatic declarations (§2723).
- Reading ὅτι as "because" where it is "that", or the reverse. διότι was originally "on account of this, that", that is "because", and only later came to mean "that" (§2578 b).
- Translating a direct quotation as reported speech because ὅτι stands before it. There ὅτι has the value of quotation marks, and the first person of the original stays (§2590 a).
- Taking οἷ or σφίσι as direct reflexives. ἑαυτοῦ and the rest are either direct or indirect reflexives; οἷ and σφίσι are only indirect (§1228 b), and the oblique cases of αὐτός appear when the subordinate clause does not form part of the thought of the principal subject (§1229).
- Applying the Attic optative rule to Homer. Outside indirect questions the optative of indirect discourse is unknown to him (§2624 c), and Monro finds the indicative in the ὅ, ὅτι, ὅ τε clauses (Monro §270).
- Reading a participle after ὁρῶ or ἀκούω as always a reported statement. When these verbs are not in indirect discourse they denote physical perception, and with ἀκούω and πυνθάνομαι the participle then stands in the genitive; in indirect discourse it stands in the accusative (§2112).
- Assuming the negative οὐ has been changed when the leading verb is one of will. In προεῖπεν ὡς μηδεὶς κῑνήσοιτο ἐκ τῆς τάξεως the μηδείς is used because the main verb denotes a command (§2710 a).
- Expecting one long consistent oratio obliqua. Greek breaks it up (§2634), and the whole clause may begin with ὅτι and continue with an infinitive (§2628).
