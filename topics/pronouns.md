## 1. Quick answer

Most of the trouble a reader has with Greek pronouns is not the paradigms. It is that Greek leaves out the pronouns English needs and uses one word where English uses three. Greek usually omits the nominative of the personal pronoun except when emphatic, as in contrasts, so ῡ̔μεῖς ἐμοὶ οὐ θέλετε πείθεσθαι is emphatic and a plain "you do not wish to obey me" is not (§1190). Goodell says the endings of the verb indicate the person clearly enough, so ἐγώ, σύ, ἡμεῖς, ὑμεῖς are used only when emphasized (Goodell §557). αὐτός has three distinct uses, self, same, and the oblique him, her, it, them (§1204). Goodell sorts the same three meanings by position, standing alone in the oblique cases, standing after the article, and standing without the article, agreeing in case with a noun or pronoun, or standing alone in the nominative (Goodell §199). Learn to tell those three apart by position, and half of the case work on this page is done.

## 2. Recognition

- A nominative personal pronoun where the verb ending already had it. Where there is no contrast the addition of the pronoun may strengthen the verb: σὺ δὲ τοὐντεῦθεν λέγε (§1191). The accented forms ἐμοῦ, ἐμοί and ἐμέ appear when emphatic and usually after prepositions (§1192).
- No third-person nominative pronoun at all. Its place is taken by ἐκεῖνος of absent persons, by ὅδε and οὗτος of present ones, by ὁ μέν and ὁ δέ at the beginning of a sentence, and by αὐτός in contrasts (§1194). Goodell says the third-person personal pronoun is rare in Attic prose, that οἷ and σφίσι occur oftenest and are reflexive, and that αὐτός stands in its place in the oblique cases (Goodell §197).
- αὐτός in the predicate position, or alone in the nominative. Then it is intensive: αὐτοὶ τὴν γῆν ἔσχον they seized the land themselves, and αὐτὸς ὁ ἀνήρ or ὁ ἀνὴρ αὐτός the man himself (§1206).
- αὐτός after the article, in the attributive position. Then it means same: ὁ αὐτὸς ἀνήρ the same man, τοῦ αὐτοῦ θέρους in the same summer (§1210).
- αὐτός unemphatic and alone in an oblique case. Then it is simply him, her, it, them: ἐκέλευον αὐτὴν ἀπιέναι they ordered her to depart (§1212).
- A reflexive referring to the chief word, usually the subject, of the clause it stands in, and less commonly to the object: γνῶθι σεαυτόν learn to know thyself, σφάττει ἑαυτήν she kills herself (§1218).
- A reflexive in a dependent clause pointing back at the subject of the main clause: Ὀρέστης ἔπεισεν Ἀθηναίους ἑαυτὸν κατάγειν, Orestes persuaded the Athenians to restore him (§1225).
- ἀλλήλους and its cases, for the reciprocal: ἠσπάζοντο ἀλλήλους they mutually embraced (§1277).
- ὅδε, οὗτος and ἐκεῖνος as demonstratives, with the deictic ending on the first two: οὑτοσῑ́ and ὁδῑ́ are emphatic, deictic forms meaning this here (§1240).
- τίς substantive or adjective in a question: τίς ἀνήρ what man? (§1262).
- Enclitic τὶς or τὶ, which cannot stand at the beginning of a sentence, meaning some one or any, some (§1266).
- A relative agreeing with its antecedent in gender, number and person, but taking its case from its own clause: λαβὼν τοὺς ἱππέᾱς οἳ ἦσαν αὐτῷ, taking the cavalry which he had (§2501).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. For αὐτός, look at the article first. In the predicate position with a substantive, or in agreement with a pronoun, it is intensive and means self (§1206); after the article, in the attributive position, in any case, it means same (§1210). Gildersleeve says αὐτός, along with ἄμφω and ἀμφότεροι, ἕκαστος, ἑκάτερος, and the three great demonstratives ὅδε, οὗτος and ἐκεῖνος, assumes the predicative position, of which the first position is the normal one and the second the afterthought position (Gildersleeve §653).
2. If αὐτός is intensive, expect an English word other than "self" as often as not. Smyth's special renderings are by itself, in itself, unaided, alone, as in αὐτὴ ἡ ἀλήθεια the naked truth; just or merely, as in αὐτὸ τὸ δέον just what we want; voluntarily; and, with an ordinal, δέκατος αὐτός, himself the tenth (§1209). Gildersleeve renders αὐτὴ . . . ἡ παρασκευή four ways at once, the armament itself, of itself, the very armament, the mere armament (Gildersleeve §654).
3. If αὐτός is oblique and unemphatic, look for the noun it refers to. αὐτοῦ and the other forms usually refer to a preceding noun in the anaphoric use, as in καλέσᾱς δὲ Δάμνιππον λέγω πρὸς αὐτὸν τάδε (§1214). But they become emphatic in a main clause when a relative clause referring to them follows, and are not emphatic when the relative clause precedes (§1216).
4. For a reflexive, ask whether the clause it stands in has the subject it refers to. If it does, that is the direct reflexive, and it is regular in prose when the pronoun refers emphatically to the subject and is the direct object of the main verb, so ἐμαυτὸν ἐπαινῶ and not ἐμέ (§1219). If it does not, and the reference is to the subject of the main clause, that is the indirect reflexive (§1225).
5. When two subjects are involved, the grammar does not decide which is meant. When the subject of the leading clause is not the same as the subject of the subordinate clause or of the accusative with the infinitive, the context must decide to which subject the reflexive pronoun refers (§1226).
6. If you expected an indirect reflexive and found something else, check the two substitutes. Instead of the indirect ἑαυτοῦ there may stand an oblique case of αὐτός, or one of the forms of the third personal pronoun, chiefly οἷ and σφίσι (§1228). These are used when the subordinate clause does not form part of the thought of the principal subject, which is usual in subordinate indicative clauses and very common in ὅτι and ὡς clauses (§1229).
7. Read αὐτός beside a personal pronoun as emphasis and not as a reflexive. αὐτός may be added to a personal pronoun for emphasis, and ἐμὲ αὐτόν and αὐτόν με are not reflexive like ἐμαυτόν (§1236). The force added is to differentiate, so ἐμὲ αὐτόν means myself and no other while ἐμαυτόν means simply myself (§1237).
8. Watch the order in the plural, because it decides the reading. ἡμῶν αὐτῶν may be either emphatic or reflexive, αὐτῶν ἡμῶν is emphatic only, and σφῶν αὐτῶν is only reflexive (§1233).
9. For a plural reflexive that looks reciprocal, it probably is. The plural forms of the reflexive pronouns are often used for the reciprocal ἀλλήλων, as in ἡμῖν αὐτοῖς διαλεξόμεθα, we will converse with one another (§1231). Greek has two further ways of saying it: the middle forms, and the repetition of a substantive, as ἀνὴρ ἕλεν ἄνδρα (§1278).
10. Use the reciprocal, not the reflexive, when the sense is each with himself. The reciprocal must be used when the idea is expressed or implied, and Smyth's contrasting pair is οὔτε γὰρ ἑαυτοῖς οὔτε ἀλλήλοις ὁμολογοῦσιν (§1232).
11. For a demonstrative, ask whether the object is near or remote. οὗτος and ὅδε usually refer to something near in place, time or thought, and ἐκεῖνος to something more remote (§1240). ὅδε points with emphasis to an object in the immediate (actual or mental) vicinity of the speaker or to something just noticed, and in the drama it announces the approach of a new actor, as ἀλλ᾽ ὅδε βασιλεὺς χωρεῖ (§1241).
12. For a demonstrative that refers to a part of the text rather than to a thing, ask whether it refers forward or back. οὗτος generally refers to what precedes and ὅδε to what follows, so τοιάδε ἔλεξεν is he spoke as follows and τοιαῦτα εἰπών is after speaking thus (§1245). The rule is a tendency and not a law: οὗτος sometimes refers forward, especially in the neuter, and ὅδε, though much less often, refers back, as in ὧδε θάπτουσιν they bury them thus, as described before (§1247).
13. If two things are contrasted, οὗτος takes the nearer. When reference is made to one of two contrasted objects, οὗτος refers to the object nearer to the speaker's thought, or to the more important, or to the one last mentioned (§1261). ἐκεῖνος refers back, rarely forward, and implies remoteness in place, time or thought, and it can simply point, as in νῆες ἐκεῖναι ἐπιπλέουσιν, yonder are ships sailing up to us (§1257). Goodell prints νῆες ἐκεῖναι ἐπιπλέουσιν among the demonstratives in the predicate that English renders here and there (Goodell §561).
14. For an interrogative, distinguish the class question from the nature question. τίς asks a question concerning the class and τί concerning the nature of a thing, as in εἰπὲ τίς ἡ τέχνη (§1265). Smyth uses τί for τίνα as the predicate of a neuter plural subject when the general result is sought and the subject is considered as a unit, as ταῦτα δὲ τί ἐστιν; while τίνα emphasizes the details (§1264).
15. In an indirect question, expect the indefinite relative rather than the interrogative. The interrogatives are used in direct and in indirect questions, but in indirect questions the indefinite relatives ὅστις and its fellows are generally used instead, as in οὐκ οἶδα ὅ τι ἄν τις χρήσαιτο αὐτοῖς (§1263).
16. For τὶς, decide whether it is quantifying or hedging. In the singular τὶς is used in a collective sense, everybody for anybody, as in ἀλλὰ μῑσεῖ τις ἐκεῖνον, and ἕκαστός τις is generally used in that sense (§1267). With adjectives, adverbs and numerals it may strengthen or weaken an assertion or apologize for a comparison: δεινός τις ἀνήρ a very terrible man, μύωψ τις a sort of gad-fly (§1268). It can also mean somebody of importance, as in τὸ δοκεῖν τινὲς εἶναι, the seeming to be somebody (§1269).
17. For a relative, take agreement from the antecedent and case from the clause (§2501). Then ask whether the antecedent is definite or indefinite, because that governs the mood and the negative: with a definite antecedent the relative clause takes any form that occurs in an independent sentence, with οὐ as the negative unless the particular construction requires μή, and with an indefinite antecedent it commonly has a conditional force and, if negative, takes μή (§2505).
18. If the relative is compound, that is usually the same question again. When the antecedent is definite the simple relatives ὅς, οἷος and ὅσος are used, and when it is indefinite the compound relatives ὅστις, ὁποῖος and ὁπόσος, though the simple ones are often employed instead (§2508).
19. If the relative has no antecedent, supply one and give it the case its own clause wants. The demonstrative antecedent is often omitted, either when it is in the same case as the relative or in a different one, and the omission occurs when the antecedent expresses the general idea of person or thing: καλὸν τὸ θνῄσκειν οἷς ὕβριν τὸ ζῆν φέρει (§2509). Where the relative has taken the antecedent's case instead, that is attraction, and it has its own page.

## 4. Translation guidance

- A Greek possessive of the first or second person is the equivalent of the possessive genitive of the personal pronoun, ἐμός for μου and σός for σου, and the article decides definiteness: ὁ ἐμὸς φίλος is my friend and φίλος ἐμός is a friend of mine (§1196).
- A possessive may need an English preposition rather than a possessive, because it can carry the force of an objective genitive: φιλίᾳ τῇ ἐμῇ is out of friendship for me (§1197).
- The personal pronouns of the first and second persons sometimes stand where English wants a reflexive, and they are so used generally as subject of the infinitive, ἐμέ and σέ rather than ἐμαυτόν and σεαυτόν (§1223). Smyth's example of the plain reflexive sense is δοκῶ μοι ἀδύνατος εἶναι, I think I am unable (§1222).
- A third-person reflexive can render an English first or second person: δεῖ ἡμᾶς ἀνερέσθαι ἑαυτούς is we must ask ourselves, and παράγγελλε τοῖς ἑαυτοῦ is give orders to your men (§1230).
- Where αὐτός stands with a reflexive, English usually wants only one word. αὐτοὶ ἐφ᾽ ἑαυτῶν ἐχώρουν is they marched by themselves (§1235).
- The reflexives of the first and second persons are not used in a subordinate clause to refer to the subject of the main clause (§1221).
- τὶς with a substantive is often just the English indefinite article, and to express indefiniteness of nature it becomes a sort of (§1267).
- Goodell gives three renderings of nominative αὐτός: Αὐτὸς ξυνειδώς φησιν; is does he say it of his own knowledge?, ἥξει γὰρ αὐτά is things will come of themselves, and αὐτὸς ἔφη is he himself (the master) said it (Goodell §558).
- A demonstrative in the nominative may be a call, not a subject. Goodell prints Οὗτος, τί ποιεῖς; as you there, what are you doing? (Goodell §561).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading αὐτός in the nominative as "he". Smyth lists αὐτός among the words that replace the nominative of the third-person pronoun in contrasts (§1194), but he also says that in the nominative standing alone αὐτός emphasizes the word understood and is not a personal pronoun (§1206). Goodell carries the same qualification: standing alone in the nominative αὐτός always means ipse, emphasising some word understood (Goodell §558).
- Reading ὁ αὐτός as "he himself". After the article αὐτός means same in any case (§1210), and the same word without the article and in the predicate position means self (§1206).
- Assuming the three uses are equally old. Smyth says only the first two are Homeric, and that in Homer αὐτός denotes the principal person or thing in opposition to what is subordinate, as αὐτὸν καὶ θεράποντα, the man himself and his attendant (§1205). Monro disagrees on the third: he lists as a Homeric use the unemphatic one in which αὐτός is an ordinary anaphoric pronoun of the third person, English he, she, it, and adds the restriction that in that use the pronoun cannot stand at the beginning of a clause or in the nominative (Monro §252). Both accounts leave the emphatic use as the primary one, and Monro's distinguishing sense is the same one Smyth prints, as in αὐτὸς καὶ τοῦ δῶρα, he and his gifts (Monro §252).
- Reading a Homeric αὐτός as a reflexive. Monro says the reflexive use of αὐτός is very rare in Homer, and that Homer's reflexive is ἕο, which is reflexive chiefly after a preposition, as ἀπὸ ἕο and προτὶ οἷ, or when it is reinforced by αὐτός (Monro §252, Monro §253).
- Reading Homer's ἕο and οἷ as reflexives everywhere. Homer uses them as personal pronouns equal to Attic αὐτοῦ and αὐτῷ, in which case they are enclitic, and also as direct or as indirect reflexives (§1195). Monro adds the accent as the test: in the reflexive use the pronoun is orthotone, and in the anaphoric use it is enclitic (Monro §253).
- Taking the pronoun classes as classes of words. Monro divides them by use instead, into deictic, anaphoric and relative, and warns that this is a classification of the uses of pronouns and not of the words or stems themselves, since the same pronoun may be deictic or anaphoric according to the context; he adds that all pronouns are probably deictic in origin and become anaphoric in the course of usage (Monro §247). The Greek grammarians' own terms are the source of ours: they divided the ἀντωνυμίαι into δεικτικαί, pointing, and ἀναφορικαί, referring (Monro §247).
- Expecting Homer's οὗτος to follow Attic usage. In Homer οὗτος is not infrequently deictic, expressing an object present to the speaker but not near him, or connected with him, and it is then chiefly used, like iste in Latin, of what belongs to or concerns the person spoken to, or in a hostile or contemptuous tone, and is regularly used of one of the enemy; more commonly, however, οὗτος is anaphoric, denoting an object already mentioned or known (Monro §251). Smyth's Attic note makes the same point but does not include the hostility: οὗτος iste may refer to a person close at hand, but less vividly (§1241).
- Reading Homeric ὅδε and κεῖνος as if they marked only place. ὅδε is almost purely deictic and marks an object as near the speaker, as Ἕκτορος ἥδε γυνή (Monro §249); κεῖνος points to an object as distant, as κεῖνος ὅ γʼ ἐν θαλάμῳ, and then in an anaphoric use distinguishes what is past or done with, the literal sense of local distance being transferred to remoteness in time or in the order of thought (Monro §250).
- Reading the article in Homer as an article. The article at the beginning of a clause may often be translated either as a demonstrative or as a relative, and it has the character of a relative when the clause it introduces is distinctly subordinate or parenthetical (Monro §262).
- Expecting an interrogative in a Homeric dependent question. Monro says the use of the interrogative in dependent questions is rare in Homer, and that the interrogatives there are τίς, πότερος, πόστος and ποῖος with their adverbs (Monro §248).
- Forgetting how the reflexive and the reciprocal are built. Goodell records that the reflexive pronouns are made by adding αὐτός to the personal pronouns and are needed only in the oblique cases (Goodell §200), and that the reciprocal is formed from ἄλλος by doubling the stem and is not needed in the singular nor in the nominative (Goodell §202).
- Treating a reflexive and a reciprocal as never interchangeable. Smyth notes that they may occur in the same sentence without difference of meaning, and that the reflexive is regularly used when there is a contrast with ἄλλοι, as φθονοῦσιν ἑαυτοῖς μᾶλλον ἢ τοῖς ἄλλοις ἀνθρώποις (§1232).
