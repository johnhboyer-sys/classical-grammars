# Questions

`topics/indirect-discourse.md`. Every claim carries a section citation;
`scripts/verify_topic_pages.py` checks them. `topic: questions` names the

## 1. Quick answer

Questions are either direct (independent) or indirect (dependent): τίς ἔλεξε ταῦτα; "who said this?" beside ἐρωτῶ ὅστις ἔλεξε ταῦτα "I ask who said this" (§2636). A direct question may have the assertive form, with the interrogation expressed simply by the tone of the voice, or it may be introduced by an interrogative pronoun, adjective, adverb, or particle (§2637). Sort it first by what is being asked: Yes and No questions, also called sentence-questions, are asked by the verb and are commonly introduced by an interrogative particle, while pronoun-questions, or word-questions, are asked by an interrogative pronoun, adjective, or adverb (§2638). The particle shows the expected answer. ἦ and ἆρα introduce questions asking merely for information and imply nothing as to the answer expected (§2650), while οὐ, ἆρ᾽ οὐ, οὐκοῦν expect the answer yes (nonne), and μή, ἆρα μή, μῶν expect the answer no (num) (§2651). Deliberative questions ask what is to be done or what was to be done, and what is to be done in the present or future goes into the deliberative subjunctive (§2639). An indirect question is built out of the direct one: indirect questions have the constructions of indirect discourse (§2596), so after a primary tense the mood and tense of the direct question are retained, and after a secondary tense they may be retained or the optative may be used instead (§2677).

## 2. Recognition

- An interrogative pronoun, adjective or adverb at the head of the sentence, with any form the simple sentence can take: τί οὖν κελεύω ποιῆσαι; "what then do I urge you to do?" and πῶς εἶπας; "what (lit. how) did you say?" (§2642). Goodell notes the same double set, since the direct interrogatives are also used in indirect questions (Goodell §621).
- The same interrogative late in the sentence, which is not a fault of order. A question gains in animation and has its emphatic part clearly marked if the interrogative word is placed late: ἡδέα δὲ καλεῖς οὐ τὰ ἡδονῆς μετέχοντα; "you call pleasant, do you not, that which participates in pleasure?" (§2637 a).
- No interrogative word at all. Any form of statement may be used as a direct question, the interrogative meaning indicated only by the context or by placing an emphatic word first: ἐγὼ οὔ φημι; "I say no?" (§2641).
- A particle carrying the expected answer: οὐχ οὕτως ἔλεγες; "did you not say so?" against μή τι νεώτερον ἀγγέλλεις; "no bad news, I hope?" and μῶν τί σε ἀδικεῖ; "surely he has not wronged you, has he?" (§2651 a, §2651 b).
- ἆρα or ἦ with no such implication: ἆρ᾽ εἰμὶ μάντις; "am I a prophet?" and ἦ τέθνηκεν Οἰδίπου πατήρ; "is Oedipus' father really dead?" (§2650).
- πότερον (πότερα) . . . ἤ opening a double question: πότερον δέδρᾱκεν ἢ οὔ; "has he done it or not?" (§2656). ἤ often stands alone without πότερον: ἔλῡε τὴν εἰρήνην ἢ οὔ; "did he break the peace or not?" (§2657).
- A subjunctive in the first person with no ἄν and no conjunction: εἴπωμεν ἢ σῑγῶμεν; "shall we speak or keep silence?" and τί δρᾱ́σω; ποῖ φύγω; "what am I to do? whither shall I fly?" (§1805 a).
- A future indicative doing the same work. The future is often used in deliberative questions: τί ἐροῦμεν ἢ τί φήσομεν; "what shall we say or what shall we propose?" (§1916). Gildersleeve prints the subjunctive and the future side by side in one sentence of Euripides (Gildersleeve §268).
- An indefinite relative where you expect an interrogative. In indirect questions the indefinite relatives ὅστις and the rest are generally used instead of the interrogatives (§1263), as in ἠρώτων ὅ τι ἐστὶ τὸ πρᾶγμα "I asked what the matter was" (§2664).
- εἰ meaning whether: ἐρωτῶντες εἰ λῃσταί εἰσιν "asking whether they are pirates" (§2671). GMT lists the same introductions, εἰ, rarely ἆρα, the interrogatives, and most relatives (GMT §665).
- A question repeated back by the person answering, in the indefinite form: A. ἀλλὰ τίς γὰρ εἷ; B. ὅστις; πολῑ́της χρηστός (§2670). Gildersleeve calls these echo-questions and reads the second person subjunctive in them as virtually indirect (Gildersleeve §381).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Decide first whether the sentence is a question at all, then whether it is direct or indirect (§2636). Smyth's summary states the general case: a simple question results from making any form of statement interrogative (§2157). When punctuation is the only evidence, the mark of a direct question is (;), like our semicolon, while the point above the line is the Greek colon (Goodell §22).
2. If it is a word-question, find the interrogative and see what it belongs to. It often depends on a participle and not on the main verb of the sentence: τί οὖν ποιήσαντος κατεχειροτονήσατε τοῦ Εὐάνδρου; "for what act then did you condemn Evander?" (§2643). It may stand between the article and its noun, condensing two questions into one: ἐγὼ οὖν τὸν ἐκ ποίᾱς πόλεως στρατηγὸν προσδοκῶ ταῦτα πρᾱ́ξειν; "am I waiting for a general to do this? from what city?" (§2645). Two interrogatives may stand in the same sentence without a connective: ἀπὸ τούτων τίς τίνος αἴτιός ἐστι γενήσεται φανερόν "from this it will become clear who is chargeable with what" (§2646).
3. Read τί and τίς apart. Gildersleeve distinguishes them in the predicate: τί asks the essence of a thing and τίς its classification, as in τί δ᾽ ἐστὶν ὁ χρόνος καὶ τίς αὐτοῦ ἡ φύσις (Gildersleeve §130).
4. If it is a sentence-question, the particle decides the expected answer, and this is the one thing English word order cannot show you. οὐ, ἆρ᾽ οὐ, οὐκοῦν expect yes; μή, ἆρα μή, μῶν expect no (§2651). Read the compounds too: μῶν is μὴ οὖν, μῶν οὐ expects the answer yes, and οὐκοῦν οὐ expects the answer no (§2651 a, §2651 b). Goodell states the same set, that οὐ in a question is like English not, implying that the answer yes is expected, and so also οὐκοῦν but less strongly (Goodell §668).
5. Do not read a question with ἆρα or ἦ as loaded. Both particles denote interest on the part of the questioner, often really? or surely?, but imply nothing as to the answer (§2650, §2650 a). Goodell agrees: ἆρα and ἦ mark a sentence as interrogative, implying nothing as to the answer expected, and ἆρα before μή or οὐ merely makes the interrogative force more distinct (Goodell §668). Smyth puts the last point as greater distinctness given to the question (§2651 e).
6. If the question has μή, ask whether apprehension or mere effect is meant. οὐ questions ask concerning facts, while μή questions imply uncertainty or even apprehension, but sometimes are asked merely for effect (§2651 e). Goodell reads the same μή as an expression of desire on the part of the speaker to negative the statement, a desire that may be merely assumed, or may be nothing more than surprise that the statement should be true (Goodell §488).
7. Watch for μή with the subjunctive that is not quite a question. Gildersleeve collects the hesitating half-questions with μή or μὴ οὐ and the present subjunctive, frequent in Plato and rare elsewhere in Attic, whose effect is that of a doubtful affirmation or negation, as μὴ ἀγροικότερον ᾖ τὸ ἀληθὲς εἰπεῖν (Gildersleeve §385). Smyth treats the same passages under μή or μὴ οὐ with the subjunctive in half-questions (§2651 d).
8. If the mood is subjunctive, ask whose deliberation it is. The deliberative subjunctive, present or aorist, is used in questions when the speaker asks what he is to do or say, negative μή, and is usually in the first person (§1805, §1805 a). The rare second person is used in repeating a question (§1805 b), and the third person generally represents the first, commonly with τὶς (§1805 c). GMT calls these questions of appeal, where the speaker asks himself or another what he is to do, and notes that in Attic they are often introduced by βούλει or βούλεσθε, sometimes in poetry by θέλεις or θέλετε (GMT §287). Smyth reads βούλει σοι εἴπω; as a fusion of two distinct questions, βούλει "do you wish?" and εἴπω "shall I say?" (§1806), and GMT reaches the same result historically, calling it a parataxis not yet developed into a leading and a dependent clause (GMT §288).
9. Do not stop at the subjunctive when looking for deliberation. It may be replaced by a periphrasis with δεῖ or χρή and the infinitive, or by the verbal adjective in -τέον ἐστί: ἡμεῖς δὲ προσμένωμεν; ἢ τί χρὴ ποιεῖν; "and shall we wait? or what must we do?" (§1807). Deliberation in the past is expressed by ἔδει, χρῆν (ἐχρῆν), ἔμελλον with the infinitive, and by the verbal adjective in -τέον with ἦν (§1808), and Smyth states that in direct questions the optative is not used to denote what was to be done (§2639). Gildersleeve puts the whole class under one head: the subjunctive question expects an imperative answer (Gildersleeve §379). Goodell classifies it as dubitative rather than deliberative and adds that such questions are often exclamatory, calling for no answer (Goodell §471).
10. Separate τί πάθω from the deliberative subjunctive proper. The subjunctive is used in τί πάθω; "what will become of me; what am I to do?", and Smyth says that the subjunctive here is not deliberative, but refers to a future event (§1811). Monro reads the Homeric instance under one heading with the rest, since in interrogative sentences the subjunctive generally expresses necessity, submission to some command or power, and glosses τί πάθω; as "what am I to suffer?" (Monro §277). Goodell keeps it with the class too, translating "What shall I experience?" and calling it frequent, in many shades of meaning (Goodell §471).
11. If a future indicative stands in a question about what to do, do not correct it to a subjunctive. The deliberative future may occur in connection with the deliberative subjunctive in the same sentence (§1916 a), and Gildersleeve prints Euripides' εἴπωμεν ἢ σιγῶμεν; ἢ τί δράσομεν; for exactly that (Gildersleeve §268).
12. A future with οὐ in a question is usually a command. The future with οὐ interrogative is used in questions in an imperative sense to express urgency, warning, or irony: οὐ φυλάξεσθε; "will you not be on your guard?" (§1918). Gildersleeve gives the same rule and Demosthenes' string of them, οὐκ ἀποκτενεῖτε; οὐκ ἐπὶ τὴν οἰκίαν βαδιεῖσθε; οὐχὶ συλλήψεσθε; (Gildersleeve §271). The aorist behaves the same way after τί οὖν οὐ and τί οὐ, expressing surprise that something has not been done, the question being equivalent to a command or proposal (§1936).
13. Ask whether the question wants an answer at all. Rhetorical questions are asked for effect and not for information, since the speaker knows the answer in advance and either does not wait for, or himself gives, the answer, and they are often introduced by μή (§2640). Smyth records their distribution, very rare in Lysias, somewhat frequent in Plato, common in Isaeus, highly developed in Demosthenes, and adds that the rhetorical question is much more favoured in Greek than in English (§2640 a). Read a negatived interrogative as an assertion: τίς and ποῖος as adjective pronouns, and πῶς and the rest, when followed by οὐ, have the force of an affirmative assertion, ποίους λόγους οὐκ ἀνηλώσαμεν; "what arguments did we not expend?" (§2649).
14. If the mood is the optative, expect a potential, not a wish. The moods used in direct questions are the same as those used in statements (§2662), and the optative there is potential, τίς φράσειεν ἄν; "who can tell?" (§2662 c). Gildersleeve argues the rule from the answer: no direct question can be put in the pure optative of wish, since no question expects a wish for an answer, so whenever the optative is found in a direct question it is a potential optative (Gildersleeve §390), and the optative with ἄν in the question expects the optative with ἄν in the answer, so that the speaker virtually answers himself (Gildersleeve §445).
15. For an alternative question, find both members before translating either. πότερον was originally the neuter of πότερος, which of the two, placed in front of a double question and later made a part of the first question (§2660). It may stand alone when the second member of the question is implicit in another sentence (§2659), and an alternative question may follow upon a simple direct or indirect question (§2658). Goodell gives the plain rule, that πότερον (πότερα) . . . ἤ introduce the members of an alternative question, and warns that English whether is no longer used that way in a direct question (Goodell §668).
16. If the passage is Homer, expect other particles. ἢ (ἠὲ) . . . ἦ (ἦε), or ἦ alone, occurs in Homer, who does not use πότερον: ἤ ῥά τι ἴδμεν ἐνὶ φρεσίν, ἦε καὶ οὐκί; "do we know aught in heart, or do we not?" (§2661). Monro states the rule of writing that goes with it, that when a question is asked in a disjunctive form the accent of the particle ἠέ, ἤ is thrown back, so that it is written ἦε or ἦ (Monro §340). Smyth and Monro disagree on how settled that is. Smyth notes that all the ancient grammarians attest the accentuation of these particles as given above, and that modern editors often adopt other accents (§2661 a); Monro says the rule rests upon the unanimous testimony of the ancient grammarians and is now generally adopted, and holds that most editors recognize an interrogative use of the form ἦε, but erroneously, since the questions in which it is found are all disjunctive (Monro §340).
17. For an indirect question, read the leading verb first and then the interrogative. Indirect questions are introduced by interrogative pronouns, adjectives, and adverbs, indefinite relative pronouns and adverbs, or by certain interrogative particles (§2663). The interrogatives of the direct question may be retained, but it is more common to use the indefinite relatives, which in interrogative sentences are employed only in indirect questions (§2664). Smyth classifies the whole class among substantive clauses: dependent questions are subordinate clauses asking a question, both parts of the sentence together forming a statement (§2575).
18. Do not require the two forms to be consistent inside one sentence. An indirect interrogative is often used in the same sentence after a direct interrogative, and the direct form precedes less often (§2666). Two interrogatives may occur in the same sentence without a connective (§2667).
19. If the pronoun is a simple relative, ask whether this is a question at all. After verbs of saying, knowing, seeing, making known, perceiving, but not after verbs of asking, the simple relatives are found where the indefinite relatives or the interrogatives might stand, and where ὅς is so used it has the force of οἷος (§2668). Smyth records the dispute rather than settling it: that the simple relatives are never thus used after verbs of asking indicates that such clauses are not true indirect questions, as they are in Latin, but some scholars allow an indirect question after all these words except ὅς, and others admit no such limitation (§2668 a). The context must sometimes determine whether a sentence is an indirect question or a relative clause (§2668 d). Goodell instead puts the construction under relative clauses, adding that by omission of the antecedent ὅς and the other simple relatives become sometimes in effect indirect interrogatives (Goodell §621).
20. Now set the mood from the tense of the leading verb. After primary tenses the mood and tense of the direct question are retained, and after secondary tenses they may be retained or the optative may be used instead, the latter being more common; but a past indicative with ἄν always remains unchanged (§2677). GMT states the same rule for indirect quotations and indirect questions together, and adds that indirect questions after primary tenses retain an indicative of the direct question in Greek, where the subjunctive is used in Latin (GMT §669). Goodell adds the two negative halves of the rule: no indirect question has the subjunctive unless the direct form has it, and the optative is never required, even after a past tense (Goodell §661).
21. If the mood is the subjunctive after a verb of asking or deliberating, put the direct question back in the first person. In indirect questions, after a primary tense, an interrogative subjunctive retains its mood and tense; after a secondary tense, it may be either changed to the same tense of the optative or retained in the subjunctive (GMT §677). Smyth gives the Attic example with εἰ, ἐπανερομένου Κτησιφῶντος εἰ καλέσῃ Δημοσθένην "when Ctesiphon was asking if he was to call Demosthenes", the direct question being καλέσω; (§2671), and GMT notes that εἰ can introduce the subjunctive here as well as the indicative or optative (GMT §680).
22. Expect the moods to be mixed in one sentence, and do not emend. After a secondary tense the mood of a direct question may be retained, usually for vividness, in the same sentence with the mood of an indirect question (§2678). Sometimes the indicative may ask for a statement of fact, the optative request an opinion of the person questioned, but in some cases there is no apparent reason apart from desire for variety (§2678 a). GMT reports the same freedom for indirect discourse at large, the indicative being merely a more vivid form of expression than the optative, with no difference in meaning, and both moods occurring in the same sentence (GMT §670).
23. Take care with the aorist. In indirect questions the aorist indicative was generally retained (GMT §670), and Smyth notes that an aorist optative might represent the aorist indicative, but that tense is usually retained to avoid confusion (§2677 b).
24. Read the negative last. The negative of the direct form is usually preserved in indirect questions (§2676), and indirect single questions introduced by interrogative pronouns, adjectives, and adverbs usually have οὐ (§2676 a). Indirect questions introduced by εἰ have οὐ or μή (§2676 c), and as the second member of an alternative question introduced by εἰ, or not is either ἢ οὐ or ἢ μή (§2676 e). μή appears after verbs of seeing, considering and the like when there is an idea of purpose or desire to prevent something (§2676 b).
25. Do not turn every μή clause into an indirect question. μή is sometimes translated by whether after verbs of fear and caution, but such dependent clauses with μή are not indirect questions, and after ὁρῶ, ἐννοοῦμαι, ἐνθῡμοῦμαι, σκοπῶ it is properly a conjunction and not the interrogative particle (§2674). Smyth's proof is worth keeping: that μή does not properly mean whether not is clear from the fact that, in these clauses, it is not used of something that is hoped for (§2674). GMT keeps the same class of verbs under verbs of fearing rather than under questions (GMT §369).
26. Do not turn every ἐᾱ́ν clause into one either. ἐᾱ́ν rarely, if ever, means whether, even after verbs of examining and considering, though in form such conditional sentences often approach closely to indirect questions (§2672). GMT states that ἐάν or ἤν never means whether, and wherever this introduces a subjunctive the expression is conditional (GMT §665, GMT §680). Goodell allows the doubtful case, saying that after expressions of doubt or inquiry εἰ with the indicative, rarely ἐᾱ́ν with the subjunctive, becomes an indirect question (Goodell §655). Smyth reports the historical claim without adopting it, that some scholars maintain that in Greek if was at an early period confused with whether in such sentences (§2672 a).

## 4. Translation guidance

- A pronoun-question is answered by supplying the missing word, so translate the interrogative and leave the rest of the clause alone: πόσον . . . ἄπεστιν ἐνθένδε τὸ στράτευμα; "how far distant from here is the army?" (§2642).
- A yes and no question wants a tag in English, and the particle tells you which tag: οὐχ οὕτως ἔλεγες; is "did you not say so?" and μή τι νεώτερον ἀγγέλλεις; is "no bad news, I hope?" (§2651 a, §2651 b). Goodell renders a μή question the same way, "You don't suppose he was anxious about death, do you?" (Goodell §488).
- ἆρα and ἦ often come out as really or surely (§2650 a), and ἦ γάρ used alone in dialogue is "is it not so?" (§2865).
- ἄλλο τι ἤ; is "is it anything else than?" and ἄλλο τι; is "is it not?" (§2652).
- εἶτα and ἔπειτα, more emphatic κᾆτα and κᾱ̓́πειτα, introduce questions expressing surprise, indignation, irony, and often mark a contrast between what a person has or has not done and what is or was to be expected of him: εἶτα πῶς οὐκ εὐθὺς ἐπήγειράς με; "then why did you not rouse me at once?" (§2653).
- ἀλλά introduces a question opposed to an expressed or implied thought of the speaker, especially an objection (§2654), and δέ sometimes introduces a suppressed thought, as an objection (§2655).
- γάρ in a question often marks surprise or indignation, and may frequently be translated by what, why, then, really, surely, as in ἦ ζῇ γὰρ ᾱ̔νήρ; "is the man really alive?" (§2805 a). The short formulae are worth learning whole, τί γάρ; "what then, how when, how else?" and ἦ γάρ; "is it not so?" (§2805 b).
- The other particles colour the question rather than change its construction. δή with an interrogative adds urgency, τί δή; "why, pray?" (§2843 a). δαί is used in colloquial Attic after interrogative words to express wonder or indignation, τί δαί; πῶς δαί; "what then? how so?" (§2848). δῆτα in questions marks an inference or consequence, πῶς δῆτα; "how in truth?" (§2851). οὖν often follows interrogative pronouns and adverbs in dialogue, τίς οὖν; "who pray?" (§2962). καί before an interrogative expression marks an objection occasioned by surprise or indignation, καὶ πῶς; "pray, how comes it that?" (§2872).
- A deliberative question is English shall I or am I to, not will I: τί δρᾱ́σω; ποῖ φύγω; is "what am I to do? whither shall I fly?" (§1805 a). GMT renders the same construction Εἴπω τοῦτο; "shall I say this?" (GMT §287).
- A deliberative question in the past wants ought: it is expressed by χρῆν (ἐχρῆν) or ἔδει with the infinitive, or by the verbal in -τέον with ἦν (§2639).
- An indirect question keeps English whether where Greek has εἰ, and English what where Greek has ὅ τι: ἠρώτων αὐτοὺς τίνες εἶεν "they asked them who they were", and ἀπορῶν ποῖ τράποιτο ἐπὶ λόφον τινὰ καταφεύγει "being in doubt whither he should turn, he fled to a hill" (§2664).
- εἰ has an affirmative force, whether, or a negative force, whether not, the latter after verbs expressing uncertainty or doubt: εἰ μὲν δὴ δίκαια ποιήσω, οὐκ οἶδα "I don't know whether I shall do what is right" (§2671 a). Goodell translates the same sentence "Whether I shall be doing right I do not know", and says εἰ may sometimes be rendered if, but more often whether (Goodell §655).
- The optative of an indirect question is not a mood English has, so put back the tense of the direct form. ἤρετο εἴ τις ἐμοῦ εἴη σοφώτερος is "he asked whether any one was wiser than I", the direct question being ἐστί; (§2677 b). GMT translates the same example the same way and gives the direct form as ἔστι τις σοφώτερος; (GMT §669).
- An indirect alternative question takes whether or, and the pairs are not interchangeable: εἴτε . . . εἴτε gives equal value to each alternative, while εἰ . . . ἤ indicates that the second alternative is preferable or more probable (§2675 b, §2675 c). πότερον . . . ἤ may denote that the second alternative is more important than the first (§2675 a).
- Answers rarely repeat the verb in English but often do in Greek. A yes and no question may be answered by repeating the verb or another emphatic word with or without one or more confirmative adverbs (§2680 a). Yes may be expressed by ναί, μάλιστα, πάνυ γε, ἀληθῆ λέγεις and the rest, no by οὔ, οὐ δῆτα, οὐδαμῶς, ἥκιστα (§2680 c), and an answer may itself take the form of a question, τί μήν; τί γάρ; πῶς γὰρ οὔ; (§2680 d). Smyth glosses οὔκουν in emphatic negative answers as not then, therefore not, so not, surely not (§2953, §2953 a), and reports that asseverative μέντοι, certainly, surely, of course, in truth, is very common in replies (§2918).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading ἆρα as though it expected an answer. ἦ and ἆρα imply nothing as to the answer expected, neither yes nor no (§2650); the expected answer is marked only when οὐ or μή is present (§2651).
- Reading μή in a question as an ordinary negative. In a μή question the negative belongs to the sentence and marks the expected answer no, whereas οὐ after μή or ἆρα μή belongs to a single word, not to the sentence (§2651 d). The exception is the group οὔ που; οὔ τί που; οὐ δή; οὐ δή που, meaning surely it is not so, where the negative belongs to the sentence (§2651 f).
- Forgetting that the compounds reverse. μῶν οὐ expects the answer yes and οὐκοῦν οὐ expects the answer no (§2651 a, §2651 b).
- Looking for μῶν outside Attic. μῶν is confined to Attic, and since the fact of its composition was lost we find μῶν οὖν and μῶν μή (§2651 c).
- Looking for ἆρα in Homer. Homer uses ἦ, not ἆρα, ἆρα is from ἦ + ἄρα, and ἦ is chiefly poetic (§2650 a). Monro describes the Homeric particle at its own value: ἦ at the beginning of a sentence gives it the character of a strong affirmation, and it is often used interrogatively, especially in questions of surprise, indignation, irony (Monro §338).
- Taking a deliberative subjunctive for a hortatory one, or missing why the negative is μή. μή is used in these questions because the construction is simply the interrogative form of the hortatory subjunctive: φῶμεν "let us say", μὴ φῶμεν; "are we not to say?" (§1809).
- Reading a subjunctive question as a question about the future. The subjunctive question does not refer to a future fact, but to what is, under the present circumstances, advantageous or proper to do or say (§1805).
- Expecting ἄν with the optative in every question. The potential optative without ἄν is rare there, as in τίς λέγοι; "who can tell?" (§2662 c).
- Treating an indirect question as though the optative were compulsory. Even after a secondary tense the direct form may be retained (§2677 a), and Goodell says that the optative is never required, even after a past tense (Goodell §661).
- Assuming a Homeric optative in an indirect question is ordinary indirect discourse. Homer has the optative for the indicative due to indirect discourse only in indirect questions: εἴροντο τίς εἴη καὶ πόθεν ἔλθοι "they asked who he was and whence he had come" (§2677 d). GMT says the same, that in Homer this construction is fully developed in indirect questions while in indirect quotations the change of the indicative to the optative after past tenses had not yet been introduced (GMT §671). Monro adds that the use of the interrogative in dependent questions is rare in Homer at all, and that nearly all the passages of this kind are in the Odyssey and in the 10th and 24th books of the Iliad (Monro §248).
- Reading a Homeric disjunctive clause as two sentences. A disjunctive question after a verb of asking, saying, knowing is generally expressed by the correlatives ἠέ (ἤ) . . . ἦε (ἦ), and in general these dependent clauses are the same in form as the corresponding direct questions (Monro §341).
- Taking the interrogative in a subordinate clause as an error. A subordinate clause introduced by a conjunction or a relative pronoun may suddenly change into a direct pronoun-question, though the construction of the clause remains unaltered: πόθ᾽ ἃ χρὴ πρᾱ́ξετε; ἐπειδὰν τί γένηται; "when will you do what you ought to do? in what event?" (§2644). The elliptical phrases belong here too, ἵνα τί, ὡς τί, ὅτι τί (§2644 a).
- Missing an interrogative used as a predicate. With a substantive and the article or with a demonstrative pronoun an interrogative pronoun may be used as a predicate adjective, and the sentence is then equivalent to an interrogative clause with a dependent relative clause: τίς ὁ πόθος αὐτοὺς ῑ̔́κετο; "what is this longing that has come upon them?" (§2647). τίς, τί and ποῖος referring to something mentioned before may take the article, τὸ τί; τὰ ποῖα; (§2648).
- Overlooking the participle in τί παθών and τί μαθών. These are used with the general sense of wherefore in direct questions, with ὅ τι in indirect, expressing surprise or disapprobation (§2064 a).
- Assuming Greek keeps the negative of a relative clause inside an indirect question. In relative clauses joined by καί and standing in an indirect question, μή must be used when the verb is to be supplied with the second clause, but when the verb is repeated either μή, or οὐ if the antecedent is definite, may be used (§2676 d). A shift from μή to οὐ in sequent alternative indirect questions appears to be due to the desire to attain variety (§2676 f).
