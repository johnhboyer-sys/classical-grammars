# The Article

Status: drafted 2026-09-07 on the pattern of `topics/supplementary-participle.md`
and `topics/middle-voice.md`. Every claim carries a section citation;
`scripts/verify_topic_pages.py` checks them. `topic: article` names the derived
record in `data/smyth/topics.json`, which counts 41 inbound cross-references
and names two hub sections, the substantive-making power of the article
(§1153) and the predicate position of adjectives (§1168).

Goodwin's Moods and Tenses does not treat the article, so this page draws on
Smyth, Gildersleeve, Monro and Goodell only.

## 1. Quick answer

The article ὁ, ἡ, τό was originally a demonstrative pronoun, and by gradual weakening it became the definite article; it also served as a relative pronoun (§1099). In Attic it marks objects as definite and known, whether individuals, which is the particular article, or classes, which is the generic article, and the context must determine which is present (§1118). There is no indefinite article in Greek, and τὶς often stands for English a or an (§1118 a). Gildersleeve states that the article's function, since it was originally a demonstrative and always keeps more or less demonstrative force, is to fix a floating adjective or substantive; the particular use came to require the explicit article, ὁ ἄνθρωπος "the man", while in the generic use, "man", it stayed optional, ἄνθρωπος or ὁ ἄνθρωπος (Gildersleeve §514).

Two further facts matter most for a reader. First, the article makes a substantive out of any word or words it is prefixed to (§1153). Second, a word standing between the article and its noun, or immediately after the article if the noun, with or without the article, precedes, is an attributive, and one standing outside both is a predicate (§1154, §1168).

## 2. Recognition

- Attributive position. A word or group of words standing between the article and its noun, or immediately after the article when the noun precedes, is an attributive: ὁ σοφὸς ἀνήρ, ὁ ἀνὴρ ὁ σοφός, or ἀνὴρ ὁ σοφός (§1154).
- Predicate position. A predicate adjective either precedes or follows the article and its noun: σοφὸς ὁ ἀνήρ or ὁ ἀνὴρ σοφός means the man is wise, and the position is often emphatic (§1168).
- Goodell states the same pair as a reading rule, that most attributive words take the place just after the article, as Οἱ ἀγαθοὶ δημιουργοί, while an adjective standing outside both article and noun is a predicate whether a verb is written or not, as Ἀγαθοὶ οἱ δημιουργοί (Goodell §552).
- The demonstratives are the standing exception. οὗτος, ὅδε and ἐκεῖνος, and αὐτός meaning self, agree with a noun that usually takes the article, and stand in the predicate position: οὗτος ὁ ἀνήρ or ὁ ἀνὴρ οὗτος, never with the article before the pronoun (§1176). Goodell adds that when the article is dropped the noun or the pronoun is a predicate instead, though proper names may omit it, Ὅδε (ὁ) Σωκράτης "this Socrates", and if another adjectival expression is used with the pronoun, the pronoun may stand in the attributive position, τὰς ἐπιστολιμαίους ταύτᾱς δυνάμεις "these forces on paper" (Goodell §553).
- An article with no noun after it is doing the noun's work (§1153).

## 3. Where Greek has an article and English has none

- For a possessive. The article often takes the place of an unemphatic possessive pronoun when there is no doubt as to the possessor: Κῦρος καταπηδήσᾱς ἀπὸ τοῦ ἅρματος τὸν θώρᾱκα ἐνέδῡ "Cyrus leaped down from his chariot and put on his breastplate" (§1121). Gildersleeve records the same use as free in prose and especially common with family relations, parts of the body and personal belongings: τὴν κλεῖν συνετρίβην καὶ τὴν κεφαλὴν κατεάγην "my collar-bone was crushed and my head broken" (Gildersleeve §534). Goodell states it as a plain difference from English, that the article alone is often used where English requires a possessive pronoun: Διαβάλλει τὸν Κῦρον πρὸς τὸν ἀδελφόν "he brings Cyrus into discredit with his brother" (Goodell §551).
- For a whole class. The generic article denotes an entire class as distinguished from other classes, as ὁ ἄνθρωπος "man" and οἱ γέροντες "the aged", and πονηρὸν ὁ σῡκοφάντης is "the informer is a vile thing" (§1122). In the singular it makes one object the representative of the class and in the plural it denotes all the objects belonging to it (§1123). Gildersleeve calls the principle the selection of a representative or normal individual and gives ὁ συκοφάντης as an example, adding that the English plural will not express the idiom (Gildersleeve §563).
- With an abstract noun. Abstract substantives generally have the article: ἡ ἀρετὴ μᾶλλον ἢ ἡ φυγὴ σῴζει τᾱ̀ς ψῡχᾱ́ς "valour rather than flight saves men's lives" (§1131). Gildersleeve says the influence of the original personification is to be considered here, and that the article with the abstract is almost on a line with the articular names of the gods, ἡ ἀρετή beside ἡ Ἀδράστεια (Gildersleeve §565). Goodell states as a rule for beginners that abstract nouns generally have the article (Goodell §551).
- With a proper name. Names of persons and places are individual and therefore omit the article unless previously mentioned or specially marked as well known, so that Θουκῡδίδης Ἀθηναῖος has none and ὁ Σόλων has one (§1136). Goodell notes that in practice names of persons often take the article, and prints Διαβάλλει τὸν Κῦρον beside a bare accusative from the same page of Xenophon (Goodell §551).
- With numerals, where English has none. The article may be used with cardinal numerals when the numeral states a definite part of a whole, when the numeral is approximate, as ἔμειναν ἡμέρᾱς ἀμφὶ τᾱ̀ς τριᾱ́κοντα "they remained about thirty days", and when the number is used abstractly (§1125).
- Where English repeats the noun. Instead of repeating a noun with the article it may suffice to repeat the article: ὁ βίος ὁ τῶν ἰδιωτευόντων ἢ ὁ τῶν τυραννευόντων "the life of persons in a private station or that of princes" (§1145).

## 4. Where English has an article and Greek has none

- There is no indefinite article at all, and τὶς supplies it when needed (§1118 a).
- The article is omitted in many adverbial designations of time, mostly with prepositions: περὶ μέσᾱς νύκτας "about midnight" (§1127). ἡμέρᾱς "by day" and νυκτός "by night" omit it too, without a preposition (§1127).
- Phrases containing a preposition very often omit it: ἐν ἀρχῇ τοῦ λόγου "in the beginning of the speech", ἔξω βελῶν "out of reach of the missiles" (§1128). Gildersleeve records the same survival, that prepositional phrases and other formulae may dispense with the article as in the earlier language (Gildersleeve §568).
- Words denoting persons used of a class may omit it: πάντων μέτρον ἄνθρωπός ἐστιν "man is the measure of all things" (§1129).
- The names of the virtues, vices, arts, sciences and occupations often omit it: τί σωφροσύνη, τί μανίᾱ; "what is temperance, what is madness?" (§1132). Gildersleeve gives abstracts without the article from Homer to Demosthenes and states that the differences grammarians have set up here are often imaginary: βία γὰρ Ἔρωτος οὐχ ἅπτεται "violence gets no hold of Love" (Gildersleeve §566).
- A substantive followed by an attributive genitive and forming with it a compound idea usually omits it: τελευτὴ τοῦ βίου "the end of his life" (§1146).
- Two coordinated concrete words forming a copulative expression may omit it, as English says man and wife, horse and rider (§1148).
- A predicate noun has no article, and this distinguishes it from the subject: καλεῖται ἡ ἀκρόπολις ἔτι ὑπ᾽ Ἀθηναίων πόλις "the acropolis is still called city by the Athenians" (§1150). Goodell states the same test, that predicate nouns rarely have the article and that the subject and a predicate noun are often thus distinguished (Goodell §551). But the rule has a real exception: even in the predicate the article is used with a noun referring to a definite object that is well known, previously mentioned, or identical with the subject, and so with ὁ αὐτός and τοὐναντίον (§1152).

## 5. Attributive and predicate position

Three orders count as attributive, and they are not interchangeable.

- The article and the attributive before the noun, ὁ σοφὸς ἀνήρ, which is the common order and puts the emphasis on the attributive (§1157).
- The article and the attributive after the noun with its own article, ὁ ἀνὴρ ὁ σοφός, which puts the emphasis on the noun as something definite or previously mentioned and adds the attributive by way of explanation (§1158).
- The noun without an article before it, ἀνὴρ ὁ σοφός, used when the noun would have no article if the attributive were dropped (§1159).

The genitive of a substantive limiting another substantive may take any one of four positions, τὸ τοῦ πατρὸς βιβλίον and τὸ βιβλίον τὸ τοῦ πατρός inside the article, τοῦ πατρὸς τὸ βιβλίον to emphasize the genitive or when a genitive has just preceded, and τὸ βιβλίον τοῦ πατρός outside it (§1161).

Which position a word takes is fixed for whole classes of words, and meaning depends on it:

- αὐτός. Attributive ὁ αὐτὸς ἀνήρ is "the same man"; predicate αὐτὸς ὁ ἀνήρ or ὁ ἀνὴρ αὐτός is "the man himself" (§1173).
- πᾶς. In the attributive position it denotes the whole regarded as the sum of its parts, οἱ πάντες πολῖται "the whole body of citizens" and ἡ πᾶσα Σικελίᾱ "the whole of Sicily"; in the predicate and usual position it means all, πάντες οἱ πολῖται "all the citizens" individually (§1174).
- ὅλος. Attributive τὸ ὅλον στράτευμα is "the whole army"; predicate ὅλον τὸ στράτευμα is "the army as a whole" (§1175).
- Adjectives of place. In the predicate position ἄκρος means the top of, μέσος the middle of, ἔσχατος the end of: περὶ ἄκραις ταῖς χερσὶ χειρῖδες "gloves on the fingers", διὰ μέσου τοῦ παραδείσου ῥεῖ "flows through the middle of the park" (§1172).
- μόνος. Attributive ὁ μόνος παῖς is "the only son"; predicate μόνος ὁ παῖς παίζει is "the boy plays alone" (§1173).
- The possessive pronouns, and the possessive genitives of the reflexive and demonstrative pronouns, take the attributive position (§1163). So τὸ ἐμὸν βιβλίον "my book" (§1182), and τὸ ἐμαυτοῦ βιβλίον "my own book" beside τὸ τούτου βιβλίον "his book" (§1184).
- The genitive of the personal pronouns takes the predicate position: τὸ βιβλίον μου (§1185).
- ἄμφω, ἀμφότερος, ἑκάτερος and ἕκαστος have the predicate position, though with ἕκαστος the article is often omitted, as in καθ᾽ ἑκάστην ἡμέρᾱν "every day" (§1179).

Goodell gives the same two lists and adds that an adjective in the predicate position with a noun in an oblique case often needs care in English: Κῦρος ψῑλὴν ἔχων τὴν κεφαλὴν εἰς τὴν μάχην καθῑ́στατο is "Cyrus went into the battle with his head bare" (Goodell §552). Smyth gives that phrase under the same heading, ψῑλὴν ἔχων τὴν κεφαλήν (§1168), and adds that a predicate adjective may stand for a whole clause of a complex sentence in English (§1169).

## 6. The article makes a noun out of anything

The article has the power to make substantival any word or words to which it is prefixed (§1153). Smyth's list, with his examples:

- Adjectives: ὁ σοφός "the wise man", τὸ δίκαιον "justice" (§1153).
- Participles: ὁ βουλόμενος "whoever wills, the first that offers" (§1153). Such participial nouns take the distinctions of tense, and Thucydides often makes a noun of the neuter participle to form abstract expressions (§1153).
- Preposition and case: οἱ ἐπὶ τῶν πρᾱγμάτων "those in power", οἱ ἐν τῇ ἡλικίᾳ "those in the prime of life" (§1153).
- The genitive alone, forming a noun-phrase: τὰ τῶν στρατιωτῶν "the condition of the soldiers" (§1153).
- Adverbs: οἱ τότε "the men of that time", οἱ πάλαι "the ancients" (§1153).
- Infinitives, which have their own page: καλοῦσί γε ἀκολασίᾱν τὸ ὑπὸ τῶν ἡδονῶν ἄρχεσθαι "they call intemperance being ruled by one's pleasures" (§1153).
- Any single word or clause: τὸ ῡ̔μεῖς ὅταν λέγω, τὴν πόλιν λέγω "when I say You, I mean the State" (§1153).

Gildersleeve and Goodell give the same account. Adjectives and participles used as substantives regularly take the article in prose (Gildersleeve §573), and the article makes substantives of adverbs, prepositional phrases and other expressions (Gildersleeve §575), including any word or phrase whatever (Gildersleeve §579). Goodell adds the two uses a reader meets most: an adverbial expression is made adjectival by standing in the attributive position, as Οἱ πάλαι Ἀθηναῖοι "the Athenians of old", and any quoted word or clause is made a noun by prefixing τό, as Τὸ μηδὲν ἄγᾱν (Goodell §555).

## 7. Proper names, gods and places

- Persons. Names omit the article unless previously mentioned or marked as well known (§1136). When the name is defined by an appositive, the official designation merely states the parentage, Περδίκκᾱς Ἀλεξάνδρου, while the popular designation with the article distinguishes one bearer of the name from others (§1142).
- Deities. Names of gods omit the article except when emphatic, as νὴ τὸν Δία, or when definite cults are meant (§1137). With an epithet the article stands with the name and with the epithet, or with neither: τῷ Διὶ τῷ Ὀλυμπίῳ against Διὶ ἐλευθερίῳ (§1142). Gildersleeve treats oaths only, and there he differs from Smyth: in oaths the article is required except with the name of Zeus, where familiarity has brought about the fading of the oath and the article is usually omitted, νὴ Δία beside the comparatively rare νὴ τὸν Δία (Gildersleeve §542).
- Nations. Names of nations may omit the article, but οἱ Ἕλληνες is usual when opposed to οἱ βάρβαροι, and the name without the article denotes the entire people (§1138).
- Countries and places. Continents take the article, ἡ Εὐρώπη and ἡ Ἀσίᾱ, and so do names originally adjectives, as ἡ Ἀττική, while other names of countries omit it, as Λιβύη; names of cities usually omit it (§1139). Geographical names are usually treated as attributives, as ὁ Εὐφρᾱ́της ποταμός "the river Euphrates" (§1142). Goodell states as a rule for beginners that names of countries, mostly adjectives by origin, commonly have the article, as ἡ Ἑλλάς, and that names of peoples often omit it (Goodell §551).
- Appellatives treated like proper names. βασιλεύς is the king of Persia without the article, while ὁ βασιλεύς is anaphoric or points at a definite person; names of relationship behave the same way, πατήρ, ἀνήρ, γυνή (§1140). Words forming a class by themselves go with them: ἥλιος, θάνατος, and θάλαττα of the sea as opposed to the mainland against ἡ θάλαττα of a definite sea (§1141).

## 8. Where it came from

- The starting point. ὁ, ἡ, τό was originally a demonstrative pronoun and supplied the place of the personal pronoun of the third person; ὁ as a demonstrative is still retained in part in Attic prose, while the beginnings of its use as the article are seen even in Homer (§1099).
- In Homer. ὁ, ἡ, τό is usually a demonstrative pronoun used substantively or adjectively, and serves as the personal pronoun of the third person: ἀλλὰ τὸ θαυμάζω "but I marvel at this", τὴν δ᾽ ἐγὼ οὐ λῡ́σω "but her I will not release" (§1100). It often approaches its later use as the definite article or is actually so used (§1102).
- What survives in Attic. The demonstrative force survives chiefly in connection with particles (§1106), and above all before μέν and δέ in contrasted expressions: οἱ μὲν ἐπορεύοντο, οἱ δ᾽ εἵποντο "the one party proceeded, the other followed" (§1107). Goodell lists the same survivals, ὁ μέν and ὁ δέ in all the cases, ὁ δέ beginning a sentence when the subject changes, and πρὸ τοῦ meaning before this (Goodell §549).
- Poetry keeps the older habit. The Epic use is adopted in general by the lyric poets and in the lyric parts of tragedy, and even in tragic dialogue the article is less common than in prose (§1104). Goodell states that poets omit the article freely where prose requires it, use it more freely as a demonstrative, and sometimes, as in Homer, as a relative pronoun (Goodell §550).
- ὁ as a relative. The demonstrative is used as a relative pronoun in Homer only when the antecedent is definite, and the tragic poets use only the forms in τ- (§1105).

The grammars do not agree about how far the Homeric use is really the article. Smyth writes that the transition from the demonstrative to the article is so gradual that it is often impossible to distinguish between the two, and that Homer ordinarily does not use the article where Attic prose requires it (§1104). Gildersleeve agrees that no sharp line of demarcation can be drawn, since the Homeric demonstrative use passes over into the articular use and the Attic articular use approaches the Homeric demonstrative, so that to an Attic ear the Homeric ὁ γέρων could hardly have been distinguishable from the everyday ὁ γέρων of prose (Gildersleeve §514). Monro puts the opposite emphasis: the difference is often greater in reality than it appears at first sight, and in Homer the article indicates not that a person or thing is known or definite but that it is presented in an antithesis or contrast, so that αὐτὰρ ὁ constantly means but he or but the other and τὰ κακά is said because in that context evils are opposed to good, while in Attic τὰ κακά implies that evils form a class distinguished from all other things (Monro §264). Smyth reaches Monro's conclusion in one line of his own, that in Homer ὁ contrasts two objects or indicates a change of person while Attic ὁ defines (§1103).

## 9. Traps

- Taking the article for a demonstrative in Attic. Its demonstrative force survives chiefly with particles (§1106), and especially in ὁ μέν and ὁ δέ (§1107).
- Reading a predicate adjective as attributive and misreading the sentence. σοφὸς ὁ ἀνήρ means the man is wise, not the wise man (§1168).
- Reading ὁ αὐτὸς ἀνήρ as the man himself. Attributive αὐτός is same and predicate αὐτός is self (§1173).
- Reading πᾶς the same way in both positions. οἱ πάντες πολῖται is the whole body of citizens and πάντες οἱ πολῖται is all the citizens individually (§1174).
- Taking ἄκρος, μέσος and ἔσχατος as ordinary adjectives when they stand outside the article. In the predicate position they mean the top of, the middle of and the end of (§1172).
- Looking for a possessive pronoun that Greek omits. The article often stands for it when there is no doubt as to the possessor (§1121), and Goodell makes the same point for beginners (Goodell §551).
- Translating a bare abstract as indefinite. The names of the virtues, arts and sciences often omit the article without a difference in meaning (§1132), and the generic article is frequently omitted, especially with abstracts (§1126).
- Deciding subject and predicate by word order. A predicate noun has no article and this distinguishes it from the subject (§1150), except where the predicate noun refers to a definite object already known (§1152).
- Assuming a single article before two nouns is careless. A single article with the first of two or more nouns connected by and produces the effect of a single notion, οἱ στρατηγοὶ καὶ λοχᾱγοί "the commanding officers" (§1143), while a repeated article lays stress on each word, οἱ στρατηγοὶ καὶ οἱ λοχᾱγοί (§1144).
- Expecting a demonstrative always to carry the article. οὗτος, ὅδε and ἐκεῖνος omit it regularly when the noun is in the predicate, usually with proper names and with definite numbers, and often when the demonstrative follows its noun (§1178).
- Reading οἱ πολλοί and οἱ ὀλίγοι as plain numbers. οἱ πολλοί is the multitude and οἱ ὀλίγοι the oligarchs as opposed to them (§1189).
- Reading ὁ ἄλλος as another. In the singular it usually means the rest, as ἡ ἄλλη Ἑλλάς, and in the plural the others, as οἱ ἄλλοι Ἕλληνες (§1188).
- Importing the Attic definite article into Homer. Monro states that we naturally do so whenever the context does not make it impossible, and that in Homer the article marks contrast rather than definition (Monro §264).
