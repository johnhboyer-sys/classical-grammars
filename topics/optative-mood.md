# The Optative

## 1. Quick answer

Three constructions share the same endings, and a short test tells them apart. The optative without ἄν in an independent sentence expresses a wish referring to the future, negative μή, and this use gives the mood its name: ὦ παῖ, γένοιο πατρὸς εὐτυχέστερος, ah, boy, mayest thou prove more fortunate than thy sire (§1814). The optative with ἄν states a future possibility, propriety or likelihood as an opinion of the speaker, and may be translated by may, might, can, must, would or should: ἅπαντες ἂν ὁμολογήσειαν, all would agree (§1824). The optative without ἄν in a subordinate clause after a secondary tense is the oblique optative, which represents an indicative or a subjunctive of the direct form and has no meaning of its own: ἔγνωσαν ὅτι κενὸς ὁ φόβος εἴη, they recognized that their fear was groundless (§2615). Goodwin's name for the second is the potential optative, which he traces to Homer, where the optative and the past tenses of the indicative with ἄν or κέ express the action of the verb as dependent on circumstances or conditions (GMT §232). Gildersleeve calls the mood as a whole the ideal mood of the Greek language, the mood of the fancy, and states that the intellectual use, the use in qualified assertions, is confined to the optative with ἄν except in poetry, that the prose examples without ἄν are comparatively few and are either doubtful or occur in fixed formulae, and that its negative is οὐ (Gildersleeve §389).

## 2. Recognition

- An optative standing alone with no ἄν and a μή for its negative, which is a wish. Gildersleeve's pair is διαρραγείης, may you burst in two, and μὴ παύσαιο μηδέποτ᾽ ἐσθίων, may you never stop eating (Gildersleeve §388). Goodell's are Εὖ ξυνεῖεν εἰσαεὶ θεοί and ὄλοιο, may you die (Goodell §476).
- εἴθε or εἰ γάρ in front of it, which marks a wish and nothing else. The optative of wish is often introduced by εἰ γάρ, εἴθε, in Homer αἰ γάρ, αἴθε, or by εἰ and ὡς, both poetical: εἰ γὰρ γένοιτο, would that it might happen, and ὡς ὄλοιτο, may he perish (§1815). Goodell prints the same two particles with Εἴθε μήποτε γνοίης ὃς εἶ (Goodell §477).
- An execration or a protestation, which is a wish under another name: ἐξολοίμην, may I perish (§1814).
- ἄν somewhere in the clause, which makes it potential: γνοίης δ᾽ ἂν ὅτι τοῦθ᾽ οὕτως ἔχει, you may see that this is so, and οὐκ ἂν λάβοις, thou canst not take (§1824). Goodwin's pair is ἔλθοι ἄν, he might, could or would go, beside ἦλθεν ἄν, he might have gone (GMT §232).
- A second person with ἄν where a command was expected, which is the courteous formula: λέγοις ἂν τὴν δέησιν, tell me your request, and προάγοις ἄν, move on (§1830). Goodwin reads the same construction as a mild command or exhortation and gives Σὺ μὲν κομίζοις ἂν σεαυτὸν ᾗ θέλεις (GMT §237). Gildersleeve reads ἄν with the optative there as suggesting a command, and prints προάγοις ἄν, lead on, pray (Gildersleeve §443).
- πῶς ἄν or τίς ἄν with the optative, which is a wish in the form of a question: πῶς ἂν ὀλοίμᾱν, oh, would that I might die (§1832). Gildersleeve gives the same construction and compares it with Latin utinam (Gildersleeve §446).
- βουλοίμην ἄν, which is a softened wish and not a statement about the speaker's will: βουλοίμην ἂν τοῦτο οὕτω γενέσθαι, I could wish that this might be the result (§1827). Gildersleeve explains why it is so common: the optative is more or less passionate and little used in the conventional language of the orators, and βουλοίμην ἄν with the infinitive is a favorite substitute (Gildersleeve §398).
- An optative in a clause dependent on a past verb of saying or thinking, with no ἄν, which is oblique: ἔλεξαν ὅτι πέμψειε σφᾶς ὁ Ἰνδῶν βασιλεύς, they said that the king of the Indians had sent them (§2615). Goodwin's paradigm of the same is Ἔλεξεν ὅτι γράφοι and Ἠρώτησεν αὐτὸν τί ποιοίη (GMT §669).
- A future optative anywhere, which is decisive. The future optative occurs only in indirect discourse after verbs of saying and thinking, in object clauses after ὅπως, and in other indirect expressions of thought (§1862). Goodell states that the future optative is not used in simple sentences (Goodell §483), and Goodwin that the future optative was not used in wishes (GMT §723).

## 3. The three uses, and how to tell them apart

1. Look for ἄν first. In Attic the potential optative regularly takes ἄν (§1824), and Gildersleeve makes the rule exclusive for prose: the potential use is confined to the optative with ἄν except in poetry, and the prose examples without it are either doubtful or occur in fixed formulae (Gildersleeve §389). So an optative with ἄν in Attic prose is potential, and one without it is a wish, an oblique, or the imperative optative of step 14, which expresses a command or exhortation with a force nearly akin to the imperative (§1820).
2. If there is no ἄν, look at the clause. A wish or a potential stands in an independent sentence (§1814, §1824); the oblique optative stands in a subordinate clause. Smyth's list of where the optative without ἄν stands in subordination is: purpose clauses and clauses of fearing after a secondary tense, the apodosis of conditional sentences, relative sentences, and as the representative of the indicative or subjunctive in indirect discourse after secondary tenses (§1823).
3. If it is subordinate, check the tense of the leading verb. The oblique optative needs a secondary tense as its leading verb: after secondary tenses, primary tenses of the indicative and all subjunctives may be changed to the same tense of the optative, but an indicative denoting unreality, with or without ἄν, is retained, and imperfects and pluperfects are generally retained (§2599). Goodwin states the rule as a choice rather than a requirement: after secondary tenses each primary tense of the indicative and each subjunctive of the direct discourse may be either changed to the same tense of the optative or retained in its original mood and tense (GMT §667). Gildersleeve says the same, that the optative is used to represent both the indicative and the subjunctive in indirect discourse after historical tenses (Gildersleeve §399).
4. Try restoring the direct form. The oblique optative can always be restored to a direct form; the wish and the potential cannot. Smyth prints the restoration beside every example: ἔγνωσαν ὅτι κενὸς ὁ φόβος εἴη is the report of ἐστί, and ἔλεξαν ὅτι πέμψειε σφᾶς ὁ Ἰνδῶν βασιλεύς the report of ἔπεμψεν ἡμᾶς (§2615). Goodwin does the same for the whole paradigm: Ἔλεξεν ὅτι γράφοι is he said γράφω, and Ἔλεξεν ὅτι γράψοι is he said γράψω (GMT §669).
5. If the optative is subordinate and still has ἄν, it is a potential that has been carried over unchanged. An indicative with ἄν and an optative with ἄν are retained in indirect discourse (§2615), and Goodwin makes it a rule of the particle: ἄν is never joined with a verb in indirect discourse unless it stood also in the direct form, and it is never omitted if it was used in the direct form, except that ἄν joined to a relative word or particle before a subjunctive is regularly dropped when the subjunctive becomes an optative after a past tense (GMT §667). Smyth records the same exception, that subjunctives with ἄν lose ἄν on passing into the optative (§2619). So an ἄν beside a subordinate optative is evidence of a potential in the direct form, and a missing ἄν is evidence of a subjunctive there.
6. Read the tense according to which use it is. Outside indirect discourse the optative's reference is always to future time and its tenses denote only the stage of the action, present for continuance, aorist for simple occurrence, perfect for completion with permanent result, as πλούσιον δὲ νομίζοιμι τὸν σοφόν, εἰ γὰρ γένοιτο, and τεθναίης (§1861). In general conditions, though, the optative is used of past time (§1861 a). In indirect discourse, when the optative represents the indicative after a past tense of a verb of saying or thinking, each tense does denote time as well as stage relatively to that of the leading verb, and the present optative there represents the imperfect as well as the present indicative (§1862). Goodell states only that the tenses of the optative do not refer to time and differ as do those of the subjunctive (Goodell §483).
7. Check the negative. The optative of wish takes μή (§1814), and Gildersleeve gives the potential optative οὐ (Gildersleeve §389). Monro notices the same fact in Homer and draws a conclusion from it: the fact that οὐ is the negative particle in all those instances shows that the optative is grammatically more akin to a future than to an expression of wish (Monro §300).
8. If the optative is potential, do not go looking for the condition. Usually these optatives are not limited by any definite condition present to the mind, and it is unnecessary to supply any protasis in thought, though in some cases a protasis is dormant in a word of the sentence such as δικαίως or εἰκότως, as in δικαίως ἂν ὑπολαμβάνοιτε, where δικαίως may stand for εἰ δικαίως ὑπολαμβάνοιτε (§1825). Goodwin traces the same series from no condition at all through a condition dormant in an adverb to a condition stated outright, and concludes that there is no necessary or logical bond of union between two forms like εἰ κελεύσειας and ἔλθοι ἄν (GMT §239). Monro says it is erroneous to regard these varieties as complex sentences with the protasis understood, and in this as in some other cases the complex is to be explained from the simple, not the reverse (Monro §300). Goodell describes the same range in terms of what supplies the circumstances, which may be an adverb of time, place or manner, a participle, an infinitive, or a subordinate clause (Goodell §481). `topics/conditional-sentences.md` handles the case where the protasis is actually written.
9. If a question is in the optative, it is not a wish. Gildersleeve's argument is that the mood of the question is the mood of the expected answer, and since no question expects a wish for an answer, an optative in a direct question is a potential optative (Gildersleeve §390). Smyth's potential question is τίς οὐκ ἂν ὁμολογήσειεν; who would not agree? (§1831), and Gildersleeve adds that the optative with ἄν in the question expects the optative with ἄν in the answer, as in τρώγοις ἂν ἐρεβίνθους; would you eat pease? (Gildersleeve §445).
10. If it is a wish, ask whether it refers to the future, not whether it can come true. In independent clauses the use of the optative in standard Greek is confined to the expression of wishes the fulfilment of which is still in suspense (Gildersleeve §388). But an unattainable wish that refers to the future may still be expressed by the optative, and wishes represented as hopeless are expressed in the post-Homeric language by the past tenses of the indicative or by ὤφελον (§1818). Goodwin says why: it is the futurity of the object of a wish, and not its probability or possibility, that requires the optative (GMT §740). Goodwin's rule is that wishes referring to a future object take the optative, and wishes referring to a present or past object which is not or was not attained take the past tenses of the indicative with εἴθε or εἰ γάρ, or ὤφελον with the infinitive (GMT §720, GMT §721, GMT §731). `topics/indicative-mood.md` covers the unattainable wish.
11. Homer is the exception to the last step, and only for the present. An unattainable wish referring to the present may be expressed by the present optative in Homer: εἴθ᾽ ἡβώοιμι, would that I were young again (§1817). Goodwin gives the same construction the same limit, that in Homer a present unattained wish may be expressed by the present optative, like a present unfulfilled condition, and that εἴθε or εἰ γάρ generally introduces it (GMT §739).
12. If there is no ἄν and the sense is still potential, the text may be wrong or the usage may be poetic. The potential optative is occasionally found in Homer and later poetry in an earlier form without the particle, as ῥεῖα θεός γ᾽ ἐθέλων καὶ τηλόθεν ἄνδρα σαώσαι and θᾶσσον ἢ λέγοι τις, quicker than a man could speak, and the construction is suspected in prose; it appears usually in negative sentences or in questions expecting a negative answer, with οὐ (§1821). Goodwin agrees that a few distinctly potential optatives without ἄν or κέ occur in Homer but are exceptions to the general usage even there (GMT §240). Monro finds the same use rare without κεν or ἄν and reads the absence as significant rather than accidental: in such cases the absence of κεν or ἄν marks the negation as sweeping and unconditional (Monro §299). Goodell records it for the poets (Goodell §478, Goodell §482), and Gildersleeve lists what may have removed the particle instead, haplography, confusion with another word, an ἄν to be understood from a neighbouring passage, or euphony (Gildersleeve §450).
13. If the optative seems to be about the past, keep the two uses distinct. Smyth groups them: the present and aorist are rarely used of the past, in Homer of past possibility, as καί νύ κεν ἔνθ᾽ ἀπόλοιτο, and now he might have perished, and in Herodotus of a mild assertion, and both uses are doubtful in Attic prose (§1829). Goodwin says the second is a different construction: the optative that expresses what may hereafter prove to be true or to have been true, as εἴησαν δ᾽ ἂν οὗτοι Κρῆτες, has nothing to do with the Homeric use of the optative with κέ or ἄν in a present or a past sense (GMT §238). Monro agrees with Goodwin and states the difference: the Homeric use is confined to Homer and chiefly found in the Iliad, while in Herodotus the meaning is not would have happened, which did not, but would be found to have happened if we knew more (Monro §300). Read a Herodotean εἴησαν ἄν as an inference about the present state of the evidence, not as an unreal past.
14. If the optative reads like a command, that is a recognised use and not an error. The optative may express a command or exhortation with a force nearly akin to the imperative: Χειρίσοφος ἡγοῖτο, let Chirisophus lead (§1820). Gildersleeve collects the same and notes that it offers a solution for a number of passages where the optative with ἄν might be expected, printing ἔρδοι τις ἣν ἕκαστος εἰδείη τέχνην (Gildersleeve §394). Monro finds it already in Homer as a gentle or deferential imperative conveying advice or suggestion, especially in the second person, as πίθοιό μοι, pray listen to me (Monro §299).
15. If nothing else fits and the speaker is conceding rather than wishing, check Homer's concessive optative. Homer often uses the optative with a concessive or permissive force: ἔπειτα δὲ καί τι πάθοιμι, after that I may suffer come what will (§1819). Monro separates that from the wish and gives it two headings of its own, rhetorical wish implying willingness or indifference to some evil, and concession or acquiescence, as in κτήματα δ᾽ αὐτὸς ἔχοις καὶ δώμασι σοῖσιν ἀνάσσοις (Monro §299).

## 4. Translation guidance

- Wish: English "may" or "would that". γένοιτο τοῦτο is may this happen and μὴ γένοιτο τοῦτο is may this not happen; with the particle, εἴθε γένοιτο τοῦτο is O that this may happen (GMT §721).
- The εἰ γάρ wish takes the same English, and Goodell's two are Εἴθε μήποτε γνοίης ὃς εἶ, mayst thou never learn who thou art, and εἰ γὰρ ἐν τούτῳ εἴη, may it only depend on that (Goodell §477). Gildersleeve's is εἰ γὰρ γενοίμην, τέκνον, ἀντὶ σοῦ νεκρός, if I could fall a corpse, my child, instead of thee (Gildersleeve §395).
- Potential: the whole English modal range, and the choice is a matter of tone. Smyth's list is may, might, can (especially with a negative), must, would, should, and rarely will and shall (§1824). Gildersleeve puts the range on a scale from strong assurance to faint presumption and says the combination is often used to indicate moral certainty rather than uncertainty (Gildersleeve §442). Goodwin says the vaguer cases can be rendered by perchance, possibly or probably, or by the auxiliaries, and that οὐκ ἂν μεθείμην τοῦ θρόνου is nearly absolute, I will not give up the throne (GMT §235).
- A potential with a negative often wants an English present of ability: οὐκ ἂν λάβοις is thou canst not take (§1824), and Goodell renders πρὸς βίᾱν δ᾽ οὐκ ἂν λάβοις as by force you would never take him (Goodell §479).
- The courteous potential takes an English imperative or an offer. λέγοις ἂν τὴν δέησιν is tell me your request and χωροῖς ἂν εἴσω σὺν τάχει is go within with all speed (§1830). Goodell's readings of the same forms are a modest request, a softened command, a determined refusal, a passionate wish and a polite question, sorted example by example (Goodell §480).
- πῶς ἄν takes "would that". Smyth's rendering of πῶς ἂν ὀλοίμᾱν is oh, would that I might die, and he adds that properly the usage is not a wish but a question how the wish may be fulfilled (§1832). Goodwin does not class it with the wishes, since ὀλοίμην ἄν and ὀλοίμην are in use wholly different constructions (GMT §728).
- βουλοίμην ἄν takes "I should like", and Goodell calls it a frequent formula and a softened expression of wish (Goodell §480). Gildersleeve pairs it with Latin velim and contrasts ἐβουλόμην ἄν with vellem (Gildersleeve §398).
- The potential of future realization takes an English future or a "will prove to be": ἀρετὴ ἄρα, ὡς ἔοικεν, ὑγίειά τις ἂν εἴη is virtue then, it seems, will prove to be a kind of health, and the perfect πῶς ἂν λελήθοι; is how can it have escaped my knowledge? (§1828). Goodwin's is Ποῦ δῆτ᾽ ἂν εἶεν οἱ ξένοι; where may the strangers be, that is, where is it likely to turn out that they are? (GMT §238).
- The oblique optative gets no modal English at all. It takes the tense the English sequence wants: ἔγνωσαν ὅτι κενὸς ὁ φόβος εἴη is they recognized that their fear was groundless (§2615), and Ἔλεξεν ὅτι γράφοι is he said that he was writing (GMT §669).
- Where the oblique optative could have been left alone, the English is the same either way. Goodwin says that after past tenses the indicative and optative are in equally good use, the optative when the writer incorporates the quotation entirely into his own sentence and the indicative when he quotes it in the original words as far as his own construction allows, and the indicative is merely a more vivid form of expression with no difference in meaning (GMT §670).
- A retained potential inside indirect discourse keeps its "would" in the English: Λέγει ὅτι τοῦτο ἂν ἐγένετο is he says that this would have happened (GMT §681).
- Where the potential optative with ἄν, especially with negatives, exchanges with the indicative, English follows the indicative. φημὶ καὶ οὐκ ἂν ἀρνηθείην is I assert and cannot deny, and οὐκ ἂν πέρᾱ φράσαιμι is I will speak no more; the courtesy Smyth reports is a comparison, that the optative with ἄν is often stronger, though more courteous, than the future indicative (§1833).
- Where a potential softens a claim or turns it ironic, keep the softening in the English: ἕτερόν τι τοῦτ᾽ ἂν εἴη is this is, or would be, another matter (§1826).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading an optative of wish as a judgement about likelihood. Goodwin puts the correction as a rule with no exceptions: it is the futurity of the object of a wish, and not its probability or possibility, that requires the optative, and no amount of absurdity or extravagance in a future wish can make anything but the optative proper (GMT §740). Gildersleeve's limit is about attainment and not probability either, that the use is confined to wishes whose fulfilment is still in suspense (Gildersleeve §388).
- Treating a wish and a potential as one construction because both may be rendered by "would". Goodwin separates ὀλοίμην ἄν from ὀλοίμην as wholly different constructions (GMT §728), and Smyth classes πῶς ἂν ὀλοίμᾱν as a potential and not a wish (§1832).
- Supplying a protasis for every potential optative. Smyth says it is unnecessary to supply any protasis in thought (§1825), Goodwin that there is no necessary or logical bond of union between the two forms (GMT §239), and Monro that it is erroneous to regard these as complex sentences with the protasis understood (Monro §300).
- Taking the word "potential" for a statement about the grammar. Goodell rejects the label: what is not really doubted may from caution or courtesy be treated as not yet certain, so that might, could, may and even must are convenient in translating, but the Greek expression is not properly potential (Goodell §480), and he calls the construction the hypothetical optative instead (Goodell §479). Gildersleeve records the objections and keeps the name for want of a better, adding that potentiality has to do with character and that the estimate goes back to the opinion of the one who makes it (Gildersleeve §434). The label is disputed; the construction is not.
- Reading an oblique optative as if the writer had chosen it for its meaning. It is a report, and the direct form is recoverable: ἔγνωσαν ὅτι κενὸς ὁ φόβος εἴη stands for ἐστί (§2615). Gildersleeve makes the same point by calling the optative the finite form of the infinitive and noting that the two run closely parallel in wishes, in commands, in indirect discourse and in complementary clauses (Gildersleeve §400).
- Expecting the oblique optative everywhere after a past tense. It is optional. The rule is that each primary tense of the indicative and each subjunctive may be either changed to the optative or retained (GMT §667), and Smyth's own wording is "may be changed" (§2599). Smyth adds that an indicative denoting unreality, with or without ἄν, is retained, and that imperfects and pluperfects are generally retained (§2599, §2623).
- Reading an unchanged ἐχρῆν or ἔδει in reported speech as an oversight. It is deliberate: the potential indicative with ἄν and the indicative in a condition denoting unreality, with ἄν or without it, always remain unchanged in order to prevent confusion with the optative of the direct form (§2623).
- Assuming a missing ἄν means the optative is not potential. It may mean that, and in Attic prose it usually does, since the potential use is confined to the optative with ἄν except in poetry (Gildersleeve §389). But the older language had the construction without the particle (§1821, GMT §240), and Gildersleeve's list of the ways ἄν could be lost from a text is worth reading before emending or before concluding (Gildersleeve §450).
- Taking every optative after οὐκ ἔστιν ὅστις as oblique. Smyth reads the optative after οὐκ ἔστιν ὅστις, ὅπως or ὅποι in the dramatists as probably potential, as in οὐκ ἔσθ᾽ ὅπως λέξαιμι τὰ ψευδῆ καλά, I could not call false tidings fair, and notes that ἄν is usually employed in that construction (§1822).
- Reading a Homeric potential of the past and an Herodotean one as the same thing. They are not, and two grammars say so. Goodwin says the optative of what may prove to have been true has nothing to do with the Homeric use of the optative with κέ or ἄν in a past sense (GMT §238), and Monro glosses the Herodotean one as would be found to have happened if we knew more, against the Homeric would have happened but did not (Monro §300). Smyth prints them together under one heading and calls both doubtful in Attic prose (§1829).
- Expecting a future optative with ἄν. Smyth says it occurs only in a few suspected passages (§1834), and Goodell that the future optative is not used in simple sentences at all (Goodell §483).
- Explaining the εἴθε wish as an elliptical condition and stopping there. Smyth reports it as an explanation and not a fact, that the optative introduced by εἰ γάρ is sometimes explained as a protasis with the conclusion omitted, as εἴθε φίλος ἡμῖν γένοιο (§1816). Gildersleeve attributes the same explanation to many grammarians (Gildersleeve §395). Goodwin holds it as his own view for the εἴθε form, that the optative in a wish with εἴθε, εἰ γάρ or εἰ is probably in its origin a protasis with the apodosis suppressed, while the pure optative in a wish is an independent verb; and he will not explain both forms the same way, saying it is no more logical to insist on explaining both forms of wishes alike than it would be in English to insist that "may I see him again" and "O if I might see him again" are originally of the same construction (GMT §722, GMT §723).
