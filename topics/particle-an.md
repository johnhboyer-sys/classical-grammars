# The Particle ἄν

## 1. Quick answer

The particle ἄν, Homeric κέν or κέ, limits the meaning of the moods, and it has two distinct uses: in independent clauses with the past tenses of the indicative and with the optative, and also with the infinitive and participle representing the indicative or optative; and in dependent clauses with the subjunctive (§1761). No separate word can be used to translate ἄν by itself, and its force varies as it modifies the meaning of the moods; in general it limits the force of the verb to particular conditions or circumstances, under the circumstances, in that case, then (§1762). GMT makes the same division and adds that in the first use we express ἄν by the form of the English verb, ἔλθοι ἄν he would go, ἦλθεν ἄν he would have gone, and in the second, with the subjunctive, it generally has no force that can be made perceptible in translation (GMT §193). ἄν is not used with the present and perfect indicative (§1794).

## 2. Recognition

- ἄν standing with a past tense of the indicative, which is the only part of the indicative that admits it (§1794). Gildersleeve puts the rule positively: ἄν belongs to that which is other than the present, and is found only in past and future relations (Gildersleeve §428).
- ἄν standing with the optative, forming the potential optative, which states a future possibility, propriety or likelihood as an opinion of the speaker (§1824).
- ἄν written into the conjunction: ἐᾱ́ν, ἤν, ᾱ̓́ν from εἰ, and ὅταν, ὁπόταν, ἐπήν, ἐπᾱ́ν, ἐπειδάν from ὅτε, ὁπότε, ἐπεί and ἐπειδή (§1768 a). The three forms of the first are ἐᾱ́ν, ordinary in Attic prose and inscriptions, ἤν, in Ionic and the older Attic writers, and ᾱ̓́ν, generally in the later writers (§2283 a).
- ἄν standing near a relative or temporal word with a subjunctive following: conditional, relative and temporal clauses requiring the subjunctive must have ἄν, which is more closely attached to the conditional, relative and temporal words than it is to the subjunctive (§1768).
- ἄν attached to a negative, an interrogative or another emphatic word rather than to its verb: οὐκ ἄν, τίς ἄν, πῶς ἄν (§1764).
- ἄν with an infinitive or a participle, standing for a finite form: ὥστε καὶ ἰδιώτην ἂν γνῶναι "so that even a common man could have understood" (§1848 a).
- ἄν with no verb at all, to be supplied from the context: πῶς ἄν; "how can (could) it be?"; τάχ᾽ ἄν "perhaps" (§1766).
- κᾱ̓́ν, which may be καί with the adverb ἄν, or καί with ᾱ̓́ν as a form of ἐᾱ́ν (§1766 b).
- Two or three ἄν in one clause, either to carry the conditional force through a long sentence or to give prominence to particular words (§1765).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. First find the verb ἄν belongs to, because it is often not the nearest one. In independent sentences with the indicative or the optative the particle is often separated from its verb for emphasis, and is attached to negatives, interrogatives, or any emphatic modifier, and it is commonly attached to verbs of saying or thinking: σὺν ῡ̔μῖν μὲν ἂν οἶμαι εἶναι τῑ́μιος "if I should remain with you, I think I should be esteemed", where ἄν belongs to εἶναι (§1764). The same displacement appears in οὐκ οἶδ᾽ ἂν εἰ πείσαιμι "I do not know whether I could persuade", for πείσαιμι ἄν (§1764 a). GMT warns that ἄν is often separated from its verb by such verbs as οἴομαι, δοκῶ, φημί and οἶδα, and care must be taken to connect the ἄν with the verb to which it really belongs (GMT §220).
2. Use the position rules as a check on that. ἄν does not begin a sentence or a clause, except after a weak mark of punctuation (§1764); GMT states the same and allows only that it may directly follow a parenthetic clause, provided some part of its own clause precedes (GMT §222). With the optative or indicative it may stand near the verb or attach to an emphatic word, very often directly after interrogatives, negatives and adverbs of time or place (GMT §219). Gildersleeve records that ἄν and κέν are both postpositive, apt to combine with a leading modifier, conjunction, relative or adverb, with only certain monosyllabic particles such as μέν, δέ, γάρ and τε allowed to intervene (Gildersleeve §459), and sorts the cases by what ἄν attaches to: the verb (Gildersleeve §460), a negative (Gildersleeve §461), an interrogative (Gildersleeve §462), a participle (Gildersleeve §463), and any leading modifier (Gildersleeve §464).
3. Before deciding anything, check that the ἄν has a verb at all. ἄν sometimes stands without a verb, which is to be supplied from the context, especially in the second member of a sentence with coördinate clauses, and in πῶς ἄν and τάχ᾽ ἄν (§1766). GMT collects the same ellipses (GMT §227) and adds the two senses of κἄν, καί with the adverb ἄν and καί with ᾱ̓́ν for ἐᾱ́ν, either of which may stand without a verb (GMT §228), and ἄν with a relative and no verb (GMT §229). Gildersleeve lists the same formulas, τάχ᾽ ἄν, mayhap, quite likely, and πῶς ἄν, how could it (Gildersleeve §458).
4. Do not read τάχ᾽ ἄν as a bare adverb of doubt. GMT states that τάχ᾽ ἄν cannot be used unless the ἄν belongs in its ordinary sense to the verb of the sentence, so that τάχ᾽ ἂν γένοιτο is it might perhaps happen and τάχ᾽ ἂν ἐγένετο is it might perhaps have happened, but the latter can never mean perhaps it happened (GMT §221). Smyth glosses τάχ᾽ ἄν as perhaps (§1766), which is a rendering of the phrase and not a licence to detach the particle from its verb.
5. If ἄν appears two or three times, do not look for two constructions. ἄν may be repeated once or twice in the same sentence, placed early to direct attention to the character of the construction, or added for rhetorical emphasis to give prominence to particular words: τίς γὰρ τοιαῦτ᾽ ἂν οὐκ ἂν ὀργίζοιτ᾽ ἔπη κλύων; "and who would not be angered upon hearing such words?" (§1765). GMT gives the same two reasons (GMT §223) and notes that a participle representing a protasis is especially apt to have an emphatic ἄν near it (GMT §224). Conversely, when an apodosis has several coördinate clauses with the same mood, ἄν is generally used only in the first (GMT §226); Smyth records the same omission and adds that ἄν is often left out with the second of two verbs that are connected or opposed (§1767).
6. Now identify the mood, because the mood decides everything else. The three answers are the indicative, the subjunctive and the optative, and they do not overlap.
7. If the mood is the indicative, first check the tense. The present and perfect indicative are never used with ἄν (§1794, GMT §195). Gildersleeve warns that because ἄν varies so much in position beginners mistake this, and names the two passages that used to be cited (Gildersleeve §433). With the future indicative, ἄν belongs to Homer and the early poets, where it has a conditional or limiting force: καί κέ τις ὧδ᾽ ἐρέει "and in such a case some one will (may) say thus" (§1793). GMT adds that the addition of ἄν seems to make the future more contingent than that tense naturally is, sometimes approaching the optative with ἄν (GMT §196), and that in Attic the construction is denied by many critics and survives only in a few passages (GMT §197). Gildersleeve calls the Attic future with ἄν a construction counted a solecism even in antiquity, most examples being corrected or corrigible (Gildersleeve §432).
8. With a past indicative, choose among three readings from the context, not from the tense. The past potential states past potentiality, probability or necessity: ἔγνω ἄν τις "one might (could, would) have known" (§1784). The unreal indicative denotes unreality: τότε δ᾽ αὐτὸ τὸ πρᾶγμ᾽ ἂν ἐκρῑ́νετο ἐφ᾽ αὑτοῦ "but the case would then have been decided on its own merits" (§1786). The iterative indicative expresses repeated or customary past action: διηρώτων ἄν "I used to ask", ἂν ἔλεξεν "he was wont to say" (§1790). Goodell lists all three under one heading for each tense, as action occurring from time to time, as action that was to be expected or probably true, and as action in an imaginary case known to be unreal (Goodell §461, Goodell §467).
9. Keep those three apart by asking what is being denied. The use of the indicative with ἄν to denote unreality is not inherent in the past tenses but developed from the past potential (§1787), and the iterative construction is connected with the past potential too, having denoted originally what could or would take place under certain past circumstances, and having become in actual use a statement of fact because the action did take place (§1791). So the test is whether the action happened: with the iterative it did, repeatedly; with the unreal it did not. Smyth warns that iterative cases are not to be confused with the apodoses of unreal conditions (§2341). Gildersleeve separates the unreal (Gildersleeve §429) from the potential of the past (Gildersleeve §430) and from intermittent action (Gildersleeve §431), and notes that in the last ἄν is used without definite reference.
10. Read the tense of an unreal indicative for its time, not for its stage. The imperfect refers to the present or the past, the aorist to the past and rarely to the present, and the pluperfect to the present and less commonly to the past (§1788). Gildersleeve gives the same three with what each opposes: the imperfect opposes a continued action in present or past, the aorist opposes attainment chiefly in the past, and the pluperfect opposes completion more frequently in the present (Gildersleeve §429). Goodell shows all of them working together in one passage of Lysias (Goodell §469).
11. If the mood is the subjunctive, expect ἄν to belong to the conjunction and to add nothing you can translate. Conditional, relative and temporal clauses requiring the subjunctive must have ἄν, more closely attached to the conditional, relative and temporal word than to the verb (§1768), and the force of ἄν with the subjunctive cannot usually be expressed in English (§1768 b). In Attic prose the subjunctive with ἄν is not employed as a form of independent statement (Gildersleeve §451), and GMT says the same, that ἄν is regular with the subjunctive in protasis, in conditional relative sentences and sometimes in final clauses with ὡς and ὅπως, but never in independent sentences (GMT §200).
12. The exception to that is Homer, so check the author. The subjunctive with ἄν, more commonly with κέν, is used in Homer in independent sentences and clauses, with the negative οὐ: ἐγὼ δέ κ᾽ ἄγω Βρῑσηίδα "but in that case I will take Briseis" (§1813). Gildersleeve records the same Homeric use of the subjunctive with κεν or ἄν as a form of independent statement, with the negative οὐ (Gildersleeve §452), and notes that the aorist is the tense used in all his examples of ἄν and that all except two are negative (Gildersleeve §455). GMT reads that Homeric subjunctive as a future potential expression, nearly equivalent to the future indicative with κέ or ἄν and sometimes approaching the optative with κέ or ἄν (GMT §201). Monro takes the particle to point to an actual occurrence in the future, and counts κέν about 630 times in the Iliad and 520 in the Odyssey against 192 and 157 for ἄν (Monro §362).
13. If the mood is the optative, read a potential. The potential optative with ἄν states a future possibility, propriety or likelihood, as an opinion of the speaker, and may be translated by may, might, can, must, would, should, and rarely will or shall (§1824). No definite condition need be supplied: usually these optatives are not limited by any definite condition present to the mind, though in some cases a protasis is dormant in a word of the sentence such as δικαίως or εἰκότως (§1825). Gildersleeve calls it the mood of qualified assertion, expressing the opinion of the speaker as an opinion (Gildersleeve §434), and Goodell calls it the hypothetical optative, expressing what would be in a supposed case or in any case likely to occur (Goodell §479).
14. Having found a potential optative, ask what tone it carries, because the range is wide. It may soften the statement of an opinion or fact, or express irony (§1826); with a negative it may have the force of a strong assertion (§1826 a); it may be used in a sense akin to the imperative for a command, exhortation or request (§1830); it is used in questions (§1831); and πῶς ἄν or τίς ἄν with it may express a wish, which properly is not a wish but a question how the wish may be fulfilled (§1832). Gildersleeve puts the same range as a scale from strong assurance to faint presumption, with may be convenient for the positive and can't for the negative (Gildersleeve §442), and separates the imperative use (Gildersleeve §443), the use in questions, where the optative with ἄν in the question expects the optative with ἄν in the answer (Gildersleeve §445), and πῶς ἄν for a wish (Gildersleeve §446). Goodell reaches the same conclusion, that what is not really doubted may from caution or courtesy be treated as not yet certain, so that the Greek expression is not properly potential (Goodell §480).
15. Do not look for a future optative with ἄν. It occurs only in a few suspected passages (§1834). Gildersleeve explains why: it could arise only from the future indicative with ἄν, and that had gone out, if it ever came in, before the future optative came in (Gildersleeve §441); GMT says the same (GMT §203). Do not emend one away, though: the same section of Gildersleeve adds that it is still found in many texts and is not to be discarded in the later time, and prints δόξοιτ᾽ ἄν from Lycurgus among the passages (Gildersleeve §441).
16. If ἄν stands with an infinitive or a participle, resolve it into a finite form before translating. The infinitive or participle with ἄν represents either a past tense of the indicative with ἄν or the optative with ἄν, and the context determines which (§1845). The present represents the imperfect indicative with ἄν or the present optative with ἄν (§1846), the aorist represents the aorist indicative or the aorist optative with ἄν (§1848), and the perfect infinitive represents the pluperfect indicative or the perfect optative with ἄν (§1849). GMT states the rule in the same words and adds that the infinitive can be used with ἄν in all cases in which a finite verb would have ἄν if it stood in its place (GMT §204, GMT §205, GMT §206). Gildersleeve gives the same instruction, that when ἄν is used with an infinitive or participle the clause must be resolved by an indicative or an optative according to the context (Gildersleeve §457), and Goodell repeats it for each (Goodell §579, Goodell §595).
17. If the sentence is conditional, go to the table rather than to the particle. Smyth classifies the forms by the protasis, εἰ with the indicative, ἐᾱ́ν rarely εἰ with the subjunctive, and εἰ with the optative, and the apodosis by whether it has ἄν or not (§2289). The particle ἄν is used in the apodosis with the optative to denote possibility, and with the past tenses of the indicative to denote either the non-fulfilment of the condition or, occasionally, repetition (§2284). The table of forms sets all six side by side (§2297).
18. Then place your sentence in one of Smyth's six numbered forms. Simple present and past conditions have the indicative in both members and no ἄν: εἰ ταῦτα ποιεῖς, καλῶς ποιεῖς "if you do this, you do well" (§2298). Unreal conditions have εἰ with the imperfect, aorist or pluperfect indicative and ἄν with those tenses in the apodosis: εἰ ταῦτα ἐποίησας, καλῶς ἂν ἐποίησας "if you had done this, you would have done well" (§2303, §2305). More vivid future conditions have ἄν only inside ἐᾱ́ν: ἐᾱ̀ν ταῦτα ποιῇς (ποιήσῃς), καλῶς ποιήσεις "if you do this, you will do well" (§2323). Less vivid future conditions have εἰ with the optative and ἄν with the optative in the apodosis: εἰ ταῦτα ποιοίης, καλῶς ἂν ποιοίης "if you should do this, you would do well" (§2329). Present general conditions have ἐᾱ́ν with the subjunctive and a present indicative apodosis with no ἄν: ἐᾱ̀ν ταῦτα ποιῇς (ποιήσῃς), σὲ ἐπαινῶ "if ever you do this, I always praise you" (§2337). Past general conditions have εἰ with the optative and an imperfect apodosis with no ἄν: εἰ ταῦτα ποιοίης (ποιήσειας), σὲ ἐπῄνουν "if ever you did this, I always praised you" (§2340). Beside those six stands the Emotional Future, which Smyth counts a variety of the more vivid future class (§2321) and which has εἰ with the future indicative in the protasis and commonly the future indicative in the apodosis, with no ἄν in either: εἰ ταῦτα λέξεις, ἐχθαρεῖ μὲν ἐξ ἐμοῦ "if thou speakest thus, thou wilt be hated by me" (§2328).
19. Two of those six can look alike, and ἄν is what separates them. A past general condition takes the imperfect without ἄν (§2340), but it may also take the iterative imperfect or aorist with ἄν: εἰ δέ τις αὐτῷ περί του ἀντιλέγοι, ἐπὶ τὴν ὑπόθεσιν ἐπανῆγεν ἂν πάντα τὸν λόγον "if ever any one opposed him on any matter, he would always bring the entire discussion back to the main point" (§2341). That ἄν is the iterative one of §1790, not the unreal one of §1786, and the protasis in the optative is the mark of it.
20. If an unreal apodosis has no ἄν, look for an impersonal expression. ἄν may be omitted in the apodosis of an unreal condition when the apodosis consists of an imperfect indicative denoting unfulfilled obligation, possibility or propriety, such as ἔδει, χρῆν, ἐξῆν, εἰκὸς ἦν and καλὸν ἦν with the infinitive: εἰ ταῦτα ἐποίει, ἔδει (ἐξῆν) αἰτιᾶσθαι αὐτόν "if he were doing this (as he is not), one ought to (might) blame him" (§2313). There ἔδει and ἐξῆν are auxiliaries and the emphasis falls on the infinitive (§2313 a). Those same impersonals are used without ἄν in simple sentences too, with the imperfect indicative denoting obligation, propriety, necessity or possibility, and there the action of the dependent infinitive is usually not realized (§1774).
21. Then ask what the sentence is denying, because the same impersonals take ἄν when the denial moves. With the same expressions ἄν is regularly used when the obligation, possibility or propriety, and not the action of the dependent verb, is denied: εἰ ταῦτα ἐποίει, ἔδει (ἐξῆν) ἂν αἰτιᾶσθαι αὐτόν "if he were doing this (as he is not), it would be necessary (possible) to blame him", but as the case now stands it is not (§2315). So the presence of ἄν here is not decoration; it moves the denial from the infinitive to the impersonal.
22. If ἄν turns up inside the protasis, the protasis is itself an apodosis. The potential optative with ἄν or the unreal indicative with ἄν, standing in the conditional clause with εἰ, is the apodosis of another protasis expressed or understood (§2353). Smyth contrasts them: εἰ λέγοιμ᾽ ἄν is supposing I would say, whereas εἰ λέγοιμι is supposing I should say (§2356). Gildersleeve marks off a different case, adherescent ἄν, where the ἄν adheres to the leading particle and yet the subjunctive is changed into the optative, which is really anacoluthic; his instance is a temporal clause, ἕως ἂν οἱ νόμοι τεθεῖεν, so it will not be met in a conditional protasis (Gildersleeve §448).
23. If ἄν seems to be missing from a subjunctive clause, check the date and the genre before emending. Smyth notes that by retention of earlier usage the subjunctive is sometimes used without ἄν where the later language commonly employs it, and that the difference is scarcely appreciable except that the omission gives an archaic tone (§1767). In Homer the pure subjunctive is regular in many places where Attic requires ἄν: εἰ alone without κέ or ἄν occurs with no appreciable difference from εἴ κε (§2327 a). Gildersleeve says that subjunctive dependent clauses outside the pure final sentence regularly take ἄν except in the older language, and that omissions may be clerical error, euphony, or a survival of the older construction (Gildersleeve §456). Goodell adds the systematic reason: ἄν is not used with subordinate μή clauses nor with quoted dubitative subjunctives, because it was not used with the simple subjunctive sentences from which those clauses arose (Goodell §666).
24. In Homer, count κέν and ἄν separately rather than treating them as one word. In Homer ἄν is preferred in negative, κέν and κέ in relative, sentences (§1763). That preference is Smyth's, Gildersleeve's and GMT's; Monro does not state it. Monro supplies the counts, and the theory that ἄν was unknown in the original Homeric dialect, with a systematic attempt to restore the exclusive use of κεν (Monro §362). Gildersleeve gives the ratio for the Iliad as four κέν to one ἄν and notes that ἄν is everywhere distinctly preferred in negative sentences and gradually thrusts κέν to the wall (Gildersleeve §426). GMT's own tally is 621 κέ against 155 ἄν in Homer, and it holds that it is still safe to assume the two particles are used in substantially the same sense in all epic and lyric poetry (GMT §194). The three tallies do not agree and the page does not reconcile them: Monro counts about 630 κεν in the Iliad and 520 in the Odyssey against 192 and 157 for ἄν (Monro §362), which is neither Gildersleeve's four to one for the Iliad (Gildersleeve §426) nor GMT's 621 against 155 for the whole of Homer, and GMT sends the reader to Monro for figures it does not reproduce (GMT §194).
25. When the context does not decide, say which two readings are open and stop. Smyth includes that case in the rule for the infinitive and participle, where the context determines whether the indicative or the optative is meant (§1845), and GMT does the same (GMT §204). For the past indicative, Smyth's own note that a protasis may often be extracted from a participle or intimated in some other word, but that there is no reference to any definite condition and no definite ellipsis is to be supplied, is a warning against inventing the condition the English seems to want (§1785). Gildersleeve makes the same distinction between ἄν used with a definite reference contained in the sentence or implied in the context, and ἄν used without a definite reference, in which case no definite ellipsis is to be supplied (Gildersleeve §424).

## 4. Translation guidance

- Nothing in English answers to ἄν by itself; it shows in the form of the verb (§1762). GMT gives the two English forms: ἔλθοι ἄν he would go, ἦλθεν ἄν he would have gone (GMT §193).
- With a definite reference Gildersleeve translates it then, in that case, or, when opposition is implied, else; without a definite reference it sometimes gives a potential colouring, and with the subjunctive and the optative it is little more than a sharper future (Gildersleeve §427).
- Past potential: one might, could or would have done it. ἔγνω ἄν τις is "one might (could, would) have known", and ἐπέγνως ἄν is "you would (could, might) have observed", where the second person is the ideal one (§1784, §1784 a).
- Unreal: would have. εἰ ταῦτα ἐποίεις, καλῶς ἂν ἐποίεις is "if you were (now) doing this, you would be doing well" or "if you had been doing this, you would have been doing well" (§2304).
- Iterative: used to, would. διηρώτων ἄν is "I used to ask" (§1790), and Smyth's expanded version supplies the sense: ἀναλαμβάνων οὖν αὐτῶν τὰ ποιήματα is the setting, and the whole means "accordingly, taking up their poems, I used to (would) ask them (as an opportunity presented itself) what they meant" (§1791). Goodell renders the same sentence "Taking up their poems, I would question them", glossing it of different poets at one time and another (Goodell §461).
- ἐβουλόμην ἄν is I should like or should have liked, and may express an unattainable wish: ἐβουλόμην ἂν Σίμωνα τὴν αὐτὴν γνώμην ἐμοὶ ἔχειν "I should have liked Simon to be (or I wish Simon were) of the same mind as myself" (§1789). βουλοίμην ἄν is the softened optative of wish, "I could wish that this might be the result" (§1827). Goodell notes the same formula as a frequent softened expression of wish (Goodell §480).
- Potential optative: the whole range from may to must. γνοίης δ᾽ ἂν ὅτι τοῦθ᾽ οὕτως ἔχει "you may see that this is so"; ἡδέως ἂν ἐροίμην "I should like to ask"; οὐκ ἂν λάβοις "thou canst not take" (§1824).
- A softened opinion: ἕτερόν τι τοῦτ᾽ ἂν εἴη "this is (would be) another matter" (§1826).
- A courteous command: λέγοις ἂν τὴν δέησιν "tell me (you may tell) your request"; προάγοις ἄν "move on" (§1830). Goodell renders the type the same way, σὺ μὲν κομίζοις ἂν σεαυτόν "you may take yourself off" (Goodell §480).
- A rhetorical question: τίς οὐκ ἂν ὁμολογήσειεν; "who would not agree?", where the answer supplied is οὐδείς (§1831).
- A wish in the form of a question: πῶς ἂν ὀλοίμᾱν "oh, would that I might die" (§1832).
- A strong assertion, not a weak one: φημὶ καὶ οὐκ ἂν ἀρνηθείην "I assert and cannot deny", where the optative with ἄν is often stronger, though more courteous, than the future indicative (§1833). Gildersleeve says the same, that so far from necessarily denoting uncertainty it is the combination most frequently used to indicate moral certainty (Gildersleeve §442).
- The infinitive with ἄν takes whatever English the finite form would take: ἀκούω Λακεδαιμονίους ἂν ἀναχωρεῖν ἐπ᾽ οἴκου "I hear the Lacedaemonians used to return home", for ἂν ἀνεχώρουν (§1846 a).
- The participle with ἄν likewise: πόλλ᾽ ἂν ἔχων ἕτερ᾽ εἰπεῖν, παραλείπω "though I might be able to say much else I pass it by", for ἂν ἔχοιμι (§1846 b).
- κἄν in a concessive clause is even if, and καὶ εἰ commonly implies that the conclusion must be true even in the extreme, scarcely conceivable case: κεἰ μὴ πέποιθα, τοὖργον ἔστ᾽ ἐργαστέον "even if I have no confidence, yet the deed must be done" (§2372). Concessive clauses are formed by καί with the εἰ or ἐᾱ́ν of conditional clauses, καὶ εἰ and καὶ ἐᾱ́ν even if, εἰ καί and ἐᾱ̀ν καί although (§2369).
- Where ἄν has no verb, translate the ellipsis: οἶδα ὅτι πολλοὺς μὲν ἡγεμόνας ἂν δοίη, πολλοὺς δ᾽ ἂν (δοίη) ὁμήρους "I know that he would give many guides and many hostages" (§1766).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Looking for a word to translate ἄν. There is none; the force varies as it modifies the meaning of the moods (§1762).
- Attaching ἄν to the nearest verb. It is often separated from its verb for emphasis and attached to a negative, an interrogative or an emphatic modifier (§1764), and GMT warns specifically about οἴομαι, δοκῶ, φημί and οἶδα standing between (GMT §220).
- Reading τάχ᾽ ἄν as perhaps with a past indicative. τάχ᾽ ἂν ἐγένετο is it might perhaps have happened and never perhaps it happened (GMT §221).
- Reading a repeated ἄν as two constructions. It may be repeated once or twice in the same sentence (§1765), and GMT and Gildersleeve both record the repetition as normal (GMT §223, Gildersleeve §467).
- Expecting ἄν with a present or perfect indicative. ἄν is not used with the present and perfect indicative (§1794), and the instances of ἄν with the future indicative in manuscripts and editions have mostly been corrected or are easily corrigible (Gildersleeve §432).
- Reading a past indicative with ἄν as unreal when it is iterative. The iterative imperfect and aorist with ἄν express repeated or customary past action (§1790), and Smyth warns that these are not to be confused with the apodoses of unreal conditions (§2341).
- Reading a past indicative with ἄν as iterative when it is a cautious statement. The past potential denotes past potentiality, probability or necessity, especially with τὶς and with the ideal second person (§1784 a). Goodell gives the same two readings of the aorist with ἄν, action occurring from time to time on occasion, and action that was to be expected or was probably true (Goodell §467).
- Supplying a protasis for every potential optative. Usually these optatives are not limited by any definite condition, and it is unnecessary to supply any protasis in thought (§1825); for the past potential Smyth says that a definite ellipsis is not to be supplied (§1785).
- Reading the optative with ἄν as always tentative. It ranges from possibility to fixed resolve (§1824 a), and with a negative it may amount to a strong assertion (§1826 a).
- Using the optative with ἄν where Greek would state a fact. When stress is laid on the idea of possibility and power, necessity and obligation, Greek uses δύναμαι, δεῖ or χρή with the infinitive (§1824 b).
- Expecting ἄν to add something translatable to a subjunctive. Its force there cannot usually be expressed in English (§1768 b), and in Attic prose the subjunctive with ἄν is not an independent statement at all (Gildersleeve §451).
- Treating a Homeric independent subjunctive with κέν as an error. It is a regular Homeric construction, with the negative οὐ (§1813), and GMT reads it as a future potential (GMT §201).
- Treating κέν and ἄν as interchangeable in Homer without checking. ἄν is preferred in negative and κέν in relative sentences (§1763), and Monro reports that the two are not evenly distributed (Monro §362).
- Emending away a subjunctive that lacks ἄν. The omission gives an archaic tone rather than an error (§1767), and Homer uses εἰ alone with no appreciable difference from εἴ κε (§2327 a).
- Expecting a future optative with ἄν. It occurs only in a few suspected passages (§1834); Gildersleeve holds that the future optative with ἄν is not in use, since it could arise only from the future indicative with ἄν, which had gone out before the future optative came in, while adding that it is still found in many texts and is not to be discarded in the later time (Gildersleeve §441).
- Translating an infinitive or participle with ἄν as if ἄν were adverbial. Resolve it first into the indicative or optative it represents (§1845).
- Concluding that an unreal apodosis without ἄν is defective. ἄν is regularly absent when the apodosis is ἔδει, χρῆν, ἐξῆν or a like impersonal with the infinitive (§2313), and its presence there changes what is denied (§2315).
- Reading a protasis that contains ἄν as an ordinary protasis. It is the apodosis of another protasis expressed or understood (§2353).
- Forgetting that ἐᾱ́ν, ὅταν and the like already contain the particle, so that the clause needs nothing more (§1768 a). Gildersleeve adds that these particles rarely forget their belongings, so that an optative after them is more than suspicious in good Greek (Gildersleeve §449).
