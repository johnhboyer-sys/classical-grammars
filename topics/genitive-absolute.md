## 1. Quick answer

A genitive absolute is a circumstantial participle agreeing with a genitive noun or pronoun that is not in the main construction of the sentence (§2070). The participle has its own subject, and that subject is normally not the subject of the leading verb (§2058); the exception is emphatic (§2073). Like every other circumstantial participle, the genitive absolute expresses time, cause, condition, concession, or simply an attendant circumstance (§2070). Translate it as a subordinate clause: "while Conon was in command" for Κόνωνος στρατηγοῦντος, "this said" for τούτων λεχθέντων (§2070 a). The participle itself does not say which relation is meant; the context decides, and often the context does not decide (§2069).

## 2. Recognition

- A noun or pronoun in the genitive, a participle in the genitive agreeing with it, and nothing in the main construction of the sentence to account for the genitive (§2070).
- The participle alone, with no noun or pronoun, when the noun can be supplied from the context: προσιόντων "as they were approaching", the Greeks having been mentioned just before (§2072 a).
- The participle alone when a general word such as ἀνθρώπων or πραγμάτων is understood: ἰόντων εἰς μάχην "when (men) are going into battle" (§2072 b). With verbs of weather the word understood is Διός: ὕοντος πολλῷ "when it was raining hard" (§2072 b, GMT §848).
- A passive participle in the genitive, without a noun, followed by a clause with ὅτι: ἐσαγγελθέντων ὅτι "it having been announced that" (§2072 c). The participle is plural when the subject of the ὅτι clause is plural or several circumstances are mentioned (§2072 c).
- ἑκών "willing" and ἄκων "unwilling" are treated as participles and stand in the genitive absolute: ἐμοῦ οὐχ ἑκόντος "without my consent" (§2071).
- With a substantive, prose adds the participle ὤν: παίδων ὄντων ἡμῶν "when we were children" (§2074). Its omission in the genitive absolute is very rare in prose, but it happens: ἡμέρᾱς ἤδη (οὔσης) "it being already day" (§2117 a).
- Particles that make the relation explicit. καίπερ marks opposition or concession (§2083). ἅτε, οἷα and οἷον state the cause as a fact on the authority of the speaker or writer (§2085). ὡς gives the ground of belief in the mind of the subject, without implicating the speaker or writer (§2086). ὥσπερ compares the action of the main verb with an assumed case (§2087).
- The genitive absolute usually precedes the main verb (§2073).
- In Homer the aorist participle is less common in this construction than the present (Monro §246).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Find the genitive participle and the genitive noun or pronoun it agrees with (§2070).
2. Ask whether something in the sentence governs the genitive. The construction arose from genitives that depended on a governing expression, as Γλαύκου ἀπιόντος depends on ἄχος γένετο, and was then extended to cases in which the governing expression did not take the genitive (§2075). So a genitive that can depend on a word in the sentence may not be absolute at all. If the participle can be joined with a substantive already belonging to the construction, the genitive absolute is not the regular choice (GMT §850).
3. Ask whether the genitive noun is also the subject of the leading verb. Normally the two subjects are not identical (§2058). When they are, the construction is irregular and emphasizes the idea contained in the genitive absolute: βοηθησάντων ῡ̔μῶν προθῡ́μως "if you assist us heartily, you will gain" (§2073).
4. Ask whether the noun appears elsewhere in the leading clause. The genitive absolute may be used where the grammatical construction demands the dative: διαβεβηκότος Περικλέους ... ἠγγέλθη αὐτῷ "when Pericles had already crossed over, news was brought to him" (§2073 a). Its subject may be identical with the object of the leading verb (§2073 b).
5. If the participle is neuter and impersonal, or has an infinitive as its subject, expect the accusative absolute, not the genitive (§2076). Impersonal verbs give δέον, ἐξόν, παρόν, προσῆκον; passive participles used impersonally give εἰρημένον, προσταχθέν; adjectives with ὄν give δυνατὸν ὄν, ἀδύνατον ὄν (§2076). Quasi-impersonal verbs take the genitive rather than the accusative absolute: οὕτως ἔχοντος "in this state of things" (§2072 b). The participle of a personal verb may be used absolutely, in the accusative, when ὡς or ὥσπερ precedes (§2078).
6. Decide the relation from the context and from any adverb or particle: time, cause, opposition or concession, condition, or attendant circumstance (§2070). If it is impossible to assign the participle exclusively to one relation, leave the English open as well (§2060).
7. Read the negative. The participle has οὐ when it states a fact and μή when it states a condition (§2728). A participle expressing a condition always has μή (§2067). Participles of opposition or concession take οὐ, with or without καίπερ (§2733). The participle with ὡς, ὥσπερ, ἅτε, οἷον or οἷα has οὐ; μή occurs there only after an imperative or a conditional word (§2732).
8. Read the tense as stage of action, not as absolute time. The time denoted by the participle is only relative to that of the governing verb and is inferred from the context (§2061). Whether the action is antecedent, coincident or subsequent to that of the leading verb depends on the context, and the present participle is generally coincident (§1872). When the genitive absolute is substituted for a protasis, Smyth's rule is that the present participle represents the imperfect and the perfect the pluperfect, yet his first example, δίκης οὔσης for εἰ δίκη ἐστίν, stands for a present; take the tense from the context (§2344).

## 4. Translation guidance

- Translate the participle as a subordinate clause with a conjunction of time, condition or cause, or as an adverbial phrase, or as a separate finite verb (§2054 a).
- Time: Κόνωνος στρατηγοῦντος "while Conon was in command"; τούτων λεχθέντων ἀνέστησαν "this said, they rose" (§2070 a).
- Cause: τῶν σωμάτων θηλῡνομένων "by the enfeebling of the body" (§2070 b).
- Opposition or concession: καὶ μεταπεμπομένου αὐτοῦ "even though he is sending for me" (§2070 c).
- Condition: τούτων γιγνομένων "if these measures should be taken" (§2070 d).
- Attendant circumstance: οὐδενὸς κωλῡ́οντος "without opposition", literally "no one hindering", or "since no one opposed him" (§2070 e).
- The English nominative absolute is the formal match: ἡμῶν καθημένων, ὅπερ ἔλεγον, μέγα ᾖσεν ὁ ἀλεκτρυών is "we sitting, as I said, the cock crew loud" (§2058). Use it only where an English writer would.
- "Without" often fits a negated genitive absolute, whether the negative is on the participle or on its subject: ἐμοῦ οὐχ ἑκόντος "without my consent" (§2071); οὐδενὸς κωλῡ́οντος "without any one preventing him" (§2058).
- ὡς with a genitive absolute: "in the belief that", "on the ground that", "under the impression that", "under pretence of", or "as if"; there is nothing conditional in the Greek, and the negative is οὐ (§2086 b). Example: ἧλθον ἐπὶ τὴν Ἐπίδαυρον ὡς ἐρήμου οὔσης ... αἱρήσοντες "they came against Epidaurus expecting to capture it undefended", where ὡς ἐρήμου οὔσης gives the belief they came in (§2073 b).
- ἅτε, οἷα, οἷον: "inasmuch as": οἷα δὴ ἀπιόντων πρὸς δεῖπνον τῶν πελταστῶν "inasmuch as the peltasts were going off to supper" (§2085).
- καίπερ: "though", "although": καίπερ μέσου χειμῶνος ὄντος "though it was midwinter" (§2083).
- For a reader who knows Latin, the genitive absolute differs from the ablative absolute in four ways (§2074). The subject need not be expressed: the participle may stand without its noun or pronoun (§2072). The subject may appear in the leading clause (§2073 a). With a substantive the participle ὤν is always added in prose, whereas Latin has to omit the participle: παίδων ὄντων ἡμῶν is nobis pueris (§2074). Greek can use the genitive absolute where Latin has to use a clause with cum or dum, because Greek has a present participle passive and an aorist and perfect participle active (§2074). Latin uses the absolute case more frequently, because it uses the perfect participle passive where Greek uses the aorist participle active agreeing with the subject: Κῦρος συγκαλέσᾱς τοὺς στρατηγοὺς εἶπεν is Cyrus, convocatis ducibus, dixit (§2074).
- In effect the genitive absolute is an abbreviated adverbial clause of time, cause, condition, concession, or attendant circumstance, and often one cannot say which relation is more prominent (Goodell §590).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Taking the genitive as possessive or partitive and attaching it to a nearby noun. Ask whether the genitive is dependent on a governing expression; the absolute construction was extended to cases in which the governing expression did not take the genitive (§2075).
- Calling every genitive participle absolute. The genitive absolute is regularly used only when a new subject is introduced into the sentence, and not when the participle can be joined with a substantive already belonging to the construction (GMT §850).
- Making the genitive noun the subject of the main verb. Its subject is normally not identical with the subject of the leading verb (§2058); when it is, the construction is exceptional and emphatic (§2073).
- Looking for a noun that is not there. The participle may stand without its noun or pronoun when the noun is easily supplied from the context or supplied grammatically (§2072).
- Reading a neuter participle such as ἐξόν or δέον as a genitive that has lost its noun. These are accusative absolutes of impersonal verbs (§2076); the summary gives συνδόξαν τῷ πατρὶ καὶ τῇ μητρί "on the approval of his father and mother" (§2059).
- Translating ὡς with a participle as "as if" in the conditional sense. The use of ὡς implies nothing as to the opinion of the speaker or writer (§2086), and the negative οὐ shows that there is nothing conditional in it (§2732).
- Forcing one relation on the participle when the context does not decide between a temporal, a causal, a conditional and a concessive force (§2069).
- Reading the tense as absolute time. The participle is timeless; its tenses express only continuance, simple occurrence, and completion with permanent result (§1872).
- Expecting ὤν to be omitted. With a substantive, prose adds ὤν (§2074); with the genitive absolute the omission is very rare in prose, and the particles ὡς, ἅτε, οἷα or καίπερ usually precede when it is omitted, though ἡμέρᾱς ἤδη (οὔσης) has none (§2117 a).
- Treating a dative with a participle in an expression of time as absolute. Ξενοφῶντι πορευομένῳ "while Xenophon was on the march" is the dative of the person interested in the action, a different construction (§1498).
