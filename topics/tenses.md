# Tenses

and `topics/middle-voice.md`. Every claim carries a section citation;
`scripts/verify_topic_pages.py` checks them. `topic: tenses` names the

## 1. Quick answer

A Greek tense carries two things at once, and only one of them is time. By the tenses are denoted the time of an action, present, past or future, and the stage of an action, that is action continued or repeated, action simply brought to pass, and action completed with a permanent result (§1850). Goodwin states the same pair and adds where each is found: the character of the action is expressed by the tenses in all the moods and in the infinitive and the participle, while the time is always expressed in the indicative and only to a certain extent in the dependent moods and the participle (GMT §20). Gildersleeve's names for the two are the stage of the action, or kind of time, and the period of the action, or sphere of time (Gildersleeve §184). So a reader who asks only "when did this happen?" is asking half the question. Outside the indicative it is the wrong half: only in the indicative do the tenses show time absolutely present, past or future (§1851), and the tenses of the moods except the indicative do not express time in independent sentences (§1859).

## 2. Recognition

- Three stems doing the work. Continued action is denoted by the present stem, γράφω I am writing and ἔγραφον I was writing; completed action with permanent result by the perfect stem, γέγραφα ἐπιστολήν I have written a letter and it is now finished; action simply brought to pass by the aorist, ἔγραψα I wrote (§1852).
- Seven tenses, unevenly distributed. Goodwin counts them and notes that the imperfect and pluperfect occur only in the indicative, and that the futures are wanting in the subjunctive and imperative (GMT §19).
- A present that is not present time. In lively or dramatic narration the present may be used to represent a past action as going on at the moment of speaking or writing, as ὁ δὲ Θεμιστοκλῆς φεύγει ἐς Κέρκῡραν (§1883). Goodwin's name for it is the historic present, used in narration for the aorist and sometimes for the imperfect, to give a more animated statement of past events (GMT §33).
- A present used to register facts, which is the annalistic present, close to the historical present: Δᾱρείου καὶ Παρυσάτιδος γίγνονται παῖδες δύο (§1884). Goodwin prints the same sentence among his historic presents (GMT §33).
- An aorist stating what is always so. The aorist may express a general truth, and it simply states a past occurrence and leaves the reader to draw the inference that what has occurred once is typical of what often occurs: παθὼν δέ τε νήπιος ἔγνω, a fool learns by experience (§1931). Goodell gives it the same name from γνώμη, a proverb, and prints Τύχη τέχνην ὤρθωσεν, οὐ τέχνη τύχην (Goodell §465).
- A perfect that English renders with a present: κέκλημαι am called, κέκτημαι possess, μέμνημαι remember, τέθνηκα am dead, οἶδα know (§1946).
- An aorist where English wants a perfect: πολλάκις ἐθαύμασα, I have often wondered (Goodell §463).
- A verb with two futures, the one from the present stem and the one from the aorist stem: ἕξω I shall have beside σχήσω I shall get, though ἕξω usually does duty for σχήσω (§1911).
- A future perfect, which denotes a future state resulting from a completed action: ἀναγεγράψομαι I shall stand enrolled, ἡ θύρᾱ κεκλῄσεται the door will be kept shut (§1955).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Ask first whether the verb is in the indicative. In independent clauses only the tenses of the indicative denote absolute time, and the tenses of the subjunctive, optative, imperative, infinitive and participle do not refer to the differences in kind of time, so that γράφειν and γράψαι alike may be used of the present, the past or the future according to the context (§1850). Gildersleeve says the same: in classic prose the indicative alone expresses the sphere of time directly, and the other moods express it indirectly (Gildersleeve §299).
2. If it is not in the indicative, read the tense as aspect and nothing else. Goodwin states the rule: the present and aorist there differ only in this, that the present expresses an action in its duration, as going on or repeated, while the aorist expresses simply its occurrence, the time of both tenses being otherwise precisely the same, so that ἐὰν ποιῇ τοῦτο and ἐὰν ποιήσῃ τοῦτο differ in kind and not in date (GMT §87). The imperative pair is the clearest of all: ποίει τοῦτο is do this habitually and ποίησον τοῦτο is simply do this (GMT §87).
3. Read Smyth's subjunctive and imperative examples beside Goodwin's, because they say the same thing twice. The subjunctive as such refers to the future and its tenses denote only the stage of the action (§1860); the imperative always implies future time and its tenses likewise denote only the stage (§1864). Gildersleeve's contrast is σκοπώμεθα let us consider a course of study against σκεψώμεθα let us consider each part by itself (Gildersleeve §304). Goodell states the rule for the subjunctive and adds that the distinction often disappears in translating but is not to be overlooked (Goodell §475).
4. For an infinitive or participle outside indirect discourse, take the time from the leading verb. The tenses of the infinitive without ἄν and not in indirect discourse have no time of themselves and express only the stage of the action (§1865), though when the context shows that stress is laid on the idea of futurity, the future infinitive, referring to future time relative to the main verb, is sometimes used instead of the present or aorist (§1865 d). The participle not in indirect discourse, as a verbal adjective, is timeless, and whether its action is antecedent, coincident or subsequent to that of the leading verb depends on the context; the future participle has a temporal force only because its voluntative force points to the future (§1872).
5. In the indicative, ask which stage the writer chose, not how long the action lasted. Smyth's picture is this: the imperfect may be represented by a line along which an action progresses, and the aorist denotes a point on the line, either the starting point or the end, or surveys the whole line from beginning to end (§1908). The same figure sorts the aorist's own three uses, the ingressive, the resultative and the complexive (§1923).
6. If the verb's present denotes a state, expect the aorist to mark entry into it. That is the ingressive aorist (§1924). Goodwin's examples are the standard ones: βασιλεύω I am king against ἐβασίλευσα I became king, ἄρχω I hold office against ἦρξα I took office (GMT §55). Goodell prints the same list with ἠσθένησα fell sick and ἐβασίλευσα became king, and notes that ἔσχον from ἔχω is especially common (Goodell §464). Gildersleeve adds that this aorist is usually the first aorist, because the verbs are chiefly denominative (Gildersleeve §239).
7. If the aorist looks like the end of a process rather than its start, that is the resultative aorist: ἔπεισα I succeeded in persuading, ἔπεσον I struck in falling beside ἔπῑπτον I was in the act of falling (§1926).
8. If the aorist covers a stretch of time, do not conclude that Greek has broken its own rule. The complexive aorist surveys at a glance the course of a past action from beginning to end, and it is used of a long period as readily as a short one: τέσσαρα καὶ δέκα ἔτη ἐνέμειναν αἱ σπονδαί, the peace lasted fourteen years (§1927). Goodwin says: no amount of duration in an act can make the aorist an improper form to express it, provided it is stated as a single past event viewed as a whole, so that ἐβασίλευσε δέκα ἔτη means he had a reign of ten years (GMT §56).
9. Between imperfect and aorist in narrative, use Smyth's table rather than a rule about length. The imperfect gives circumstances, details and the course of an action, general description, endeavour, and actions subordinate to the main one; the aorist gives the mere fact of occurrence, consummation, isolated points, attainment, and the main actions (§1909). Goodell's version of the contrast is πολλᾱ̀ς προφάσεις ηὕρισκεν, he kept finding many pretexts (Goodell §459).
10. If the present or imperfect will not resolve into an accomplished act, read it as attempted. The present may express an action begun, attempted or intended, as προδίδοτον τὴν Ἑλλάδα, they are trying to betray Greece (§1878), and the imperfect does the same in the past, as ἔπειθον αὐτούς, I tried to persuade them (§1895). Goodell calls the first the present of endeavor and adds that to be repeatedly engaged in an action may imply endeavor without success (Goodell §454, Goodell §459).
11. For a perfect, ask what state is standing now. The perfect denotes a completed action the effects of which still continue in the present, and the state may be that of the subject or of the object: τὰ οἰκήματα ᾠκοδόμηται, the rooms have been constructed and their construction is finished (§1945). Goodwin says that the perfect represents an action as already finished at the present time (GMT §42), and Gildersleeve notes that it is for that reason sometimes called the present perfect (Gildersleeve §226).
12. If the state matters more than the act, translate the perfect by an English present. When the perfect marks the enduring result rather than the completed act it may often be translated by the present (§1946), and then the pluperfect is rendered by the imperfect, ἐκεκτήμην was in possession (§1952). Goodell gives the same list, ἕστηκα stand, τέθνηκα am dead, πέφῡκα am, κέκτημαι possess, μέμνημαι remember, and adds ἔγνωκα γάρ, yes, I know him (Goodell §456).
13. If the perfect seems to be an action and not a state, check the class before calling it intensive. Many perfects seem to denote an action rather than a state and to be equivalent to strengthened presents, verbs of the senses like δέδορκα gaze, of sustained sound like κέκρᾱγα bawl, of emotion and of gesture; but Smyth adds that most if not all of them may be regarded as true perfects, since πέφρῑκα is I have shuddered and am now in a state of shuddering (§1947).
14. For the future, ask whether it is continuative or aoristic, because Greek marks the difference only sometimes. The action of the future is either continuative like the present or, like that of the aorist, expresses simple attainment, so πείσω is I shall try to persuade or I shall convince (§1910), and where a verb has two futures the one from the present stem is properly continuative and the one from the aorist stem marks simple attainment, though ἕξω usually does duty for σχήσω (§1911). Gildersleeve explains why the question is often unanswerable: present and future have no special aoristic form in the active and middle, though in the passive it is possible to make a distinction (Gildersleeve §185). The absence of special forms for designating the kind of time in the future helps to account for many modal combinations (Gildersleeve §186).
15. Before deciding a tense is odd, check whether it is doing duty as a primary or a secondary tense. The gnomic aorist is regarded as a primary tense, as is the aorist used for the perfect, and the historical present as a secondary tense (§1858). Goodwin notes the same two, that the historic present is a secondary tense and the gnomic aorist is a primary tense (GMT §21).
16. If an aorist subjunctive follows ἐπειδάν, expect an English perfect or future perfect. Goodwin explains why: the meaning of the particle refers the aorist to time preceding the action of the leading verb, so that ἐπειδὰν τοῦτο ἴδω, ἥξω is after I shall have seen this, I will come (GMT §90). Smyth states the same result, that in dependent constructions the action of the aorist subjunctive is generally anterior to that of the leading verb, and that this is only an inference from the connection of the thought (§1860).

## 4. Translation guidance

- English has no exact equivalent of the Greek stages, and Greek has none of the English progressive. Smyth says so and adds what the periphrasis of the present participle with ἐστί does, which is to adjectivize the participle or to characterize the subject, as ἀρέσκοντές ἐσμεν, we are acceptable (§1857).
- The plain present is the plain English present: ἀληθῆ λέγω, I am telling the truth (§1875). Goodell's list of the ordinary uses adds Οὐδεὶς ἔτι ἡμῖν μάχεται, no one fights with us any longer (Goodell §454).
- The present of general truth takes the English present: ἄγει δὲ πρὸς φῶς τὴν ἀλήθειαν χρόνος, time brings the truth to light (§1877). Goodwin explains that since the limits of the action on either side of the present moment are not defined, the present may express a customary or repeated action or a general truth, as Τίκτει τοι κόρος ὕβριν (GMT §24).
- The historical present takes an English past. Smyth translates ὁ δὲ Θεμιστοκλῆς φεύγει ἐς Κέρκῡραν as "Themistocles fled to Corcyra", printing the present in parentheses beside it (§1883).
- Some presents are already perfects in sense and want an English perfect: ἥκω I am come, I have arrived, and οἴχομαι I am gone (§1886). A further group expresses an enduring result and may be translated by the perfect: ἀδικῶ I have done wrong, νῑκῶ I am victorious, I have conquered (§1887). Goodell records the same reading for ἥκω and οἴχομαι (Goodell §454).
- The imperfect takes the English past progressive or a past of habit: Στράτευμα συνελέγετο, an army was being collected, and ταῦτα τοῖς φίλοις διεδίδου, these he used to distribute to his friends (Goodell §459). Smyth's imperfect of customary action is the same thing (§1893), and his plain imperfect is ἐβασίλευεν Ἀντίοχος, Antiochus was reigning (§1889).
- The aorist takes the English simple past: Ἐνταῦθʼ ἔμεινεν, there he remained (Goodell §462). Goodwin's formula for it is the same, that the aorist indicative expresses the simple occurrence of an action in past time (GMT §53). Gildersleeve calls the aorist one of the two great narrative tenses and says it is best studied in connection with the other, the imperfect (Gildersleeve §238).
- Where English wants a perfect and Greek gives an aorist, follow the English. In Greek the aorist is often employed where English uses the perfect denoting a present condition resulting from a past action, as παρεκάλεσα ῡ̔μᾶς, I have summoned you (§1940). Goodell says the same and extends it to the pluperfect, καὶ στρατηγὸν δὲ αὐτὸν ἀπέδειξε, he had appointed him general also (Goodell §463).
- The gnomic aorist takes an English present. Goodwin says the gnomic aorist and the gnomic perfect are usually to be translated by our present (GMT §154), and his instance is Κάτθαν᾽ ὁμῶς ὅ τ᾽ ἀεργὸς ἀνὴρ ὅ τε πολλὰ ἐοργώς, the idle man and he who has laboured much alike must die (GMT §155).
- The empiric aorist, which is close to the gnomic aorist and has an adverb of experience, is commonly to be translated by the present or perfect: ἀθῡμοῦντες ἄνδρες οὔπω τροπαῖον ἔστησαν, faint heart never yet raised a trophy (§1930).
- A perfect that marks the enduring result rather than the completed act may often be translated by the present (§1946). Gildersleeve renders ἀκηκόατε, ἑωράκατε, πεπόνθατε as "You have heard, you have seen, you have felt" (Gildersleeve §226); Goodwin renders ἀκήκοα μὲν τοὔνομα as "I have heard the name" (GMT §46).
- The compound perfect, participle with εἰμί, states the continuance explicitly. Goodwin notes that each part of the compound generally retains its own signification, so Πεποιηκώς ἐστιν is he is in the condition of having done (GMT §45). Goodell records the same about the compound perfects and adds that in use there is often no clear distinction between the compound and the simple forms (Goodell §456).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Taking the aorist to mean short and the imperfect to mean long. The imperfect of continuance implies nothing as to the absolute length of the action, and it does not indicate prolonged action in contrast to momentary action of the aorist; what it does is put the reader in the midst of the events as they were taking place, where the aorist simply reports that an event took place (§1908).
- Insisting on a reason for every choice between them. Goodwin says it is sometimes difficult to see any decisive reason for preferring one tense to the other, that the Greeks occasionally failed to make use of this as of other fine distinctions when either form would express the sense equally well, although they always had the distinction ready for use when it was needed, and that we should avoid hair-splitting (GMT §88, GMT §57). Goodell agrees that the distinctions are sometimes subtle and elusive and that the choice of tense depends on the point of view (Goodell §466). Gildersleeve gives the same caution for the ingressive rendering, that it is not a proof but only an illustration (Gildersleeve §239).
- Reading time into an aorist subjunctive, optative, imperative or infinitive. In the subjunctive, optative and imperative the kind of time is implied only by the mood-forms and not by the tenses (§1850). Goodwin adds that the present and aorist subjunctive and imperative are always future, except that in general conditions the subjunctive is general in its time, and gives Μηδὲν φοβηθῇς fear not beside μηδὲν φοβοῦ be not timid as an example of the distinction of the two tenses (GMT §89).
- Assuming Greek marks the order of events in a dependent clause. In dependent clauses Greek has no special forms to denote the temporal relation of one action to another, and leaves the reader to infer whether one action happened before, at the same time as, or after another (§1850). Goodwin makes the same point, that the Greek uses its verbal forms much more freely than English to denote merely relative time (GMT §22).
- Forgetting that the present stem can be aoristic. The present stem may denote the simple action of the verb in present time without regard to its continuance, as θαυμάζω I am seized with astonishment and ἀστράπτει it lightens, and that is called the aoristic present (§1853).
- Expecting a Greek form for entering a state in present time. There is none: Greek has no special form to denote entrance into a state in present time (§1924).
- Reading the two stems as two vocabulary items. They are two stems of the same verb, and Smyth distinguishes them as line and point: ἔρχεσθαι is go and ἐλθεῖν is come, arrive; φέρειν is carry and ἐνεγκεῖν is bring (§1856). Gildersleeve calls the aorist the tense of concentrated action, the absolute tense, and says the form is peculiar to Greek as compared with Latin and English (Gildersleeve §185).
- Naming the tenses by their forms and expecting the classification to hold. The primary tenses refer to present and future time and the secondary to past time, but the gnomic aorist, the aorist used for the perfect and the imperfect indicative referring to present time count as primary, and the historical present as secondary (§1858). Goodwin says the distinction applies properly only to the tenses of the indicative, but adds that it may be extended to any forms of the dependent moods which have the same distinction of time (GMT §21). Gildersleeve gives the same division under the names principal and historical (Gildersleeve §187).
- Reading the Greek perfect as the English one. Monro says the perfect denotes a lasting condition or attitude, ἕξις, and that if we compare any perfect with the corresponding aorist or present we usually find that the perfect denotes a permanent state and the aorist or present an action which brings about that state; he adds that the number of Homeric perfects which can be rendered by "have" is comparatively small, and that there is nothing in Greek like the Latin fuit Ilium (Monro §28). On that account the so-called perfecta praesentia, βέβηκα, ἕστηκα, μέμνημαι, πέποιθα and οἶδα, are not exceptions but the commonest instances of the rule (Monro §28).
- Two names for one thing. Smyth calls the perfect that sets forth a general truth based on experience the empiric perfect, as ἡ ἀταξίᾱ πολλοὺς ἤδη ἀπολώλεκεν, lack of discipline ere now has been the ruin of many (§1948); Goodwin calls the same use the gnomic perfect and pairs it with the gnomic aorist (GMT §154). The use is the same and the names are two.
- Assuming the historical present is as old as Greek. Smyth says the use does not occur in Homer (§1883), and Goodwin says the historic present is not found in Homer (GMT §33). The gnomic perfect is likewise absent from Homer (GMT §155), while the gnomic aorist is not: Monro finds the aorist used when an assertion is made irrespective of time, in similes and in gnomic passages, and calls these uses very common in Homer (Monro §78).
- Taking the historical aorist for the original one. Smyth's definition is that the aorist expresses the mere occurrence of an action in the past, the action regarded as an event or single fact (§1923), and Goodwin's is the same (GMT §53). Monro argues the other way from Sanskrit: the historical use to assert the happening of a single event in the past is almost unknown to the earliest Sanskrit, the aorist is there used of what has happened in the immediate past, and if the Greek perfect is originally a kind of present there is a presumption that the aorist was originally akin in meaning to our perfect, so that the ordinary historical aorist is a derivative use (Monro §78). Monro quotes Gildersleeve in that argument for the paired rule, that when the perfect is used as a present the aorist is used as a perfect (Monro §78).
- Missing the Homeric aorist of the immediate past. Monro finds it used of that which in an especial sense is thought of as now past, and notes that it sometimes gives a question a tone of impatience (Monro §76). He also finds the aorist used of present time, where it expresses a culminating point reached at the moment of speaking (Monro §78), and describes the aorist stem as giving the meaning of a verb without the accessory notion of progress or continuance, as ἐνόησε perceived, understood (Monro §75).
- Reading every aorist participle as prior. Monro says the participle of the aorist is sometimes used to express exact coincidence with the action of the principal verb, especially with verbs expressing the manner with which a thing is said or done, and that a present participle there would imply a distinct subordinate action (Monro §77). Smyth agrees for Attic: the action of the aorist participle is generally antecedent, as δειπνήσᾱς ἐχώρει, after supper he advanced, but it is sometimes coincident when it defines or is identical with the action of the leading verb (§1872).
- Taking the perfect and the aorist as interchangeable because English merges them. Smyth notes that in Greek of the classical period the aorist and perfect are not confused, though the difference between the two tenses is often subtle (§1940). Gildersleeve puts the relation in a figure instead: the perfect looks at both ends of an action, the time between the ends is considered as a present, and when one end is considered the present is used and when the other the aorist, so that the aorist is the shorthand of the perfect (Gildersleeve §227).
- Reading ἔσχον as a plain past of ἔχω. Gildersleeve records the ingressive translation as especially common there, ἔχω I hold against ἔσχον I took hold, ἔχω I possess against ἔσχον I took possession, and notes that ἔστην and ἔβην admit the same rendering without the same sharp contrast between state and entrance upon a state (Gildersleeve §241).
- Forgetting that the tenses are used nearly, but not quite, as in English. Goodell says so at the head of his treatment and adds that the force of a tense is affected by the general meaning of the verb, by the context, and by other circumstances (Goodell §453).
- Reading a bare present or aorist infinitive as having its own time. Its time depends on the context and is that of the leading verb, and Smyth's aorist example, τοῦ πιεῖν ἐπιθῡμίᾱ, the desire of obtaining drink, is not past at all (§1865). The imperative pair works the same way: τοὺς γονεῖς τῑ̔μᾱ is honour thy parents as a course of conduct, and βλέψον πρὸς τὰ ὄρη is cast a glance toward the mountains (§1864).
- Treating the future perfect as a future with no added meaning. It denotes a future state resulting from a completed action (§1955), and when stress is laid upon complete fulfilment it may imply rapidity, immediate consequence, or certainty (§1956).
