# Middle Voice

Status: drafted 2026-09-07 on the pattern of `topics/genitive-absolute.md`
and `topics/supplementary-participle.md`. Every claim carries a section
citation; `scripts/verify_topic_pages.py` checks them. `topic: middle-voice`
names the derived record in `data/smyth/topics.json`, which gathers the
inflectional sections and the syntax together.

## 1. Quick answer

The middle voice shows that the action is performed with special reference to the subject: λοῦμαι is I wash myself (§1713). Smyth's fuller statement is that the middle represents the subject as doing something in which he is interested, and he may do something to himself, for himself, or he may act with something belonging to himself (§1714). Gildersleeve says the middle denotes that the subject is in some especial manner involved or interested in the action of the verb, and in some of its uses it corresponds to the English reflexive, but the signification is much wider and shades off until it ceases to present any translatable difference from the active (Gildersleeve §145). So the middle is not a reflexive pronoun in verb form. It marks that the subject is interested in the action, and how much of that interest can be translated depends on the particular verb, not on the voice.

## 2. Recognition

- Middle endings, which are a different set from the active (§366 a). The participle is the clearest: -μενο- appears in the middle, and in the passive except in the aorist (§470 c).
- Forms that could be passive instead. The passive borrows all its forms, except the future and aorist, from the middle (§356 b), and Goodell states the same fact from the paradigm side, that the passive and middle have the same form except in the future and aorist (Goodell §244).
- A direct reflexive, where self is the direct object. This goes with verbs expressing external and natural acts, the verbs of the toilet among them: ἀλείφεσθαι anoint oneself, λοῦσθαι wash oneself, κοσμεῖσθαι adorn oneself, στεφανοῦσθαι crown oneself, γυμνάζεσθαι exercise oneself (§1717). Gildersleeve limits the class the same way, chiefly to natural or habitual actions, and notes that the only middle for self-murder is ἀπάγξασθαι, to hang oneself (Gildersleeve §146).
- An indirect reflexive, where self is the indirect object: πορίζεσθαι provide for oneself beside πορίζειν provide, φυλάττεσθαι guard against beside φυλάττειν keep guard, αἱρεῖσθαι choose, that is take for oneself (§1719). This is the common case. Gildersleeve says the same: much more frequent is the indirect middle, in which the subject is more or less remotely involved, sometimes with sharp distinction from the active and sometimes without any perceptible difference (Gildersleeve §147).
- The subject acting with something of his own: ἐσπασμένοι τὰ ξίφη "having drawn their swords", τροπαῖον στησάμενοι "having set up a trophy", γυναῖκα ἠγαγόμην "I married" (§1721).
- ποιεῖσθαι with a verbal noun where the active would mean something else. λόγον ποιεῖσθαι is deliver a speech and λόγον ποιεῖν is compose a speech; ὁδὸν ποιεῖσθαι is make a journey and ὁδὸν ποιεῖν is build a road (§1722). Gildersleeve gives the same list and the same reading, the active for the literal side and the middle as a periphrasis for the corresponding simple verb (Gildersleeve §156).
- A dual or plural subject with a verb of contending, conversing or greeting, where the middle marks a reciprocal relation: οἱ ᾱ̓θληταὶ ἠγωνίζοντο "the athletes contended", ἀνὴρ ἀνδρὶ διελέγοντο "they conversed man with man" (§1726). Gildersleeve derives the use, that as the reflexive may be used for the reciprocal, so the middle, which represents the reflexive relation, may be used to intimate the reciprocal (Gildersleeve §149).
- A verb with middle forms and no active at all. Deponent verbs have an active meaning but only middle, or middle and passive, forms (§356 c). Gildersleeve's definition is that the deponent is a middle form which has no active (Gildersleeve §177).
- An accusative of the part affected beside a middle: ἐπαίσατο τὸν μηρόν "he smote his thigh" (§1717 b). Gildersleeve prints the same construction with τὰ μέτωπα κόπτονται μαχαίρῃσι "they gash their foreheads with knives" (Gildersleeve §155).

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

1. Ask first where in the object the subject's interest lies, because that is what the middle marks. The object may belong in the sphere of the subject, as λούομαι τᾱ̀ς χεῖρας I wash my hands; it may be brought into that sphere, as τοὺς ὁπλῑ́τᾱς μετεπέμψαντο they sent for the hoplites; or it may be removed from it (§1716).
2. If the subject is acting on himself, expect the direct middle, which is rare. Self is there the direct object (§1717), but the direct reflexive idea is far more frequently conveyed by the active and a reflexive pronoun (§1717 a). Monro says the same for Homer, where the use in which the agent is the direct object of the action, as λούο-μαι I wash myself, is comparatively rare (Monro §8).
3. If the action is unnatural or difficult, expect the active with a reflexive pronoun and not the middle at all. Instead of the direct middle the active voice with the reflexive pronoun is usually employed, often of difficult and unnatural actions, as μισθώσᾱς αὑτόν hiring himself out, which is not μισθωσάμενος, hiring for himself (§1723). Gildersleeve states the same rule with the same examples and adds that extreme unnaturalness is expressed by αὐτὸς ἑαυτόν and the like (Gildersleeve §151, Gildersleeve §152).
4. If a reflexive pronoun occurs with a middle, it marks emphasis. The reflexive pronoun may be used with the middle, as ἑαυτὸν ἀποκρύπτεσθαι to hide himself, often for emphasis and in contrasts (§1724). Gildersleeve says the reflexive relation may be brought out more clearly by adding the reflexive forms to the middle (Gildersleeve §153).
5. If the subject has the act done by someone else, consider the causative middle: ἐγὼ γάρ σε ταῦτα ἐδιδαξάμην "for I had you taught this", ἑαυτῷ σκηνὴν κατεσκευάσατο "he had a tent prepared for himself" (§1725). Do not rely on it. Smyth adds that this force does not belong exclusively to the middle (§1725 a), and Gildersleeve says it is an occasional use of the middle, not a characteristic one, and must not be pressed as differentiating it from the active (Gildersleeve §150).
6. If the subject is dual or plural, test for the reciprocal middle, and remember Greek has three other ways of saying the same thing. The reciprocal relation may also be expressed by the reflexive pronoun with the active, as φθονοῦσιν ἑαυτοῖς, by ἀλλήλων with the active, as ἀμφισβητοῦμεν ἀλλήλοις, or by repeating the noun (§1727). Monro finds the reciprocal use already in Homer, in ἀμειβόμενος taking his turn and in the middle form of μάχομαι (Monro §8).
7. If both voices exist for the verb, look the pair up rather than reasoning from the voice. As contrasted with the active, the middle lays stress on the conscious activity, bodily or mental participation, of the agent, as in βουλεύεσθαι deliberate against βουλεύειν plan and σκοπεῖσθαι consider against σκοπεῖν look at (§1728). Smyth's numbered list of the pairs that differ in meaning is: αἱρεῖν take and αἱρεῖσθαι choose, γαμεῖν and γαμεῖσθαι, δανείζειν lend and δανείζεσθαι borrow, μισθοῦν let for hire and μισθοῦσθαι hire, πείθειν persuade and πείθεσθαι obey, φυλάττειν τινά watch some one and φυλάττεσθαί τινα be on one's guard against some one (§1734). Gildersleeve refers the same material out of the grammar, saying that the details of difference between the active and the middle belong to the dictionary, though many of them are conventionally of great importance (Gildersleeve §156). Goodell sorts the same pairs by which kind of middle they are, direct, indirect, or subjective (Goodell §500).
8. In a verb in -ευω, ask whether the subject is acting in a manner appropriate to his state. πολῑτεύειν is be a citizen and πολῑτεύεσθαι is act as a citizen, perform one's civic duties; πρεσβεύειν is be an envoy and πρεσβεύεσθαι is negotiate as envoy (§1728 b).
9. If the active stands where you expected a middle, do not force a distinction. The active is often used for the middle when it is not of practical importance to mark the interest of the subject in the action, so that μεταπέμπειν send for and τροπαῖον στήσαντες setting up a trophy occur beside the middles (§1732). Gildersleeve treats the same substitutions and adds that the middle is not compulsory (Gildersleeve §148).
10. Now separate middle from passive. Do not start from the form. Gildersleeve states the principle: the distinction of passive from middle is a distinction of function, not of form, and only one comparatively late form is uniformly differentiated, the future in -θήσομαι (Gildersleeve §137). Monro goes further and says Greek has no passive endings distinct from those of the active and middle, so that it is desirable to speak not of passive forms but of the passive meaning or use of a form (Monro §8).
11. In the present, imperfect, perfect and pluperfect the form will not decide it, so the sense and the construction must. With the exception of some futures and the aorist, the middle forms do duty as passives, so that αἱρεῖται is both takes for himself, that is chooses, and is chosen (§1735). Look for an agent, and read the passive when one is there.
12. In the aorist the form usually decides, and the two are worth trusting. The passive has the endings of the middle except in the aorist, which has the active endings (§366 a). But the aorist passive of some active verbs has a reflexive or middle sense, either sometimes or always: ἐκῑνήθην was moved or moved myself, ἐφάνην showed myself, appeared, against ἐφάνθην, which usually means was shown (§814). Smyth's list of those verbs is: αἰσχῡ́νω disgrace with ᾐσχύνθην felt ashamed, πείθω persuade with ἐπείσθην obeyed, φοβέω terrify with ἐφοβήθην was afraid, πορεύω convey with ἐπορεύθην marched (§815).
13. In the future, the middle form may have a passive meaning. In many verbs the future middle has the meaning of the future passive, as ἀδικέω wrong with ἀδικήσομαι shall be wronged (§807). Goodell gives the same list, ἀδικήσομαι shall be wronged, ὠφελήσομαι shall be benefited, πολιορκήσομαι shall be besieged (Goodell §393). Gildersleeve adds the historical reason: the future middle and the future passive are coincident as to form in the earlier language, and there is only one distinctly future passive form in Homer (Gildersleeve §168).
14. If both future forms exist for the verb, read the difference as one of aspect and not of voice. The future middle forms in -σομαι are developed from the present stem and express durative action, while the later future passives in -ήσομαι and -θήσομαι are developed from the aorists in -ην and -θην and are aoristic, so that τῑμήσομαι is I shall enjoy honour and τῑμηθήσομαι is I shall be honoured on a definite occasion (§1738). Gildersleeve prints the same contrast in two columns, durative against aoristic, for βλάπτω, τιμάω, ὠφελέω and δηλόω, and warns that even in the most careful authors such distinctions may lie dormant (Gildersleeve §168).
15. If the future middle has an active sense instead, that is a third possibility and a common one. Many verbs have no active future and use the future middle in an active sense: λαμβάνω λήψομαι, γιγνώσκω γνώσομαι (§805). Goodell's list includes ἀκούω, ἀκούσομαι hear, ὁράω, ὄψομαι see, πάσχω, πείσομαι suffer, and φεύγω, φεύξομαι flee (Goodell §392).
16. If the verb is deponent, ask which kind before you read its aorist. If its aorist has the middle form, a deponent is called a middle deponent, as χαρίζομαι with ἐχαρισάμην; if its aorist has the passive form, it is a passive deponent, as ἐνθῡμέομαι with ἐνεθῡμήθην (§356 c). The aorist passive of a middle deponent, when it occurs, has a passive force: αἰτιάομαι accuse, ᾐτιᾱσάμην accused, ᾐτιᾱ́θην was accused (§810). Goodell gives the same three-form pattern with the same verb (Goodell §394).
17. With a passive deponent, do not read the passive form as a passive meaning. Deponent verbs whose aorists have the passive form but the active or middle meaning are called passive deponents, as βούλομαι with ἐβουλήθην, and most of them express mental action of some sort (§811). Gildersleeve lists the same verbs and explains the drift: the form, though not distinctively passive at first, became distinctively passive, so that a passive conception is often possible even where the sense is active (Gildersleeve §169). Goodell notes that most passive deponents have the middle forms in the future, as βουλήσομαι beside ἐβουλήθην (Goodell §395).
18. If a deponent does look passive, check whether it is one of the few that can be. Some deponent verbs have a passive meaning, avoided by good writers in the present and imperfect but common in the perfect and pluperfect: ὠνέομαι is buy and am bought, ἐωνήθην was bought, ἐώνημαι have bought and have been bought (§813). Goodell records the same double reading for the perfect, εἴργασται he has worked or it has been worked (Goodell §396).
19. The passive can be the passive of the middle as well as of the active. αἱρεῖται is is taken or is chosen, βιάζεται is does violence or suffers violence, ᾑρέθη is was taken or was chosen, and this use of the passive as passive of the middle is post-Homeric (§1742). Gildersleeve gives the same passives of middle verbs, βιάζομαι I suffer violence and ᾑρέθην I was chosen, and adds that it is not so in Homer (Gildersleeve §170).
20. When the aorist looks passive but the verb has no passive sense anywhere, suspect an intransitive aorist rather than a passive. The second aorist in -ην is primarily intransitive and shows active inflection, and many so-called passive forms are merely intransitive aorists of active verbs (§803). Monro reaches the same conclusion from Homer: the aorist in -η appears to have originally had an intransitive sense, of which the passive sense was a growth or adaptation, as in ἐχάρη rejoiced and ἐφάνη appeared (Monro §44). Goodell says that the aorist passive was originally not passive but merely intransitive, and that in some verbs this intransitive sense continued in common use, as always in ἐχάρην (Goodell §501).
21. If the verb has two aorists or two perfects, the first is generally transitive and the second intransitive: ἵστημι set has ἔστησα set beside ἔστην stood and ἕστηκα am standing (§819).
22. When no difference appears in English, say so. The force of the middle often cannot be reproduced in translation, and in some cases it may not have been felt at all (§1728).

## 4. Translation guidance

- Direct middle: an English reflexive, or an intransitive English verb. λοῦμαι is I wash myself (§1713); ἵστασθαι is stand, literally place oneself, τρέπεσθαι is turn, φαίνεσθαι is show oneself, appear, and ἀπόλλυσθαι is destroy oneself, perish (§1718).
- Indirect middle: often a possessive in the English, or nothing. τὴν ῥᾳθῡμίᾱν ἀποθέσθαι is "to lay aside your indolence", and τοὺς ἐχθροὺς ἀμῡ́νεσθαι is to ward off the enemy for themselves, that is to defend themselves against the enemy (§1720).
- Where the middle marks the subject's own resources, English wants a possessive: ἐσπασμένοι τὰ ξίφη "having drawn their swords", παῖδας ἐκκεκομισμένοι ἦσαν "they had removed their children" (§1721).
- The ποιεῖσθαι periphrases take the simple English verb, and the active takes a different one. εἰρήνην ποιεῖσθαι is make peace, of one nation at war with another, and εἰρήνην ποιεῖν is bring about a peace between opponents; ναυμαχίᾱν ποιεῖσθαι is fight a naval battle and ναυμαχίᾱν ποιεῖν is bring one on (§1722).
- Reciprocal middle: an English reciprocal. ἐπιμείγνυσθαι ἀλλήλοις is "to have friendly intercourse with one another" and ταῦτα διανεμοῦνται is "they will divide this up among themselves" (§1726). Gildersleeve's rendering of κεφαλάς τε συναράσσονται is "They butt one another's heads" (Gildersleeve §149).
- Causative middle: English wants have something done. ὅσοι ὅπλα ἀφῄρηνται, ταχὺ ἄλλα ποιήσονται is "all who have had their arms taken from them will soon get others made" (§1725).
- Where a passive form has reflexive force, English wants an intransitive or a reflexive: κῑνηθῆναι is set oneself in motion, ἀπαλλαγῆναι is remove oneself, and σώθητι is "save yourself" (§1733).
- Where the passive means allow oneself to be, say so: the passive may have the sense allow oneself to be, get oneself, as in ἀπεχθήσει Γοργίᾳ "you will incur the hatred of Gorgias" (§1736).
- Deponents of bodily and mental action take the plain English verb: ἅλλεσθαι jump, πέτεσθαι fly, ὀρχεῖσθαι dance, βούλεσθαι wish, αἰσθάνεσθαι perceive, ἡγεῖσθαι consider (§1729). Monro groups the same class for Homer and adds the French parallels, je m'aperçois for I perceive and je me doute for I suspect (Monro §8).
- A deponent is still either a direct or an indirect middle underneath, and the English follows: ὑπισχνεῖσθαι is undertake, promise, literally hold oneself under, and κτᾶσθαι is acquire for oneself (§1730).
- Where the middle marks vigour, put it in the English verb: σεύεσθαι is dart against θέειν run (§1731).
- Where two aorists sit side by side, keep them apart in English: ἐσώθην is saved myself, was saved, against ἐσωσάμην saved for myself; ἐψεύσθην is deceived myself, was deceived, against ἐψευσάμην lied (§818).
- Do not invent an English difference where Greek has none. ἀκούεσθαι, τῑμᾶσθαι, ἀριθμεῖσθαι and ἀπορεῖσθαι are Smyth's own examples of middles whose force cannot be reproduced (§1728), and Gildersleeve's point is that the middle shades off until it ceases to present any translatable difference from the active (Gildersleeve §145).

## 5. Common mistakes

- Reading every middle as a reflexive. The middle represents the subject as doing something in which he is interested, which may be to himself, for himself, or with something of his own (§1714).
- Reading a present or perfect middle form as necessarily middle. The passive borrows all its forms except the future and aorist from the middle (§356 b), and the passive has been developed from the middle, so that the middle forms do duty as passives (§1735).
- Reading a future middle as necessarily middle in sense. It may be active, as λήψομαι and γνώσομαι (§805), or passive, as ἀδικήσομαι (§807).
- Reading an aorist in -θην as necessarily passive. The aorists in -θην that are called passive are often active or middle in meaning, as ἥσθην took pleasure in and ὠργίσθην became angry (§804).
- Reading ἐφάνην as a passive. It is φαίνω's intransitive aorist, appeared, and the passive ἐφάνθην usually means was shown; the middle φαίνομαι means show oneself, appear, and the passive means am shown, am made evident (§819).
- Pressing the causative middle. Smyth notes that the force does not belong exclusively to the middle (§1725 a), and Gildersleeve warns that it must not be pressed as differentiating the middle from the active (Gildersleeve §150).
- Expecting a direct middle for a violent or unnatural act. Those take the active with a reflexive pronoun, as τὰ ὅπλα παρέδοσαν καὶ σφᾶς αὐτούς "they surrendered their arms and themselves", though ἀπάγχεσθαι hang oneself is regular (§1723).
- Taking the first aorist middle as a passive. The future middle is often used passively, but the first aorist middle is almost never (§1715). Traces of the passive use of the aorist middle do appear in Homer, as ἔβλητο was hit, but that use was largely abandoned when -ην and -θην came to be used as special marks of the passive (§802).
- Assuming the passive of a verb that has both voices must be the passive of the active. The passive may be passive of the middle as well, and when deponent verbs have a passive force the future and aorist take the passive form, as ἐβιάσθην I suffered violence against ἐβιασάμην I did violence (§1742).
- Reading a second aorist passive as a passive when the verb never means anything passive. It was originally a second aorist active of the -μι form used intransitively, as ἔφηνα showed beside ἐφάνην appeared, and ἔφθειρα destroyed beside ἐφθάρην am destroyed (§1739).
- Treating Homer's usage as Attic. The middle is rather more common in Homer than in later Greek, and there it extends to verbs of seeing and hearing, ὁρῶ-μαι and ἀκούομαι beside the actives (Monro §8). Gildersleeve gives the same fact in numbers, where ἰδέσθαι occurs thirty-eight times against nineteen instances of ἰδεῖν (Gildersleeve §147).
- Looking for an active where the language never had one. Deponent verbs are properly middle, in the indirect or subjective sense, and the active was not thought necessary (Goodell §501).
- Forgetting that the middle endings themselves are the evidence. The middle has a different set of endings from the active, and the passive has the endings of the middle except in the aorist (§366 a); in the participle the middle and passive alike show -μενο-, except in the aorist passive (§470).
